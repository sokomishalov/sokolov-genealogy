     AN INTERIM REPORT ON SOLODYRI AND SURROUNDING VILLAGES

Prepared in July, 1995, by Richard Benert, 75 W. Laurel St., Bethlehem, PA,
18018.  Tel. (6l0) 865-2898.

This report is based almost entirely on letters from, and conversations with,
former residents of Solodyri and its neighboring villages, Neudorf and Rogowka.
Hopefully, with new access to archival sources, future research will yield
information not accessible through the excellent memories of these helpful
people.  But for those who are interested in knowing what we know about
Solodyri up to this point, the following few pages will provide a brief
summary.  I have not yet checked their contents against established histories
of the period, so they may contain a few basic historical errors which will in
due time be eliminated.  Nor can I vouch for the absolute truth and accuracy of
everything that has been told to me by my sources, except to say I am sure that
all the information has been offered in good faith and to the best of
everyone's memory.

I wish first of all to acknowledge the people on whom I have relied for
information.  The first person I met from Solodyri, other than my father,
Rudolph Benert, was my cousin, Alice (Boehnert) Buchholz, of Creston, B.C.,
whom I "discovered" in 1988 although she had been living in Canada since 1951.
The person who has been most generous with information has been Alice's
childhood friend and neighbor, Alice (Deblitz) Baumbach, of Wauwatosa,
Wisconsin.  Through her, I made contact with Robert Hadel of Duluth, Minnesota,
Martha (Schilke) Patzke of Bremen, Germany, and several Solodyrians living in
and around Schwaebisch-Gmuend, Robert Holstein, Assaf Kohlert, Sofia and Eugen
Bunkowski and Goldine Klang.  In addition, Don Miller must be mentioned with
thanks for information and photographs emanating from his Volhynian tours.
David Obee of Calgary bids fair to become the chief genealogist of all the
villages in this part of Volhynia.

Much of what we know at this time about Solodyri comes to us from the parents
of these former Solodyrians.  Our correspondents  were children, after all,
when living there, but their attentive ears captured valuable bits of
information.  While documentary evidence concerning the origin of the village
is lacking, Adolf Kohlert and his wife luckily told their son, Assaf, that
after the freeing of the Russian serfs in 1861, a large landowner in the
village of Dworisch, finding it hard to find agricultural workers among native
inhabitants of the area ("die faule Ukrainer wollten nicht arbeiten," says
Assaf), decided to bring in German farmers from the west, like thousands of
other landowners were doing.  Whether a landowner by the name of Mikadoski is
the one the Kohlerts had in mind, he is the one from whom Alice Baumbach's
grandparents, Wilhelm and Wilhelmina Kethelhut, purchased their property.  He
reportedly greeted the Kethelhuts in perfect German.  In any case, the German
immigrants to the new village came mostly from East Prussia and Poland.

Long before any Germans got to this area, it was inhabited by bees, which
should not be too surprising.  Apparently, humanly produced accommodations for
these bees were not sufficient, for many of them built their own hives in holes
in the trunks of trees, called "dyri" in Ukrainian.  According to Assaf
Kohlert's parents, a beautiful name for the new village suggested itself to
someone who reflected upon the sweet ("solod") substance hidden in these holes.
How refreshing that, among the welter of villages whose names ended in the
predictable "owka" or "ufke" tacked onto the name of a person, someone (was it
the Ukrainian landowner?) may have been imaginative enough to commemorate the
honey of the local bee population!

The early history of Solodyri probably differed little from that of other
Volhynian German villages coming to life since the 1860s. Someday, hopefully,
we will know something about the contracts entered into by our ancestors with
the Ukrainian landowners of the area, and how this collection of individual
farmers coalesced into a viable village with its school, churches and
governmental structure.  At this time I can only repeat what Wilhelm and
Auguste (Lange) Schilke told their daughter, Martha, about the lives of the
early German settlers.  The Schilkes lived in the adjacent village of Neudorf,
but the two villages functioned almost as one.  It was their impression that,
among the motives for Germans to leave Prussia and Poland and settle in
Volhynia, a desire for religious and civic freedom was uppermost.  They had
undoubtedly been offered exemption from military service and at least some
taxes.  The hard work of clearing forests (mostly oak) was impressed on young
Martha's mind, along with the fact that the wolves, foxes and snakes so
prevalent in these forests had decreased in number with the clearing so that
what remained by c.1920 were mostly rabbits.  Unfortunately, the forest berries
and mushrooms had also declined in number.  The earliest settlers had planted
"potatoes and beans" and, because cleared land was still minimal, had raised
relatively more cattle and sheep than they did later when cleared land allowed
for heavy investment in grains.  The Schilkes stressed that not all farmers had
succeeded to an equal degree.  Some had worked harder.  Some had been blessed
with greater strength and some with larger families, both crucial elements in
the enterprise of farming.  A few had remained poor, and owned no land at all.
These usually rented land, and their children formed a pool of maids, servants,
cow-boys and baby-sitters for the more prosperous farmers.

Life in Solodyri must have been good for forty years or so.  It is not
inconceivable that families vied with each other to improve themselves and make
their farms as attractive as possible.  Even years later, and after having left
his father's farm, my father boasted that this farm, purchased in the 1890s and
improved with the planting of an orchard and the erection of the required
Germanic picket fence, was probably the nicest farm in the neighborhood.  I
suspect his neighbors felt the same way about theirs.  Certainly the family of
Eduard Sell had nothing to be ashamed of.  Born in Poland in 1860, he came to
Volhynia about 1880.  He married Anna Funk, also from Poland and born in 1864.
After a few years, they were able to buy about 18 hectares of land in the
northwest section of Solodyri and built a house.  They had ten children and
only one died.  As the children grew old enough for such work, they built a
large Scheune for storing grain, then a Stall for cows and horses. Eventually
they had nine cows, four horses, numerous pigs, and all the necessary machinery
for good farming.  In short, in the words of his daughter, Agnete, they lacked
nothing ("wir hatten an nichts Mangel").  Such contentment as this can be seen
also in the opinion of Martha Patzke's maternal uncle who, after travelling
twice to the United States and visiting other countries like Turkey and Sweden,
allowed that each country might have its virtues, but in Russia was to be found
the "most peaceful and easiest life."

All this came to an abrupt end, of course, in the summer of 1915 when Volhynian
Germans, distrusted by the Russian government as possible collaborators with
the German army, were exiled to the east.  The stories of tragedy and suffering
are endless, and most have undoubtedly gone unrecorded.  Thanks to Don Miller's
visit to the Zhitomir archives, we now have a list of those deported which
appeared in a Volhynian newspaper in 1916.  (I have appended the list for
Solodyri, Neudorf and Rutkowski Chutor to this report, with the warning that
its accuracy and completeness are both subject to suspicion.)  Agnete (Sell)
Bunkowski's story is probably quite typical.  She wrote that her family was
forced to leave in January, 1915 (she must have meant 19161), along with other
families whose sons were fighting in the Czar's service (other families, such
as Alice Baumbach's parents and my grandparents and uncle, having been forced
to leave some months earlier).  This favor allowed them to travel in open sleds
in the dead of winter.  Agnete's mother was ill, and died shortly after
commencing the train ride, in cattle cars, from Korest.  Burial was out of the
question.  She was removed from the train, and on it went.  Agnete's
grandfather was next to die.  His body left the train in Woronesh.  Eventually,
they joined 1600 others in a barracks in "Siberia."  Within three months,
typhus had taken 1000 of them, including Agnete's other grandfather, Peter
Sell, her 10-year-old brother, Assaf, and a 2-year-old niece.  The remaining
four family members then were relocated to a Tatar village, memorable to Agnete
for its strange language, its cockroaches and its bekerchiefed women who were
not allowed to talk to strange men.

Among the joys of returning to Solodyri in December, 1917, at 12 P.M., after a
7-week journey by cattle car, was the tumultuous reception given them by the
two family dogs who came running the moment they heard familiar voices calling
their names.  Since their number was reduced to only four, they fit more easily
into the one room made available to them by the three Galician families living
in their house.  The Galicians returned to "their Austria" after a few months,
leaving a house bereft of furniture, which had all been stolen.  Apparently,
"the Russians brought a few things back," Agnete remembered, and her father,
Eduard, labored to restore things to something resembling their former state.
It helped much when, after two months, her two brothers (Peter and, possibly,
Leonard?), who had served in the army, returned home, and Peter brought with
him "two beautiful horses and a wagon."  The government also helped, she wrote,
by contributing a horse--not exactly a gift, since the government had
confiscated their four horses three years before.  Agnete married August
Bunkowski, from Skolobow, in July, 1918.  They either inherited or otherwise
acquired 8 hectares of land.  Their father (she doesn't say which one) bought a
property with house and barns, and this property (if I read her correctly) was
later divided among four children.  The times were difficult, but they
recovered.  Between 1919 and 1924, five children (Irma, Eugen, August, Almidija
and Assav) were born.

A slightly different version of this experience comes from the pen of Goldine
Klang's mother, Adoline, who was a sister of Agnete (Sell) Bunkowski.  In her
"Tagesbuch," she wrote of "Der grosse Leidensweg," the exile of 1915-1917, with
a touching note of resignation and a seeming belief that the government was
only doing what it had to do.  As the war broke out in 1914, so her husband,
Albert Klang, "musste" go off to war.  As the conflict came so close to
Solodyri that "the windows rattled," so the authorities "musste" send them away
"from our beautiful Volhynia."  After identifying the location of the great
barracks in "Siberia" as Orenburg, where 2000 Germans had already been
transported, she expressed gratitude for three months of cooked meals that were
prepared for them there, with "meat every day."  "We could not complain about
the government," she wrote.  But then began the spread of illness--scarlet
fever, she said.  Two of her four children died, along her mother and two
grandfathers.  She chose to say nothing of the suffering of those years, except
for these deaths, and quickly hastened to the return to Volhynia, which they
had left "full" but was now, in 1918, "empty."  They had no horse, no cow, no
wagon, and the large Scheune was gone.  Starting over was made more difficult
by higher prices.  A cow that had cost 50 rubles in 1914 now went for 800.  But
Albert did return from the war, and they managed to buy an initial 3 desiatin
of land.  Goldine was born in 1920.  She remembers that, before the good times
ended around 1928, the family had built a 4-room house, a Stall and a Scheune,
had a vegetable garden, an orchard, flower garden 10 hectares of land, two
ponds (one with fish, one for ducks and geese) and a small woods for firewood,
mushrooms, berries and nuts, and where the children happily played.

Martha Schilke, from Neudorf, was 5 years old when the deportation occurred.
She and the other children could not understand why the Strashniki, especially
their chief, Oradnik, would whip the German men whenever they stopped to rest
their horses.  Nine weeks of travel brought her group of exiles to Samara on
the Volga.  There they must have made contact with Volga Germans, but
apparently the contact was not in all cases  pleasant.  Perhaps a few Volga
Germans had problems enough of their own, causing actions that may have led the
Schilkes to feel, in Martha's words, that the Volga Germans  thought of the
Volhynians as criminals (Die Wolga Deutschen haben uns als schwere Verbrecher),
and that as a group they "had no idea that there really were other people in
the world" (Die hatten keine Ahnung das ueberhaupt noch andere Leute auf der
Welt sind).  Knowing no details, I leave it to others to ponder this charge!
At least one other person, however, has corroborated it.

Martha Patzke's account of the return from exile indicates that in 1917 those
who had some money could rent a wagon for the return to Volhynia, and that
often a few families would rent a wagon together.  The Russian Revolution of
1917 did not bother the Volhynians at all, she says, because they were so happy
to be back in their homeland, empty buildings and all.  The Galicians had not
shown much enthusiasm for farming, but they had continued to use the Baptist
Kapelle in Neudorf for worship.  Martha remembers soldiers in the ensuing civil
war coming to eject a full house of worshippers from this church.  The civil
war, she recalls, was a time of confusion, when there seemed to be a new
government every other day.

The initial remoteness of the Revolution from Volhynian concerns may be
reflected in the somewhat sarcastic, tongue-in-cheek summary of it by Alice
Deblitz's mother:  "People say that in St. Petersburg they have elected Lenin."
Lenin's "election" did not take effect in Volhynia for several years, of
course, due to the civil war.  First, according to Alice, it was the Reds
fighting against the Tsar's army.  Then, as Poland tried to take advantage of
the situation and expand eastwards, the Reds fought the Poles.  German troops
were sent as peacekeepers to Zhitomir, but when they left in 1918, the
so-called "Petlura" bands began to roam about killing thousands of Jews and
others, and causing great fear among the Germans.  The new Communist government
established its authority in Solodyri only in 1921.  But throughout the civil
war, says Alice, the Germans tended to remain neutral, many of them wanting to
believe the propaganda of the Reds, and also remembering how kind the Red
soldiers had been when the exiles had returned in 1917, meeting them at the
railroad station, carrying their packs and being nice to their children.
Without such hopes in a future filled with communist promises, she thinks, the
German farmers would not have worked so hard to rebuild after the war.

The coming of Communist government did not bring immediate drastic changes to
either the government or the economy of the village.  Private property remained
safe from government plundering for about 5 or 6 years.  The governing
structure of the village was not seriously changed.  There had been, at the top
of things, a "Schulz" or Buergermeister, who was elected by the village
assembly, probably, thinks Alice Baumbach, by a show of hands.  He stood under
the authorities of the Kreis in Pulin, a town some ten kilometers to the
southwest.  He handled administrative matters and decided small legal cases.
(Larger cases, like theft and arson, were tried in Pulin, and above that court
was the court in Zhitomir).  According to Martha Patzke, the Buergermeister had
the "Feuerkasse."  Exactly what this meant I have yet to learn.  The tax burden
for the village was determined by authorities in Pulin, leaving the apportioned
levy on individual farmers up to the Buergermeister, more correctly called
"Village Council President" (Dorfratsvorsitzender) after 1921.  The Russian
term for the office was "Presedatel."  Alice Baumbach thinks that the "taxes
were not always correctly apportioned."  Helping the Buergermeister in
apportioning taxes was his bookkeeper.  In addition, he had a secretary.
These, together with four unarmed village policemen (only the Buergermeister
had a pistol), made up the village council (Dorfrat).  The headquarters of the
council, called the "Kanzlei" was located in the village center (in the home of
Ludwig Lueck), across the street from the post office (in the home of Herman
Liedtke) and the new school which was built in 1929.  Because of the close
proximity to Neudorf and Rogowka, these villages were included within the
jurisdiction of the Solodyri council and its "president."

Whenever village business required the assemblage of all the adult (probably
male) inhabitants, says Assaf Kohlert, a small note was sent out from the
Kanzlei and was circulated from farmer to farmer until the whole village knew
that the Dorfversammlung would meet "at 6 o'clock."

The Buergermeister from 1922 to about 1928 was Leonard Sell, a man who seems to
have been held in universally high regard.  When collectivizaton began to take
on immense proportions in 1928, it was decided that he had to step down in
favor of men from the poorer classes of society (and more controllable by the
Communist Party).  Sell, Alice Baumbach thinks, was too intelligent, religious
and wealthy for the authorities (he was an active Baptist, played in the
church's brass choir, and was the son of a "well-to-do" farmer, Peter Sell).
By contrast, his successors were men of poor education and little culture, and
were appointed to the office, elections having become obsolete.  It was said of
Sell's immediate successor, Leo Hoppe, that he first learned to write his name
after his new duties as Buergermeister forced him to.  Little was demanded of
these new Búrgermeisters other than the ability to sign and enforce Party
directives.  Next in line was August Gruenwald ("a cunning and conscienceless
fellow," Alice says) who had risen fast in the Party and had entered the
administration of the Kreis in Pulin.  After him, in 1932 or 1933, came two
Kaufmann brothers who were not natives of Solodyri.  Perhaps, Alice thinks,
they may have been among the communists from Germany who became active in the
Party in Pulin.  Three others she remembers were named Lerch, Graeber and
Oster.  Robert Hadel also remembers Graeber, and a Jewish functionary known as
"Halbmillion," as part of the gang of "Agronoms," or "brutal party members" who
ran the village in the 1930s. It was Grúnwald and the Kaufmanns who presided
over the decimation of the "kulaks" of Solodyri from 1930 to 1937.  They reaped
their reward a few years later when, in 1941, invading German troops took them
to Pulin where they were tried (no doubt briefly) and executed.

The collectivization of agriculture in the 1930s, with all its attendant
horrors and tragedies and its monumental misreading of Karl Marx, was but the
last of a series of efforts by the government to introduce communism.  Less
drastic attempts had been made in preceding years, in which the government
tried gradually to graft a socialist economy onto the old "free" economy of the
village.  Perhaps the first sign of things to come was the new village
"cooperative" store which replaced the pre-revolutionary store owned by a
Jewish merchant named Jasel.  It was in Jasel's store that Alice Deblitz's
Grandfather Kethelhut had made his weekly purchase of herring, pipe tobacco,
two bottles of beer and "whatever grandmother needed."  Beer and schnapps were
also available in casks.  Jasel's store was gone when the exiles returned after
1917.  To replace it, a cooperative was set up apparently in the home of Arthur
and Elsa (Alice's sister) Zoecknick, who lived next door to the post office and
across from the village hall or Kanzlei.  Its first clerk was Alice's
half-brother, Arthur Nartschinski.  Soon after, an actual store was built just
south of the Irsa(?) River on the road towards Skolobow.  At first, Alice says,
it had everything people needed, but later, thanks to central planning, only
"salt and vodka."  Special orders were no longer possible, since the stock was
determined solely by the bureaucratic chain of command. The exact time the new
store was built I do not know, but Alice Baumbach thinks it was 1927 or 1928.
It functioned at least as a place where "men with nothing better to do" could
hang around and tell jokes, says Alice.  Arthur Nartschinski kept the store
only briefly and was replaced by  Julius Wink.  According to Alice, this
unfortunate man was imprisoned for failing to make the books tally, a problem
caused, she says, by the "ruling men" of the village not paying for their
schnapps.  The next storekeeper, Bernhard Spletzer,soon left for Zhitomir, to
be followed by Jankel Drubitsch, who remained at least until 1934.

The lack of supplies in the cooperative was probably the reason why regular
trips were made to nearby towns for shopping.  Pulin and Zhitomir were major
centers, but the favorite place seems to have been Horoshek (variously called
Goroshek, Gorshik, Horoski and Wolodarsk, but usually called "Roshek."  Here
were several stores owned by Jews where one could buy sugar, salt, oil, dairy
products, candy, clothing, kitchenware, and, for the children, heads for the
puppets that were later made at home.  Alice remembers visiting Tshfranski's,
where in the corner was a samovar for tea with sugar lumps nearby for children.
Here one could also eat Wurst and Semmel.

Under the old Czarist economy, and even under Lenin's New Economic Policy,
surplus produce from farms could be sold in nearby towns like Pulin and
Zhitomir.  For a short time in the mid-20s, numerous farmers, including my
uncle, August Boehnert, tried growing hops with considerable success, in
addition to the usual wheat, barley and oats.  The hops were sold to Jews who
used them for brewing beer, according to Assaf Kohlert.  Such a profitable
enterprise could not, of course, be allowed to interfere with socialist
planning, so after about 1927, I believe, it was no longer allowed.  Economic
mis-planning was no doubt also responsible for the accumulation of surplus
grain beside the railroad tracks. Formerly it had been taken to market and
sold.  Now, while waiting to be picked up by unreliable trains, it began to
sprout.  This did nothing to increase the baking of bread, notes Alice
Baumbach, but the sprouting grain was good for the production of schnapps.

Before their economy was ruined, the villagers of Solodyri, like those of all
other such villages, were as nearly self-sufficient as they could be.  No doubt
most of them used the local windmills to make flour from grains they grew
themselves and oil from their flaxseed.  Rudolph Kaufmann, Samuel Tiede and a
Casimir family in the southern part of Solodyri owned windmills, according to
Alice Buchholz, and Robert Hadel remembers one owned by a man named Heidemann
in Solodyri, another in Neudorf, and an oil mill owned by a Sell in Ostrowka,
northwest of Neudorf.  Assaf Kohlert remembers "three or four" large windmills
in the village.  Many families seem to have had spinning wheels (Robert
Holstein's grandfather, with his seven daughters, had one, of course!), but
looms were apparently less common.  Assaf Kohlert's mother spun her linen
thread, but took it to her uncle Gottlieb Driesner in Rogowka, whose wife wove
it on a large loom.  Articles of clothing were no doubt usually made by mothers
and sisters, but tailors were available.  Goldine Klang identifies Rudolph
Brede (or Broede) as a tailor for men, and Robert Hadel's mother, Emilie, had a
shop for women, with several girls as apprentices.  (Emilie and Herman Hadel
moved to Solodyri from Horoshek in the mid-20s, lived at first with Ludwig
Lueck, and built a house in 1928 near the Luecks.  Frau Hadel's shop was
therefore a late addition to the village economy.)  These local tailors seem,
however, to have had competition from outside.  Assaf Kohlert remembers when a
large wagon arrived at his parents' farm from Horoshek with a load of "Stoff."
His parents apparently either bought a suit of clothes for his brother which
didn't fit, or else ordered a suit.  A few weeks later the merchant returned
for payment, but also a Jewish tailor named Suendel came (he may also have been
the merchant--it is not clear from Herr Kohlert's letter) with four or five
apprentices.  They "sewed from early till late" until the job was done.

Another product brought to Solodyri from outside was "Weisserd," or "white
earth," (no doubt lime) sold from door to door for whitewashing houses.  It was
mined somewhere in the region, and needed only to be mixed with water.

Shoemaking was also carried on by several farmers of Solodyri.  My own
grandfather, Karl Wilhelm Boehnert, was known as "Klossebienert" for his skill
at making wooden shoes, a skill he no doubt learned from his father, who
learned it from his father in Riesenkirch, West Prussia.  Goldine Klang
mentions a shoemaker named Wink (?) who made not only wooden shoes, but leather
"pantoffel" (?) as well, and another who made fine leather shoes, although she
does not provide his name.  Hermann Hadel also made shoes.  Also typical of
such villages, Solodyri had "several farmers with their own forges," according
to Assaf Kohlert.  Among other craftsmen were the masons, Robert and Fried (or
Fred) Hinz, and the furriers Emil Tolkmit and his son, Emil, Jr.  Julius (?)
Freigang was a cabinet maker, and my grandfather, Wilhelm Karl Boehnert, made
certain kinds of furniture, surely a skill possessed by many others.   A
generation before our reporters were born, Neudorf was the home of a
cabinetmaker named Timm whose son, August, is listed as a joiner on the S. S.
George Washington in March, 1911.  Albert Ittermann was, for a time, an
apprentice to Timm, before going on to study law, theology and eventually
become a Baptist pastor.  Solodyri also had at least one knife smith, one
Albert Bavin, but he was not among the more prosperous inhabitants of the
village.

Life in Solodyri in the mid-1920s, after the civil war, inflation and the
strains of rebuilding farms were over, must have been fairly pleasant.  Or at
least, in the words of my cousin Alice Buchholz, "life was not miserable."  Our
correspondants all have pleasant memories of their childhoods.  Goldine Klang
remembers fondly the climbing and sliding opportunities provided by haystacks,
and recounts how children "felt well" in the beautiful woods in and around the
village.  On the other hand, Alice Baumbach's mother wouldn't let her play in
the woods for fear of wild dogs and other dangers.  Nor was it considered
proper, she says, for girls to climb trees like the boys. My cousin Alice's
enjoyment of a misshapen linden tree in the pasture behind the Scheune must
mean that this particular social moré was not universal.

The village seems to have had a considerable population of puppets, but
manufactured toys were apparently scarce.  Goldine Klang recalls children
making play houses or rooms out of broken dishes and cups and visiting each
other as guests.  Of children's games I know only what has been related (in
passing) by Robert Hadel, that he played "Verstecken" and "Wilde Sau" in
haystacks, "Blinde Kuh" at some location which could not have been far from the
haystacks, and a table game called "Tcumpf" (?) or "kozyr," which he played at
the homes of August Bunkowski and Gustav Bebernick, whose wives were
sisters--Agnete and Hulda Sell.  In a village where music was so important that
the Baptist church boasted a brass choir and a string choir in addition to its
vocal chorus, it was probably not uncommon for children to "harmonize" with
others, as Goldine Klang did with Sofia Schlender.  Goldine's brother played
the violin, her sister the zither.  Music was also performed in the school, a
place which all of our former Solodyrians seem to have held in high regard.  To
this we will give our attention shortly.

I asked our correspondents to comment on relations between Germans and other
ethnic groups, namely, Jews, Ukrainians and Poles.  There seem to have been no
problems.  Martha Patzke says there were two Jews in Neudorf who apparently
were brothers and who managed a village store.  One of them, she says, lived in
a house on land given him by her father!  As for the Ukrainians, who lived
mostly in nearby Dworisch and Skolobow, relations with them also seem to have
been peaceful.  Assaf Kohlert reports that his father had good relations with
people in Dworisch, and Alice Baumbach characterizes the four Michalenko
families who lived near the border with Skolobow (one of them lived next door
to her) as "good neighbors."  A few apparently Ukrainian families lived within
Solodyri, bearing names like Beroshenko, Maidan, Jakowenko, and Dazlov.  The
names of Nartschinski, Majewski and Bunkowski, among others, show that a few
Poles, or people of Polish extraction, lived here also, often intermarrying
with Germans, as was typical in Poland and Prussia as well.  Not much can be
said on the basis of so little evidence, but ethnic differences seem to have
been happily accepted and lived with.  One of the Michalenkos served as
secretary to the Búrgermeister, August Grúnwald, a sign that he was, like
Grúnwald, among the less well-off residents of the village.  On the other hand,
in 1915, Christina Dazlov owned 22 hectares of land and Ludwig Maidan owned 23,
so Ukrainians were not inevitably poor.

There were those in Solodyri who, for one reason or another, acquired a smaller
share of the relative prosperity of the mid-20s than others.  My father's
cousin, Otto Boehnert, wrote to him in March, 1925, that he had come to
Volhynia from Siberia the previous June, but that things were so bad he wanted
to return. Only lack of money held him back.  The price of a cow, he reported,
was (once again) 25 to 50 rubles, but even so he had only one, and it was not
paid for.  For him and his family, it was "very difficult to live."  It was for
people like Otto, August Grúnwald and Arthur Hoppe that the post-Lenin
government decided to break up large and prospering farms and distribute some
of their land and machines to poorer farmers.  I do not know if my father's
cousin benefited from this, but certainly many others did.

Exactly when this property transfer began and how it was pursued is not clear
from our scanty evidence, but it seems to have been a forerunner to the full
collectivization that was established by l933.  My grandfather, Wilhelm Karl Bô
hnert, wrote to my father in March, 1925, that the land was no longer theirs,
but belonged to the state.  If anyone had a lot of land, he said, it was being
taken and given to the landless.  In some cases, the sacrifice was relatively
"minor."  August Boehnert had to allow poor neighbors to graze cattle on his
land.  Arthur Zoecknik's cow stall was expropriated. In other cases, the loss
was great. Gustav Deblitz lost four hectares to Arthur and Maria Hoppe, who
built on them a house out of materials from Zoecknik's stall!.  Ludwig Lueck
had to share his home with the Hadel family from Horoski while the Hadels built
their house (finished in 1928) on land that had belonged to the Luecks.

It appears that such land transfers may have been part of a larger land reform
that placed limits on the amount of land (four to six hectares) that a young
person could receive from his or her parents.  Alice Baumbach suggests that
Hoppe received his four hectares at about the same time that Gustav Deblitz
alloted  six to his son, Joseph.  This limitation may explain why my
grandfather granted only four hectares to my uncle, August, while "squandering"
two on my father, who was far away in America.  (It could also have been a ploy
to induce him to return).  Moreover, keeping five hectares for himself and
giving one to Emil Kaufmann, his son-in-law, left five hectares unaccounted
for.  Could they have gone to some homeless person?  Could they be the pasture
that August was forced to share with someone else's cattle?  Times were
obviously getting bad when, in 1930, Gustav Nartschinski (Alice Baumbach's
half-brother) married and received his four hectares with a cow stall, and then
had to use materials from the stall to build his house!

Along with land reform, the government resorted to increasingly punishing
taxation in the late 20s.  Since these "wealthy" farmers had increasingly less
money, this taxation increasingly required the payment of taxes in kind
("Sonstiges"), which meant paying in grain, flax, dairy products and meat
(often in the form of calves), extending even, in the last years, to things
like "spoons in the cupboard," as my aunt, Hertha, complained.  Levies began to
exceed income.  Cows were sold to meet monetary payments.  And after 1930
families were literally thrown onto the streets for non-payment.  Alice
Baumbach tells of Meta, an acquaintance of hers from the Pulin area, the second
oldest daughter of six children, whose father had already been arrested, who
was not even allowed to replace the wooden "pantoffel" she was wearing with
warm shoes when they came to eject her mother from their house in the middle of
winter.  (The father was never seen again; a son was shot, and the three
youngest children died of starvation).  Such stories could, of course, be
multiplied endlessly.  Adoline Klang's husband, Albert, was sent into exile,
along with her sister's husbands, although the authorities showed a burst of
generosity in allowing her to take a horse and wagon and a cow when she was
sent to Woroschilowgrad.

The effect of all this on the psyche of the villagers was, of course,
disastrous.  In earlier years, in the mid-20s, it was not unknown for farmers
to give aid to their less prosperous neighbors.  Friedrich Deblitz, for
example, frequently extended a helping hand to Albert Bavin, the struggling
knife smith, and his wife, who was a sister of the Hoppe brothers.  Bavin owned
no horses and only one cow.  To August Grúnwald, Deblitz gave feed for his
horse and cow.  When Arthur Hoppe was building his house on the four hectares
taken from Gustav Deblitz, Friedrich allowed the Hoppes to draw water from his
well, and his wife worried about the pregnant Maria Hoppe as their rush to
finish the house before winter required her frequent presence on a ladder.
Such a tolerant attitude was possible, perhaps, because the villagers (once
again according to Alice Baumbach) for a time believed that the government was
taking only such land as landowners really "didn't need."  Such naiveté, of
course, was to be short-lived.

It cannot be surprising that the policies of the government eventually brok the
villagers' spirit.  Laughter became rare, reports Alice Baumbach, and people
distrusted each other.  Intelligent people, she says, learned to keep their
mouths shut.  Many, like my uncle August, must have lost interest in farming.
Resistance seemed useless.  Even before the arrests and deportations began in
1929 and 1930, the

government in 1928 undertook a search for any persons who had fought against
the Reds in the civil war.  Lenin's promises had secured at least the
neutrality of the Germans, but the G.P.U. (later, the K.G.B.) located a son of
a Polish family in Solodyri, Jusik Wengelowski, who had fought for Poland,
hiding in a haystack with his cousin.  The cousin was killed and Jusik was
wounded.  Later, a younger brother was also killed.  Such events can only have
quelled any thoughts of resistance.

How many Solodyrians decided to leave Russia at this time I do not know, but
among them was Albert Klang, who made it to West Prussia.  Unfortunately, his
wife was not allowed to join him and the marriage was dissolved.  Her second
husband, Richard Nitz, was arrested in 1930, once again leaving Mrs. Klang
alone.  Another emigré was my uncle, August Boehnert.  He applied for German
citizenship in 1927, but had to escape the dragnet of 1929-30 by sleeping
outside the house before the application went through in October, 1930, and he
was free to take his family to East Prussia.  He was allowed to sell the
personal property attached to the farm, and it was purchased by Robert Tiede, a
young man who stands out as a rare person in this period who still thought of
getting ahead in this world.  He had recently bought his brother Samuel's
windmill, and needed the nearby Boehnert house to live in.  Unfortunately, his
efforts marked him as an unmistakable kulak.  He was not seen again after his
arrest in 1933.

It was in 1933 that the process of collectivization was completed by the
establishment of a kolchose with the farm of August Freigang as its center and
the house of Robert Holstein's father as an office.  The process began of
requiring farmers to tear down their houses and rebuild them near the center
point.  A bell tower was erected and each day it rang to signal all to assemble
and received work assignments.  Now arrest and exile awaited anyone who failed
to show proper enthusiasm for the system.  This was probably the case with
August Bunkowski, who forfeited his home the next year to a Jewish family named
Singermann, was accepted for a short time in the Bebernik's home before being
sent into exile.  One wonders if Robert Tiede's arrest in 1933 was also such a
case.

In spite of the government's successful intimidation, however, farmers in
Solodyri and several neighboring Ukrainian villages (such as Bolarke and
Skolobov)did stage an abortive uprising when in 1933 hunger and hopelessness
overcame their fears.  As Robert Hadel tells the story, the uprising occurred
in the late fall when people from several villages got together to march on the
village council (Dorfrat) and the grain warehouse (Getreidelager).  After
several days of this, government troops were stationed at the Lueck farm (which
was the location of the Dorfrat).  "The next morning, when the starving masses
marched from the forest near Beberniks' farm, with black and red flag (Leben
oder Tod) toward the "Dorfrat," they made it to about the Bunkowskis' house,
when hell broke loose.  With mounted bayonets, the soldiers went after the
people in the barns of Bunkowskis.  Many got arrested and never seen again."
Some of them must have fled southwards, for Alice Baumbach remembers seeing the
marchers running across her father's fields.  Among those executed, according
to Alice, were Ewald Heidemann from Liski, a man named Naschelewski from
Neudorf and, most astonishing of all, two teenage girls from Rogowka, Selma
Gatzke and Olga Riske.  Alice, by the way, places this uprising in March and
April, 1932.  Any assistance in dating this event will be appreciated.

As if not enough people had been killed or exiled by 1935, in that year began
yet more imprisonments and disappearances.  All the families I have
corresponded with suffered in one way or another, and the details are
mind-numbing.  A woefully partial account is all I am capable of rendering.
Robert Holstein lost two brothers, Arthur and Adolf, along with Adolf's son.
Adolf Kohlert's family was exiled in 1935 to Cherkov where he died in 1937.
Richard Weiss and Emil Tolkmit were exiled in 1935.  Albert Klang had already
been taken in 1930; his family was sent to Woroschilograd in 1935.  Of Gustav
Deblitz's four sons, Joseph and Alexander were sent to the Don region in 1935,
and by 1937 all four were imprisoned.  Alexander and Benno were executed.
Alexander's wife, Klara (Hinz, daughter of August Hinz and sister of Bertha and
Martha) wound up in Kazakhstan until 1989.  Gustav's wife, Julianna (Liedtke),
and daughter, Elsa, were sent to the Urals where Elsa eventually suffered
mental breakdown.  The other daughter, Adina, was never found after the war,
nor was the wife of the fourth son, Nikolai.  Sofia (Driechel) and Eugen
Bunkowski managed to see each other now and then during their exile, and
finally managed to get married in 1948.  Robert Holstein's father was one of
few who lived out their years at home, in his case in Rogowka.  After his house
became an office for the Rogowka kolchose, he was given a small house to live
in until his death in 1941 at the age of 81.  Old age seems to have been a form
of protection.  My own grandparents, though they certainly could have been
classified as kulaks, were allowed to live out their lives in Neudorf after
their son, August, had sold the farm and moved to Germany.  Even their "good"
fortune had its bitter side.  The house they moved into in Neudorf had belonged
to their daughter-in-law's mother, Emilie Lange.  This unfortunate woman's
husband, Daniel, had died of a nervous breakdown in 1930, and she had lost her
house due to non-payment of taxes.

It seems that a government that solves its obedience problems by imprisonment
and exile is not loath to use such methods against its own functionaries.
Among those imprisoned in these years was a much respected schoolteacher,
Richard Schulz. Goldine Klang has sent a photo, probably taken in 1932,  of
Schulz sitting at a piano in the new schoolhouse finished about two or three
years previously.  With him in the photo were Leonard Sell, the former
Búrgermeister and good friend of Schulz, (and Goldine's uncle), Sell's two
nieces, Martha and Bertha Hinz, Schulz's wife, Alma (also Schulz, from the
Martinowka area), and her sister, Irma, with "die schoene Paula", who lived in
Schulz's house and also seems to have been a beloved teacher.  The picture was
taken in the first grade classroom.  The wooden screen in the background was
movable to expose the stage behind it where musical and dramatic performances
were given, and occasional moving pictures.  The grand piano, which I initially
found to be a surprising possession for a village school in Russia, had
(according to Goldine) been expropriated from a wealthy family, but (according
to Alice Baumbach) it belonged to Richard and Alma Schulz.  The photo is
thought to have been taken by Robert Schoen, recently arrived as a teacher and
soon to marry Bertha Hinz.  Alice Baumbach thinks the group may have been
together to celebrate Christmas or New Years.  Schulz was not merely posing at
the piano, for he regularly led Gesangstunde from the keyboard for musical
villagers.

It was a Christmas gathering at his home (in 1933?) that proved to be Schulz's
undoing.  Having invited the Friedrich Deblitzes and another couple for the
evening, they listened to Christmas music from Berlin on Schulz's radio, a
forbidden form of activity.  Alice Baumbach thinks someone must have been
listening from outside the house, for the incident was reported to
Búrgermeister August Grúnwald, and Schulz was sentenced to three years in
prison in Zhitomir.  Perhaps he was lucky.  Alice thinks that in 1937 he would
have been shot.  Alma soon moved to Zhitomir and was allowed to take the piano
with her.  Irma worked as a servant to a doctor, and Schulz's children, Erhard
and Irene, studied to become music teachers.  In 1936, after Schulz gained his
release, the family was moved to Ufa in the Urals.

According to Goldine Klang, Schulz, like all the teachers of this period, was a
party member, trained at the pedagogical institute in Odessa, and sent to
Solodyri by officials in Pulin.  It was Schulz who directed the building of the
new, six- or seven-grade school (opinions vary) that replaced the old four
grade school that stood near the Baptist Bethaus on the road to Skolobow.
Another teacher named Damsch had, like Paula Schulz, apparently taught in the
old school and stayed on after Schulz's arrival.  Other teachers who came in
the early 1930s were Robert Schoen, Gustav Gensch and David Zander.  I have
learned nothing about them except that Damsch and Schoen also ran afoul of the
authorities and were imprisoned or exiled, Schoen going in 1937.

Judging from what our correspondents have said about their few years in school,
one gets the feeling that it was a very important part of their lives, in spite
of the dual role it played after the Revolution of 1917.  Before the war, the
school had been German and Lutheran.  Its teachers were also cantors in the
Lutheran Church.  Two teachers before Damsch were named Schwanke and Rohleder.
Baptist children attended this school but had to pay for it.   After the
Revolution, the school continued to teach German and, even as late as the early
30s, present the works of Goethe and Schiller, including such plays by Schiller
as Kabale und Liebe (according to Goldine Klang).  On the other hand, it was
also an agency of government propaganda, as most schools are in any country,
but in this case, a propaganda that was at cross purposes with the German
culture of these villages.  Martha Patzke notes, with a touch of sarcasm, that
she had to learn German when she began school in 1919 from a Russian teacher,
and although the shelves were full of books, they were all in Russian, which
meant they were "written by God Himself."  Learning German, she says, involved
a lot of memorization.  The next year, a teacher named Willy Reder (or Roeder)
arrived from Germany, but he had come, says Martha, because "he had heard of
the great freedom the Communists had offered."  From him she learned the
rudiments of history and geography.  But her chief memory of her three years of
school was constantly hearing how things would be better in the future for
children.  They made all kinds of promises, she says, and many believed them,
especially the poor who otherwise had nothing.  (I should note that, since
Martha grew up in Neudorf, she may have been writing about a school in Neudorf.
In the 1880s, a man named Peter Ittermann taught Russian and German in Neudorf,
and there was still a three- or four-grade school there in the early 1930s,
according to Alice Baumbach).

One can perhaps sympathize with the aspirations of the revolutionaries of the
early 1920s, but, as is always true of mystical and revolutionary experiences,
they must become institutionalized in order to be maintained.  At some point
which I have yet to learn, a ranking system for children was established
(described by Robert Holstein) in order to keep these revolutionary hopes
alive. A child in the first grade might be an "October Child," then proceed to
the rank of "Pioneer," and eventually achieve the status of "Komsomol" by the
tenth grade, without which one could not graduate.  This was, of course, not
possible in Solodyri, with its seven-grade school.  And, since no other
correspondents have even mentioned this system, I am unaware that it was even
an important part of the school in Solodyri.  But Goldine Klang assures us that
the school was used for celebrating Soviet holidays with music, songs and
plays.

In spite of the political slant of the school, I get the impression that the
children enjoyed going to it and valued what they learned there.  If their
education included propaganda, the propaganda failed!  Perhaps it was the fine
character of Richard Schulz that saved the day.  And for Goldine Klang, the
expropriated piano offered her the opportunity to accompany her brother on the
violin and her sister on the zither.

On the other hand, children were required to work in the kolchose fields at
certain times such as planting and harvesting.  On such days, they all marched
to the kolchose refectory at the Freigang farm where Alice Bavin had prepared
some soup (Hirsebrei) and ladled it out.  Since the Deblitz family was on
especially good terms with the Bavins, Alice Baumbach admits she sometimes got
a little extra helping.

Along with the school, the cultural life of our villages was dominated by the
church, or rather, the churches, since Solodyri had a Lutheran church as well
as its Baptist Bethaus.  But the largest church in the area was the Baptist
Kapelle in Neudorf, built in 1906, a large red brick edifice with seating for
about 1500 worshippers in its nave and 500 more in its surrounding balcony.
This large sanctuary was used only once a month when Baptists from several
surrounding villages met there, or on other special occasions.  On the other
three Sundays, Neudorf Baptists met in a side chapel, and Solodyrian Baptists
(who made up about half the population of the village) met in their Bethaus not
far from the Lutheran Church.  My correspondents have all been Baptists, and I
have so far learned nothing about the Lutheran church except that it had a
brass choir that rehearsed, says Alice Baumbach, on either Friday or Saturday
evenings, and could be heard as far away as the Deblitz farm where Alice's
mother made sure the windows were open for the occasion.  The Baptist Bethaus
also had a small brass choir as well as a mixed choir of singers.  During the
1920s, its services were led by two deacons, Gottlieb Driesner and Karl
Deblitz.  In 1933, it was converted into a veterinary clinic.

The following year, the large Kapelle in Neudorf became a place where "bringing
in the sheaves" took on a literal and unwelcome meaning; it became a granary in
the service of communal agriculture.  This must have been an especially cruel
blow to any who were left who had participated in constructing this beautiful
church.  Its red bricks were apparently made from local clay, and whoever
designed it produced a noteworthy house of worship, as photographs taken by Don
Miller show.  It was in this church that the great interest in music among
these German Baptists received its fullest expression.  In addition to its
choir and brass choir (Posaunerchor), it had a choir of string players.  In
1908 (?) the Baptists of the region held a "Posaunenfest" in Neudorf, with its
Kapellmeister, Josef Ittermann, directing. It was such a success that another
fest was held shortly thereafter in Sorotschin, with 85 brass players coming
from Sorotschin, Horoschik, Iwanowitsch, Horstschick and Rutkowski-Chutor. The
director was again Josef Ittermann.  Rev. L. Braier, in his report of this
event to Der Hausfreund, noted that, as he traveled around from station to
station that summer, meetings

were better attended when brass choirs played, and he urged the players not to
grow weary in this work, but to continue right up until they joined the great
choir above!

It would be interesting indeed to know the history of these Baptist churches in
Volhynia, and perhaps some day sources will come to light.  Baptists were among
the immigrants from Poland in the early 1860s, and organizing them fell first
to a Rev. Alf, The major work, however, was done by Rev. Karl Ondra, who was
converted in 1863 in Sorotschin, and whose name is identified with the church
in Neudorf as well as others.  In a trial in Horoshek in 1877, Ondra was
accused of instigating the Stundist movement among Russian believers, and was
sent back to Poland for three years.  To take his place, Rev. S. Lehman, Rev.
Vogel and Rev. Braier and others came to Volhynia.  Large churches developed in
a few villages, and smaller churches in neighboring villages became "stations"
of these larger ones.  Thus Solodyri, Jazevits and Horoshek were stations of
Neudorf.  In addition to Ondra and Lehmann, Neudorf's pastors were Rev. Mohr,
who baptized my father in 1906, Rev. Eduard Wuerch, who was at the church from
at least 1906 to 1926, when he went to the church in Cholosna (which he left in
1928 to go to Canada), and Rev. Wilhelm Schmidgall, who is remembered fondly by
our correspondents.  His last sermon in Neudorf was preached on Pentecost,
1934.  He died two years later in a labor camp, a fate he may have shared with
Rev. Heinrich Baumbach, Alice's father-in-law, whose last pastorate was
Sorotschin and who simply disappeared.

Before the end came, the Baptist movement throughout Volhynia was vibrant and
growing.  In the last years before religion began to be energetically opposed
by the government, revivals occurred, and even in ordinary years, the number of
baptisms in the outdoors Neudorf "Tauftisch" (or indoors, for there was a
baptistery in the Kapelle as well) exceeded one hundred each year.  What was
probably the last revival was reported by my grandfather in a letter of
February, 1928, when he was still able to believe that the people still enjoyed
religious freedom.  How quickly things were to change!  But at least we have
the small consolation that, even though the present-day Solodyri/Neudorf bears
scarcely any resemblance to its former condition, the Kapelle is now being
rebuilt by Ukrainian Baptists.
