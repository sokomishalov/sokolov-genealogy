Contract

Akkermann, 30 Aug 1868


I, at the end undersigned citizen of Reval, Andreas Adam Hellberg, and proxy of
her Highness, the Princess Kotschubei, born Countess Benkendorf, as filed in
the ______? district court on the 27th Jun of 1860 sub No. 75 confirm the
contract with the colonists of the Akkerman district:

From the Colony Teplitz:  Friedrich Bender, Konrad Weidenbach, Gottlieb
Kraemer, Andreas Kraemer, Gottlieb Schuh, Johannes Faelchle, Georg Schmauder,
Leopolt Staeub and Simon Kurz.
From the colony Dennewitz: Michael Freitag, Martin Joerke
From the colony Beresina:  Andreas Fregin with colleagues to have this contract
finalized under the following conditions:

1.  I, Hellberg, have handed over the parcel of land assigned by the Princess
Kotschubei to the above named colonists.  It is in the Akkermann district in
the Province of Bessarabia, consisting of 2, 230 dessiatines including any
establishments, all wells and a windmill located within the below defined
borders and for lease for 12 years. Namely calculated from 23 Oct 1868 to 23
Oct 1880 for the founding of a colony with the name "Neufall".

In the East this land borders a furrow passing through the Budak Plantation,
beginning at the dam to the orchard, then to the woods and to the estate of
owner Mosarowitsch. On the other side, beginning with the farm of the Budak
Economy, to the land parcels of the Princess Kotschubei, which she leases to
the inhabitants of the Budak villages Kleinbalabanka., Kajabei and Adamofka.

Princess Kotschubei keeps three hundred dessiatines during the first years of
the existence of this contract until Oct 23, 1869, namely the land between the
new Koscharen (sheep pens?), the land of the Budak proprietor and the land in
the Budak valley. The therein located new Koscharen (sheep pens?) and trees are
available to them for herding cattle, horses and sheep. It is established under
item 2 in this contract with the conditions and a lease payment in the amount
of 600 Rubel and a fee of 15 Rubel.

2.   We, the above named colonists commit ourselves to each pay to the princess
during the first 6 years 1 Rubel 75 Kopek annually for use of the land and
after six years each year the amount of 2 Rubels 25 Kopek for each dessjatin
semi-annually in advance. Namely to pay the first half on 23 Oct and the 2nd
half on 23 Apr. 1.

3.   We colonists commit ourselves until the end of this contract to take care
of all the basic fees in connection with the leased land willingly. Also to
annually deposit 5 Kopek per dessiatine payable to Princess Kotschubei at each
pay period for fees and lease.

4.  We colonists are bound during the first 4 years of the lease by this
contract to build 32 houses, each measuring 7 Faden 2 Arschin long, 4 Faden and
4 Arschin wide. In one half of the loft to make 2 rooms for foot baths painted
with oil paint. In the middle an add-on for a kitchen and in the other half a
room made of stone or trampled dirt.  The building made of trampled dirt is to
have a stone foundation in the height of one Arschin.  The outer walls have to
be covered with wood slats for support   The princess Kotschubei gives each
colonist the right to take down the sheep pens in the Budak and use the
materials to build the houses.  All other necessary materials are to be
acquired at our own expense.  The colonists also are obliged to plant no less
than 40 trees per house at their own expense and to take care of them.

T81-634-5435004-012

My Experiences in the Lutheran Parish Rectory in Russia.
by Anna Schrenk
as Translated by Tony Kienzle - Silverdale Washington

(This record was found on DAI film T81-634, frame 5435004.)

My experience extends for many years, for our dear father was active as a
pastor in Russia for 45 years.  Of those 45, 19 were in the Trans-Caucasus, 22
years in the provost district of Odessa in the Glueckstal colony and 4 years as
a resident spiritual leader in the charitable institution of Provost Alber.
He came to Provost Alber's assistane because Provost Alber did not have enough
physical strength to carry out all the duties of the office of this large
parish.

From this last place of assignment my father returned to his German homeland as
a 75 year old tired and ailing fellow.  I was able to care for him another 3
years until November 1911 when he entered into his final rest with the Lord's
people.

Following his death in the spring of 1912 I returned to Russia, following the
call of a young pastor, Pastor Winger (?)3 in the colony of Hoffnungstal in the
Odessa district.  Since Pastor Winger was single and had 2 younger sisters to
look after, I was to take over the matronly duties (mother figure, if you will)
in the parsonage and in the parish, I represented the pastors' wives. I had
done this work in my father's congregation or parish a long time, because our
dear mother had passed away a number of years previously as of 189(3?)

The first years of my stay in Hoffnungstal went by calmly and peacefully as I
was used to from former times.  Then along came World War I and created an
entirely different situation.  The start of the war, marked the start of
suffering for the German colonists of Russia; and suffering for the Lutheran
parish rectory.

At first it was mainly the congregations and parish houses (rectories) in the
west and southwest of the empire, which were in danger because they were soon
near the combat zone.  It was not distress due to religious views that one had
to endure, but distress due to the fact that we were German.  Suddenly, they
(the Russians) viewed the Germans as enemies and traitors, although they
formerly had lived with them in peace and harmony.  To be sure the sons of
German colonists and many heads of families faithful to their oath of
allegiance, went to war on the Russian side without grumbling, I am not aware
of any case that one of ours would have become a traitor or deserter. They did
leave however heavy hearted, that I can attest to, because they would be
fighting their German brothers, against the German homeland, to which the
German colonists held close ties, associations in spite of the fact that none
or at best only a few local boys knew anything personally about Germany and in
spite of the fact that the mother country Germany ever concerned itself with
whose who were scattered in far off Russia.

Whenever there was a draft, a farewell church service was held with communion
and these church services were always very touching. With time they were
forbidden as was later the case with preaching in German.  Pastors helped by
putting together appropriate Biblical texts and read them.  Meanwhile again and
again appropriate verses were sung.  These church services were well attended
and brought a special blessing, that one was appropriately or justly introduced
to the Bible.

In the first two years of the war, this prohibiting of preaching in German
didn't exist, on the other hand it was a punishable offense of 3,000 Rubles
(equivalent to 6,000  Reichmarks) or a 3 month jail sentence to be caught
speaking German on the street.  Since we were a closed German settlement we
could get by without these punishments.  However it often occurred that someone
on patrol caught somebody in the act of speaking German, minutes were written
down, but most people were able to buy off through money or food items. I am
aware of one case, where one of the leading men in the village felt it was
below his dignity to buy himself off and would rather go to prison.

Young Pastor Winger (Wingeler?), with whom I was working was conscripted in
1914-1915 as a field chaplain, but returned after 6 months, because he was not
able to hide his German sentiment to the leaders of the military establishment.
It wasn't quite a year later, that one night (they always chose to come by
during the night) he was given orders to leave (expelled) because of his German
sentiment. Not only he, but also all our fellow workers a Baltic teacher, who
was living here as a retirees, the same for all the women on staff and a fellow
doing a year's internship with us. All were forced to leave the village within
48 hours and moved 100 kilometers (62 miles) east of here.  I was the only one
excluded from this order, even though I had the greatest German sentiment of
all having been educated and trained in Germany. Even today I consider it a
miracle of the Lord, how that the Almighty protected me during the entire war
period from the dangers I was subject to. "How many times did the merciful Lord
spread His protective winds over me!"

Due to the pleading to a higher official in Odessa, the order was cancelled
this time.  However a few months after Pastor Winger had married, he got
another such order and this time he had to leave and shortly afterward also his
young wife.  They stayed with many others, mostly Baltic pastors in Saratov.
Pastor Winger was able to transfer later to Karkow.

I gave up my house and household items and moved back to Grossliebental, where
I had formerly worked in the charitable institutions and where Provost Alber
had expected me.  That was the start of the most difficult time for me.  It was
in the summer of 1916 when I transferred there and even as of then Odessa and
the surroundings had become border area.  There was always a military presence
there and one was always guarded and observed, even the outlawing of preaching
German came to be during this time.  Provost Alber who had been retired by now,
but was still active in his office, now had a young pastor, a Pastor Koch who
was Provost Alber's assistant and later became Alber's successor.

On Christmas eve 1916 an entire regiment of reservists from the eastern
Gouvernements were hurriedly being marched to the Rumanian front via
Grossliebental.  These poor people were dead tired and many let themselves fall
on the soaked street because they simply were not able to go on.  On this night
we had to quarter these reservists, up to 50-60 fellows.  Even in the parish
rectory we had a number.  They were from Siberia. I felt so sorry for these
guys, that were driven on as sheep to their slaughter.  That was our Christmas
1916.

In February following Christmas came the revolution. One was paralyzed when it
was said the Czar had been dethroned--the person who up to that time had been
honored almost as the god.  For a few months as long as the Kerensky government
was at the helm, we lived relatively calm, but the hope that the horrible war
would come to an end was never fulfilled.  To end the external war and replace
that one with an internal, civil war was granted to the Bolshevist government.

Soon there were street fights in and around Odessa between Bolshevist rebels
and Ukrainians until ultimately the Bolshevists were victorious.  Then began
the Bolshevist agitation.

Previously under the Czarist government and Kerensky Government there were 2
decisive events.  The first was the Congress of Black Sea Germans in Odessa,
where representatives from all the districts in the south were gathered and the
so-called liquidation law was presented, which had been signed by the Czar
himself.  The German colonists were given the choice of surrendering their
German character and joining the Russian Orthodox church or leave Russia as
poor as they arrived without any monetary compensation.  Almost unanimously
they declared themselves ready to leave the country, than to deny their faith
and their German character.

The second significant event was during the Kerensky Government, as I recall.
There were Serbian officers stationed in Grossliebental, who were Austrian
prisoners of war mostly Czechoslovakians that were to form a new regiment for
the Russian-Rumanian Front. Concerning the terrible things that these poor
guys were subjected to and the oath of allegiance they were to swear to the
Russian flag, much could be written.  I don't want to go into all that here but
rather tell of an event involving the parish rectory.  One evening a Serbian
officer was treacherously assassinated.  The Germans were accused and the day
after there was a command by the Governor General that all of Grossliebental
had to be cleared within 48 hours to the eastern provinces, I think it was to
the Ural Mountains area.  The distress had reached its zenith.  The village
leaders now urgently requested the 2 pastors to approach the Governor General
and plead to have the command be declared null and void.  After the leaders
first earnestly prayed, after they were spiritually strengthened they took off
for Odessa in their official dress which was considered something of great
value at that time yet in Russia.  In their official dress they approached the
higher powers and were pleading for the parish. At first the person of the
upper power was merciless but when the elder Provost, Mr. Alber was presented
he became friendlier and finally declared the command null and void, with the
stipulation that within a week's time the guilty person was delivered.  With
the Lord Almighty's hand it was successful. It was one of the Czech soldiers
and so the disaster was averted.  Such events took place and the sword hung
over the parish and the parish rectory.

It was different in 1917. The Bolshevist Regiment began to spread out and
extend themselves. At first there were attacks that constantly kept the colony
in fear, so that every night 60 men had to stand guard or watch at the village
entrances The parish rectory was especially threatened by these attacks, so
that one never knew at night if one would be alive the next morning.  Praise
the Lord, He watched over us and never let any thing happen to us.

Over time however Bolshevists had firmly entrenched themselves in the village.
They hoisted the red flag over the village office building and over the
hospital and we were in their hands.

One of the first deeds of the Bolshevists was supposed to be the assassination
of the bourgeoisie in and around Odessa after all the Czarist officers had been
liquidated as much as did not escape the clutches of the Bolshevist rebels.

The people of Odessa were successful in requesting help and assistance from the
German military via telegram and on St. Bartholomew's day (August 24), a German
and Austrian company of troops reached the city and our village.  What a joy it
was and many grateful prayers went up to the Lord.  Immediately all the Red
Bolshevists disappeared and we were able to breathe freely again, since Odessa
was occupied by the German military. That was in March 1918.

For a few months we in the parish rectory were very grateful. Occasionally we
had visitors of high ranking German military personnel.  This was always a
reason to rejoice.  So it was one day in August that the commanding officer of
the occupying army, a Duke Waldersee and his Adjutants were dining with us in
the parish rectory.  Provost Alber requested Duke Waldersee's assistance in
securing our permission to immigrate to Germany. Duke Waldersee followed
through on the kind request and promised Provost Alber, his wife and me to seek
such permission within 2 weeks time.  He appealed to his friend, the King of
Wuerttemberg.  After 12 days the official permission was in Odessa from whom
the military officials gave the directive to be ready to leave in 3 days time.
We were to be taken along by a German military train to the German borders.  We
departed from Odessa in the final days of September 1918 with a grateful heart.
We were joined by 60 young men who enlisted for combat on the western front.
To be sure I departed with the firm idea of returning to my place of work here
in Grossliebental following the cessation of the war.  Unfortunately that never
came to pass.  Today I am so grateful to the lord in retrospect that He plucked
me out of this country (Russia), which became a place of hell for many of my
fellow countrymen.  With a sorrowful heart I remember them and pray to God He
will strengthen their faith and preserve them.
