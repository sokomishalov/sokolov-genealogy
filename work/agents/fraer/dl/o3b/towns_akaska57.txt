Akaska, South Dakota
Golden Anniversary Historical Book
1907 - 1957

DEDICATION Dedicated to the memory of those early pioneers in thankfulness for
the heritage that is ours.

Forward We trust that you may enjoy this brief history of our village and the
families in the surrounding area, some of whom were living here before the
beginning of our village.

Through good times and bad times they have elected to remain bound by family
ties, necessity and a belief that here was a good place for them and their
families to live.

The time allotted was far too short for the great amount of subject matter to
be covered and if there are discrepancies and omissions of pertinent items, it
is only because of lack of time to sift and weigh what was presented and
because much of our material is not first hand -- memories are fleeting and no
two individuals see a situation in the same way.

We take great pleasure in presenting our 50th Anniversary Booklet to you.

             History of Akaska

The town of Akaska was established in the spring of 1907, on the west half of
NW 1/4 of Section 2 and the N 1/2 of the NE 1/4 Section 3-121-77 west of the 5th
P. M. The NW 1/4 of Section 2 was homesteaded by a man by the name of Singleton
Smith. who received patent on this tract of land on June 1, 1888 during
President Grover Cleveland's administration.

The N 1/2 of the N 1/2 of Section 3 or lots 1, 2, 3 and 4 was homesteaded by a
man named Evan M. Isaac, who received patent dated November 2, 1903 during the
administration of President Theodore Roosevelt. The NW 1/4 of Section 2 changed
hands a number of times and was sold on May 27, 1901 by the then owners, Fred
Daggett and Nancy B. Daggett, his wife, and Fred N. Waite and Dora Waite, his
wife, to the Iowa Livestock Investment Co., who on December 6, 1905 also
acquired title to Lots 1, 2, 3 and 4 or the N 1/2 of the N 1/2 of Section
3-121-77. On February 8, 1907 Iowa Livestock Investment co. sold the NW 1/4, of
Section 2 and the N 1/2 of the NE 1/4 or Lots 1 and 2 of Section 3 to the Dakota
Town Lot Co., of which Thomas A. Way was president. This then became the site
for the town of Akaska. The W 1/2 of the NW 1/4 of Section 2 and the N 1/2 of
the NE 1/4 of Section 3 was staked out into lots, blocks, outlots, streets and
alleys by J. C. Carpenter, deputy state surveyor. The townsite consists of 25
blocks and 7 outlots. This survey was completed on or before the 7th day of
May, 1907. The entire area was extremely heavily populated at that time. It is
safe to say that there were more than a million inhabitants in the Akaska area.
The whole Swan Creek Valley was a huge prairie dog town, populated by prairie
dogs, ground owls and rattlesnakes.

The first family to move into what was to be the town of Akaska was the John
Kalmbach family, who came here from a farm 13 1/2 miles southeast of Akaska the
last days of March in 1907. The first child to. be born in Akaska was Carrie
Kalmbach now Mrs. Rudolph C. Berg of Onaka, S. D.

The Dakota, Minnesota and Pacific railroad company was buying right-of-ways in
February 1907 and with the arrival of warm weather, grading operations were
soon in full swing. One camp was located near where the brick factory was later
built. This camp was known as Brown's Camp. Other camps were strung all along
the right-of-way at one-half to a mile intervals. The town started to build up
very rapidly, the lumber being hauled from Selby. Many of the business places
being in operation by the time the railroad was in operation which was late in
the summer of 1907.

The M. & St. L. railway company bought up the right-of-ways from the Dakota,
Minnesota and Pacific railway, laid the tracks and gave service until the line
was abandoned in 1941, leaving Akaska and other towns along the line without
railroad service.

Daniel Schanzenbach Sr., and his sons, Jake and Fred, were operating a grocery
and general merchandise store. Frank Stark started a grocery and meat market.
Otto Doherr was cashier of the Swan Creek State Bank, Karl J. Kundert started
the Akaska State Bank and Fred Griffin started to publish a newspaper. Bill
Krinkle had a hotel, George Ballensky a saloon and Jake Repp had his house
moved in from Evarts and because he couldn't get his blacksmith shop moved
right away, he had to build a structure which he used temporarily. (This
building is now occupied by Edwin Schooling) .

After the railroad came in the Imperial Lumber Co., put up a lumber yard of
which Henry B. Foster was manager. The Central Lumber Co., put in a yard of
which Aug. Rettke was to become manager. Gottlieb Huber and his brother, Jake
bought a grain elevator and also handled a complete line of farm implements.
(The pool hall now owned by Bob Berg was the original implement shed. ) The
Pacific Elevator Co., also put up a grain elevator. John Neumiller Sr., started
a meat market. One of the amusing features of the town was the fact that it had
no jail and the town was full of construction workers and rowdy characters and
when they got out of bounds, Jake Repp, who was the Town Marshal, would escort
them to the railroad siding and with the help of others, would boost them into
an empty box car and lock the door on them until they had sobered up or cooled
off.

There were no wells in town and all water for drinking and household use had to
be carried or hauled from the creek. The railroad grading crew had what they
called a "water monkey." He had a sort of yoke that fit partly around his neck
and rested on his shoulders. This yoke extended past his shoulders a few inches
and at each end a wire was attached with a hook on it on which the water pails
would hang and it kept him busy all the time carrying water for the workers.
One man, who homesteaded southwest of town moved back to Minnesota. He has a
spring in his back yard that runs the year around and when he was back here
years later, he said every time he looks at that spring he thinks of how d...
dry he used to be when he was in South Dakota. Most of the people that
homesteaded here that came from the states farther east had claim shacks of
frame construction but nearly all of the people who came here direct from
Europe had either sod or mud brick louses and these were usually the people who
stayed here. Maybe not because they wanted to stay but because they didn't have
the money to go back to the place they came from. There were no graded roads,
no telephone lines and very few fences and when people were out in the winter
time and got caught in a blizzard, quite often they would get lost and freeze
to death. It was sort of an unwritten law at that time to leave a lighted lamp
in the window all night when a blizzard was raging so anyone lost in the storm
had a better chance of finding shelter.

          Odds and Ends, Snips 'n Snaps, This 'n That

These are an accumulation of historical notes which the readers of this book

Chas. Griffin homesteaded the land Carl Kuehl lives on.

Frank Gehrki on May 1, 1907 became assistant cashier of the Akaska State Bank.
Cashier later until Jan. 1, 1909 when the bank was sold to K. J. Kundert. His
wife, Agnes L. Ward, homesteaded southeast of town. He still owns it.

First newspaper was named "Akaska Leader."

Martin Heiser, now of Tolstoy, was first section foreman in Akaska.

The first printing office was started in 1907 by Fred Griffin. It was burned
down in a 1908 fire. In 1910 the printer was J. P. Halloran.

Two lumber yards started in 1907. Central, managed by August Rettke, and
Imperial by Jake Gehrki. Later by Long.

First restaurant operated by Stein.

The band under the direction of John Law, had for some of its members Jake D.,
Fred D., Christ D., and John J. Schanzenbach, Johnny Ballensky, Richard
Schweinfort and Jacob G., George G., and Christ G. Schilling.

The baseball team had Jake D., George D., Fred D., Christ D., T. J., C. J., and
Jake J. Schanzenbach and Richard Schweinfort. The baseball diamond was
northwest of the elevators in the pastures.

The first business building was a bank named the Akaska State Bank and was in a
building moved from Selby by Tony Birkholt and Myler. They got stuck in the
creek north of Akaska so George Schanzenbach had a team of horses handy and
they pulled them out. When they got to town Myler paid Schanzenbach by check
and he walked into the bank and cashed it at once as the bank was all set up
for business. Soon after this another bank was started. Doherr was the cashier
and this was known as the Swan Creek State Bank. This building was burned in
the 1908 fire.

In 1908 a big fire swept out a block on the north side of Main street.
Buildings destroyed included: Swan Creek Bank, Pool Hall and Hotel by Kringle,
the Printing Office by Griffin, Saloon by Ballensky Bros., Hardware Store by
Watteke, and the Butcher Shop by Neumiller.

A second fire in Akaska in 1916 and on the south side of Main street destroyed
the Kundert Store and Starks Butcher Shop.

The Akaska Clay Works, producing clay bricks, started in 1909. They dug an
artesian well nearby from which there was enough natural gas to furnish the Dan
Schanzenbach Sr., home with gas for heating and cooking and also several other
homes were supplied. A second artesian well was dug by the railroad near the
north elevator.

R. A. Tofflemire and E. F. Haynes operated the Akaska Real Estate co.

For a brief period of time Dr. Heme served this territory, living here with his
wife and 2 sons.

Geo. Schanzenbach ran a livery barn and Ed Bertsch, a dray line.

The first postmaster was a Miss Tofflemire and the business was conducted in
the Haynes-owned "Akaska Real Estate office. '

The building housing the present L & E grocery store has quite a history. There
was a town named Collinsville just east of Bangor (across the road from the
monument). A Mr. Collins first owned and operated the Collinsville Store and
later a family named Nesbitt lived in the second floor rooms. Rudolf Chesky
purchased this building and moved it across to Bangor where he operated a store
for many years. When the county seat was moved to Selby the store was sold to
someone who had it moved to Akaska where it has been used for various
businesses since then.

                 SWAN CREEK VALLEY

In the 1880's and 90's the Swan Creek Valley was used by a few ranchers namely:

Boehmer & Whitlock, who ran cattle and horses at the mouth of Swan creek down
on the Missouri river.

Jack Hendrickson, who ran horses and cattle on the south fork where Fred
Ritter's home place is now located. The elder Ritter purchased the Jack
Hendrickson range.

Phil Lewis and Willis Wilson ran horses a little north and mostly west of
Akaska. Some of the springs still are known as "Lewis Springs."

Dagget & Waite ran sheep in the Akaska valley and north including the Albert
Kulm property. They had one sheep camp Just west of the present town limits.

Sam Glerup ran horses and cattle south and east of Akaska, selling out in 1920
to Jackson & Ridgeway. This range is now owned by W. R. Crawford.

This takes in the Swan Creek Valley close to Lowry where the Lars Anderson
family had settled about 1885. Others had come and moved on after selling their
homesteads to these few ranchers. One quarter section in particular was owned
by the Moody Bible Institute, -- some one must have thought that it was either
so great or so worthless that only the Lord knew what to do with it!

The farmers came in 1898 and early 1900 and settled in earnest. They began
breaking sod for wheat, flax and speltz. Walter Crawford can remember when his
father bought speltz for 11¢ per bushel. Later they got the fever and did some
sod breaking in order to raise some feed.

                     Then . . . and Now

In this beautiful land of ours, we all see prosperity, modernism and mechanism
as this world has never seen before, but now let's stop and look back. Once
this very land was bare, no fences, no roads, no farms, no fields or power
lines. Nothing except grass knee deep and off and on a few trees along a fast
running creek or river.

Such was the picture of the early day settler. He often traveled for many days
'til he found a spot that was to his liking and not yet claimed by someone
else. This land he would carefully look over for a suitable place on which to
build a home.

He had a covered wagon drawn by oxen or if he was a bit wealthier he owned a
team of horses and a cow or two. The wife had a few hens and a pretty colored
rooster. So precious were these birds that they were often pets. There was a
kitten and a dog. In the wagon were all their other worldly goods. The most
necessary items was all they could bring. Bedding, clothes, cooking utensils,
knives and an ax, a pick, spade, fork, a water bucket, kerosene lantern, some
food and grain and seeds carefully tucked away.

Now here they were. There was no lumber except the wagon. The problem was how
were they going to build "the shelter?" In Dakota it gets cold in winter. Yes,
very cold, and the blizzards at times become so severe that one has little
chance to live through them without shelter of some kind; so there wasn't time
to waste!

Many found that to make blocks or bricks from sod cut carefully with the spade,
worked well. Soon they had the walls built. The roof was made by laying long
logs across the wails and covering them with sod or whatever was available to
make it as waterproof as they could. These sod houses served for a short time.
Then the pioneer usually built a second house of adobe bricks, -- these were a
clay brick mixed and poured into a form like we do concrete today. For this
type of house lumber was used for the frame roof, the doors, window frames and
the floor. The walls were plastered with a mixture of clay, straw and manure.
Over this plaster then came the wall paper which made the interior very
cheerful.

These adobe houses stood for many years and were very warm in winter and cool
in the summer. The floors were earth if the family couldn't afford more lumber.
To clean and scrub the floor was a particular chore. The broom was made of
rushes from the nearby creek, tied to a good sized sturdy handle. Water and
soil were used to level the floor and when thus cleaned it was sprinkled with
clean sifted sand and then the floor was clean again !

The shelters for the animals were usually very crude. A dugout in the hillside
with small trees and branches for the front wall, then banked with hay, straw
and earth. Straw sheds were made by putting up a frame of crude lumber, then
covering this with a mesh of wire or light lumber. Over the mesh they piled the
straw. The straw barns will serve for a time but mice, snakes and rats also
liked to live in them. And if it rained a lot outside, it finally rained
inside too. So the pioneer soon built a small wooden barn or a barn of adobe
bricks whichever he could do easiest.

        A typical South Dakota claim shanty

For water supply the farmer usually placed the buildings on a high hill along a
creek or river. Small dams were built by loosening the earth with a plow and
then shoveling it into a bank or a small scraper, pulled by a team, piled up
the dirt. Wells were dug by hand or by old fashioned well digging rigs powered
by horses going around in a circle. Eave troughs and rain barrels were used and
cisterns were dug with cement walls or brick masonry depending on the material
available.

Food was an even greater problem. They started from scratch. The little pig had
to be fattened, slaughtered, carefully scalded, then scraped and dressed. The
hams and shoulders were salted down and later smoked. The trimmings were ground
for sausages and the head was cleaned and made into head cheese, using the
stomach for a bag to put the meat into as cloth could only be used for clothes
and where it was most needed. The lard was rendered and stored in crocks in
storm cellars or cellars under the house. The cracklings were salted down and
also stored away in crocks and later warmed for breakfast or a quick lunch.

Other animals, such as cattle, sheep, rabbits and goats were raised for meat.
Fowl of all kinds were kept on the farm. It was not uncommon to find chickens,
guineas, geese, ducks, turkeys and pigeons.

Vegetables had to be hand raised and carefully stored in the cellar or salted
and cured. Sauerkraut was packed in barrels or crocks; also dill pickles and
watermelons.

Grains were ground and used as breakfast food. Whole wheat kernels were cooked
as we do rice today. Very delicious dishes were prepared but much of that
talent has been lost. We of today, would have to be taught the "know how."

For coffee, barley was roasted and used and cornmeal mush and cornbread fed
many a pioneer.

Fruits were gathered at the river or nearby creeks for jams and jellies.

The heating unit in many pioneer homes was the old fashioned "pot bellied"
heater, straw burner or the crudely built-in fireplace and oven made of mud
bricks. To bake, the fire was pub into the oven and heated to the right
temperature, then scraped out with a long handled hoe. To tell if the oven was
too hot a wisp of hay dipped in water was used as a thermostat. After it was
cleaned out the loaves of all one uniform size were placed into this mammoth
oven on a long handled scoop, often 12 in number. Then the soft stone door was
shoved into place. To seal the door wet cloths were packed over the cracks.
Land sakes ! What a baking day! We buy our loaves sliced at the bakery today.

And butter, "dear me." We all need butter on that delicious fresh bread. Well,
we go to the corner grocery and buy a pound of butter but the pioneer took a
club in his hand and walked the pasture. After "bossie" was located she had to
be brought home to get brushed up for the milking. Little brother chased away
the flies. Dad milked her.  Mother, at the house, strained the milk and put it
in a cool place. Later she dipped off the cream and saved until one day she had
enough to make butter. Out came the old churn, a crock with a long stick coming
through the hole in the cover. At the base of the stick or handle were two
short boards criss-crossed to fit the bottom of the crock. Sister had to pull
this handle up and down and up and down until her arms were so very tired. She
peeked in often but it was still cream. "Now its getting gritty looking,
Mother." "Well churn some more and soon you'll have butter." Yes, there was
butter and buttermilk. Now Mother took over -- she lifted out the butter and put
it into a large wooden bowl with nice clean fresh water in it and washed it.
Then she poured off the water and got salt from the pantry. She carefully
sprinkled it all over the butter. Then she worked it. Now the butter was ready
to "pat out." Mother bounced it in the palm of her hand to get all the water
out. Then she put it into a mold, smoothed off the top, turned it upside down
and pushed it out. Look, it had a pretty design on it and we had fresh butter
pretty enough to set before a king. Mother made butter balls, too, and put one
into a butter dish; with the end of a warm spoon or knife she put designs on
it. Then she covered it and carefully put the butter away in a cool place. The
balls of butter were sold and we ate fresh butter out of the pretty dish until
the next week when we did all that same work over again!

In our day we sell the milk and cream, but in pioneer days travel was slow so
butter was sold as was eggs, hides and livestock. Refrigeration was not yet in
existence as we have it today. In exchange for the farm products, the pioneer
took home staple groceries and maybe a bit of calico for the little wife or
sent money to the far off city for a new pair of shoes.

Quilts were made from odd pieces of materials carefully put together to form
pretty designs and patterns. To make them warm, wool was washed and carded and
placed between the materials and then tied into place with bits of colored wool
yarn.

Socks were hand knitted and mittens, scarfs and sweaters, too. Woolen cloth was
woven from woolen yarn spun on a spinning wheel. The red and green combination
was often used. A couch throw made this way lasted a life time.

Art was also in the pioneer home. They had to do much or their own designing,
dyeing and coloring. The little girls were taught how to sew the various
stitches in sewing and embroidering, also the art of lace making was taught.

For the pioneer family there was never an idle moment.. Everyone worked and
everyone cooperated. Only on Sundays were they allowed to have leisure time
after the necessary chores were done.

Worship was in the home in daily devotions and reading and studying the Bible.
In the more thickly settled areas they would get together at a neighbor's home
and have some missionary minister come to that house. Later when schools were
built the school house was used during the week for school and on Sunday it
became the place of worship. Thus the school house became the community center.

For recreation they had husking bees or butchering bees, also quilting parties
and there was dancing and ball playing -- also music, -- such instruments as the
violin and accordion were very popular. The pioneer had his troubles, too.
Often there were accidents and sickness and home remedies such as putting a raw
potato on a burn to soothe the pain or unsalted butter was used to draw out
blood poisoning or axel grease was used for infected sores. Peppermint was used
to tame upset stomachs and camomile tea was served to the sick and
convalescent. Goose grease and even lard were used in place of vaseline and
salves. Other remedies were turpentine, skunk oil, carbolic acid and liniment.
For chest colds the mustard plaster was made and there was the onion plaster
poultice and cornmeal poultice. The heat pad was of heated salt or barley and
to warm the feet and icy bed, flatirons or bricks were used. The pioneers had
to use every means possible to protect and forward life because transportation
in that day couldn't begin to compare with that of the speed of today. By so
doing new things were discovered, new inventions came into being, that today
they have for a reward great progressiveness. They saw their children prosper
and their children's children may long give thanks that their forefathers were
safely led to this land of abundance and freedom.

                Did You Know

That certain young wife, who tiring of late poker sessions in a certain
establishment, tossed a rock through the window and in the sprint that
followed, reached home before her husband did!

That a certain pioneer lady, who while employed as hotel cook finally tired of
a certain gentleman's attentions, so she picked him up and dumped him
head first into a huge flour barrel?

That a certain gambling man ate the cards he didn't like in his hand or the
gambler, who found he had too many cards ate the surplus in a sandwich?

That young matron whose cattle had strayed and been shut in another's corral
took her gun and walked over and let them out. No more trouble!

That young girl (being not too long in this country) who on being sent to the
store for groceries, picked them up and started for the door. The clerk
noticing this, called out "Shall I charge them?" "No," was the quick reply
"just write it on a piece of paper."

That other young girl being sent to the store for "sterilized gauze" puzzled
the clerk when she requested "antiseptic kerosene."

                Akaska Churches

In these groups of early settlers for this area, their worship services were
not overlooked, aside from daily devotions in many, homes. They were eager and
pleased to welcome mission pastors who would hold services in private homes
whenever they were traveling through their districts.

As early as 1905 a group of Lutherans (Iowa Synod) had been meeting in various
homes but now they bought an old school building, remodeled, and moved it to
Akaska. It was used by the congregation for many years until in the early 30's
it was struck by lightning. Then the members built on this site a basement
edifice but, owing to so many members moving away the congregation disbanded
and the property was sold.

                       Family Biographies

OLE ANDERSON

Ole Anderson, accompanied by Matt Halvorson and a friend. came from Telemarken,
Norway in 1901. Ole had been employed in a paper factory there. The two men
went to Milwaukee, Wis., and the following year, Matt and Ole came to Potter
county, S. D., to visit Ole's sister before going to the state of Washington.
They started working on Dakota farms and liked what they were doing so well
that they stayed and filled on homesteads in Fayette township.

Ole lived on his homestead until his death on Dec. 9, I945. His son, Arthur now
lives on the home place. Mrs. Anderson nee Ragna Hanson, came to the United
States from Hadland, Norway with her parents at the age of 10 years. Her family
first settled near Sioux Falls. Several years later her father homesteaded in
Potter county so the family moved there in 1893. She also proved up a homestead
near her fathers Arthur farms her homestead now. she passed away on Sept. 27,
1949. Their children are: Ruby, Butte, N. D.; Arthur, Akaska; and Otto,
Gettysburg.

Because the Anderson homestead was not many miles from the Missouri river they
had many visitors. Indians would camp overnight near his home and occasionally
for a day or two. They would ferry across the river to Le Beau, then drive
across country to the southern part of the state. In the winter the Indians
would come across on the ice and trade with Mr. Anderson and others.

Mr. Anderson and Matt Halvorson had homesteaded on nearby quarters, so close
in fact, that at one time they went 50-50 on the expense and labor of building
a barn for their livestock. Somehow a minor disagreement sprang up concerning
something, and apparently Matt was a man of action rather than words, for when
Ole went out to their barn one day he found that it was only half there, -- Matt
had sawed it in two and taken away his half of the barn!

THE BALLENSKY FAMILY

Geo. Sr., Paul, Geo. (Big) Geo. (Little)

From the peninsula of Crimea. jutting out into the Black Sea, a part of the
area known as the Ukraine in South Russia, in 1879 or '73 came two brothers,
Paul and George Ballensky Sr. In the Paul Ballensky family were Jacob, George
and Christine (Hermann of Tripp, S. D.), children by the first marriage, -- and
Katherine, (Kuertz, Elgin, N. D.) by the second marriage, all born in Russia
except John Paul, who was born in the U. S. In the Geo. Ballensky family were
George, Katherine (Wiedenbach, Tripp), and later in this country was born John.
These two brothers had farmed together on a large tract of land in Crimea. The
one widow said it was 900 acres or more. When it was time to sell their grain
they would haul it all at once in a long wagon train for the journey to
Odessa was of several days length. They would remain overnight and do their
trading and shopping before returning home.

        Mr. and Mrs. George Ballensky and Family

The father and grandfather of these brothers had come from Germany to Russia
but in the 1860's and 1870's the restrictions on these people's privileges were
being tightened and hearing of the wonderful opportunities here in America,
they left this territory which had been their homeland for one generation and
embarked for the New World.

They settled at Mt. Pleasant, Ia., where they had relatives, remaining there
about a year. Paul located on a homestead about 1/2 mile west of Lesterville,
S. D., where they resided until they moved to Yankton. After the death of his
wife he lived near Akaska for a time with his son, George, who was unmarried,
until the time of his own death at the age of 90. This son, George had
homesteaded about 2 1/2 miles northeast of Akaska but in his last year he left
this homestead and made his home with his brother, Jacob at Yankton until he
passed away April 7, 1925.

The brother of Paul, George Sr., also lived around Lesterville for a time and
he, too, had a quarter section of land in Hutchinson county about 10 miles
southeast of Menno. When his son, George (Big) was grown, homestead land was
almost unavailable around there so he worked at odd jobs. In 1885 he and
Margaretha Ochszner were united in marriage at Scotland, S. D. During the first
years of marriage they made their home in Yankton and later at Armour. From
there they came to Grandfather Ochszner's home near Java, making the trip in a
covered wagon, bringing bedding, dishes, clothing and some food. They also
brought a cow, horses and a colt. The journey lasted for a week and was very
tiring and they looked forward to the evening when they could stop at a farm
house for a supply of fresh milk.

Their homestead site was six miles south of the county seat town of Bangor.
Their first home was of mud brick with one side for living quarters and the
other side for livestock. The first barn was built into a hill with a straw
roof which gave them many a scare at milking time when the other cattle being
driven home would get excited and running over the straw roof, would fall down
over the side.

The mother of Mr. Ballensky (Katherina) came here from Lesterville after her
husband's death. She, too, homesteaded south of her son's farm and lived later
in town in a small house which is a summer kitchen on the Henry Lutz farm. she
passed away before the family moves to Montana.

Johnny Ballensky, (a brother) also made his home with them for a time until his
marriage to Bertha Remmich. Mrs. Remmich (Bertha's mother) lived in a small
house just north of Carl Kuehl's present home. This house is now the Equity
office. Johnny and family, Virdella and Harold moved to Vancouver, Wash., where
both he and his wife passed away some years ago.

The family remained on their farm until 1913 when they sold out and moved to a
ranch 45 miles north of Miles City, Mont. After the accidental death of her
husband in 1916 Mrs. Ballensky and her three youngest children returned to
Akaska where she lived until her death on June 8, 1954 at the age of 88 years.

The children comprising this family were: Albert, Hardin, Mont.; Lydia, Mrs.
Geo. Schanzenbach, Selby; Emil, Akaska; Rhoda, Mrs. Ben Zimmerman, Cedar
Rapids, Iowa; Julius, deceased; William, Akaska, and Tillie, Mrs. Adolph
Heier, Gayville, S. D.

Not long after coming to Hoven they settled on a farm known as the Ballensky
farm. While on this place they did much sod breaking and work for the farmers
in the surrounding neighborhood.

Later they moved into the town of Akaska. Mrs. Berg was in poor health but
never complained. The community was shocked to hear of her death on April 14,
1925.

THE BERRETH FAMILY

The Frederick Berreth Sr., family came to the United States in November 1883.
Included in the family were two half-sisters, Margaretha and Sophia, and five
full brothers and sisters: Fred, John and George, all born in or near Worms,
South Russia, and Emma and Anna. The family located at Menno, S. D., where he
started a butcher shop which he operated for several years before moving to a
farm 10 miles southeast of Menno in Hutchinson county. In 1890 he purchased a
farm in that same neighborhood where his second wife passed away.

During the fall of 1897, Mr. Berreth and his third wife, Dorothea Sara, came to
Walworth county. His sons, George, Fred and John traveled by covered wagon from
Menno. In the summer of 1898 the elder Berreth filled on his homestead 2 1/2
miles northeast of Akaska where he lived and farmed until 1907. He sold out and
moved to Canada where he filed on some land, living there for about a year and
a half but returned to this state with his sons, George and John. He died here
in 1909.

Frederick Berreth Sr., was married three times and was the father of 21
children, 8 by the first marriage, 10 by the second and 3 by the third; of
which 6 died in Russia and 9 in America. Remaining are: George of Aberdeen;
Mrs. Emma Horton of Rapid City; Mrs. Anna Kurle of Tacoma, Wash.; Theodore and
Rueben of Canada; Mrs. Sara Marshall of Hope, B. C., Canada, and 3
stepchildren, Lydia, Mrs. Phil Oxner of Lowry; Annie of Linton, N. D., and one
stepson.

Fred Berreth Jr., filed on some land east of Akaska but did not stay to make
final proof.

John Berreth filed; on land 3 miles northeast of Akaska on which he made final
proof. In 1907 the town of Akaska was started and he moved there and worked in
the elevator for several years and then went into business for himself in a
saloon and later in a garage and implement shop. He served in various
capacities on the school board, town board, as Justice of the Peace and for
some years as county commissioner. In 1940 he and his family moved to Maywood,
Calif. There he worked in a shell factory for several years. He passed away in
1951 and his wife, the former Pauline Ochszner, passed away in 1954. To this
union were born 3 children: one daughter, who died in infancy; Ella, now Mrs.
Frank Gray of Maywood, and Vesta, now Mrs. Wendell Simmons of E1 Segundo, Calif.

George Berreth spent most of his single life working out. His first employer in
Walworth county was a man by the name of Cal Smithers located southeast of
Bangor, at the magnificent sum of $5.00 a month and board. His job was caring
for 18 cows and their calves. In 1900 his sister, Emma and he came back to the
southern part of this state, returning in 1902 to Walworth county where, in
1904 he filed on a quarter section of land 3 1/2 miles northeast of Akaska.
From Bert Thornton he purchased an old claim shanty and moved it to his claim
on which he did not make final proof but commuted paying $1.25 per acre -- 3
years proof. In 1907 he married and this year he and his wife will observe their
Golden Anniversary on Nov. 19. They farmed for one year on brother John's place
and the next fall moved to the wife's fathers farm for two years. In 1910 the
couple moved to Java where he returned to his trade as a carpenter until 1917
when he managed the Java light plant for 6 months. Then he was employed by John
H. Wolf in the garage and machinery business until Mr. Wolf died in 1918 of
influenza, an epidemic that was sweeping the country. For 10 years he worked as
an automobile mechanic until 1930 when he again took up carpentering, moving to
Aberdeen in 1938 and continuing at his trade for 17 years in the employ of Fred
A. Peterson, now a retired contractor. Roy, their son, also resides in Aberdeen.

THE BOEHMER FAMILY

Frank J. Boehmer was born in Pakwaukee, Wis., in 1863. He came to the state and
homesteaded on a quarter west of Gettysburg about 1883. He freighted
merchandise from Blunt to Old Forest City for his sister and brother-in-law,
who had a store there. He proved up on his homestead and moved to Scranton
where he went into the store business with J. F. Whitlock, trading mostly with
the Indians, whose language he could speak fluently. His widowed mother kept
house for him until his marriage to Cora Belle Dean in 1897. The young couple
ranched for many years on land and with stock he had acquired by trading with
the Indians, later building the ranch up to 12,000 acres and running 1000 to
1500 cattle on it.

To this union 5 children were born: Dean, George, Claude, Nellie and Elizabeth.
Claude passed away in infancy. George was taken by the flu in the winter of
1920. Nellie, now Mrs. Roy L. Houck, lives near Gettysburg, Elizabeth, now Mrs.
F. J. Wolvington at Belle Fourche, and Dean near Akaska.

Mr. and Mrs. Boehmer's first ranch home was the old hotel building at the town
of Scranton. Their barns were the old log stage barns of Scranton. In 1907 the
new town of Le Beau was started, joining Scranton, the railroad pulling in
there in the spring of 1908. At this time the homesteaders came in and made it
necessary for him to cut down his herd onto more or less his own holdings and
lease lands close to his ranch.

During the life at Le Beau, he was president of the First State Bank and was
also interested with John C. Byrne in the livery and drayline business.

George and Dean attended grade school in Le Beau and the family moved to
Fulton, S. D., in 1913 so they could attend high school, the men returning to
the ranch for the summer work. It was in August 1915 that Mr. Boehmer was taken
seriously ill and passed away. The boys continued to run the ranch with hired
help. Mrs. Boehmer stayed in Fulton most of the time so the girls, Nellie and
Elizabeth could attend school, afterwards moving to the ranch. When the oldest
daughter was married and moved to Gettysburg, Mrs. Boehmer and Elizabeth rented
an apartment there and Elizabeth finished high school and later married. Mrs.
Boehmer passed away in 1939 at the ranch home of hear son, Dean.

Dean has stayed on the ranch and with hired help has built the original ranch
into the largest ranch in the county, buying land that his father had leased.
The ranch buildings have been moved closer to the river because of better stock
shelter and water. The house was moved from Evarts to Le Beau and then moved to
the ranch and modernized. The old ranch house was torn down. The barn on the
ranch is the old livery barn from Le Beau which was also remodeled.

Dean is active in public affairs, being range man for the A.A.A. for nearly 20
years, president of the Board of the National Farm Loan association in Mobridge
for several years and one of the promoters and director and president of Cam
Wal Rural Electric cooperative since 1947.

The Boehmer ranch comprises around 9300 acres of deeded land and about 1700
acres of rented land with two sets of buildings facing about 10 miles of
Missouri river front and running about 500 cows with their calves.

It is interesting to note that after the flooding of the Oahe river dam at
Pierre, Dean's ranch will be liquidated to approximately 3000 acres, split into
four parcels.

The home ranch is now operated by Dean's nephew, Tom Houck, and the north ranch
is operated by Earl Payne and son, Dick.

THE COOK FAMILY

Mrs. Maud Hinckley, the former Maud Cook and her sister, Ardelle Cook
homesteaded 2 1/2 miles southeast of Akaska in August 1906. The Cook families
former home was in Illinois. Their ancestors came originally from England and
Scotland to New Jersey and then to Illinois. The Cook girls' homesteads were
sold to a family by the name of Kranzler and the Cooks moved to Yakima, Wash.,
in 1912.

While on the claims the girls both taught schools in the neighborhood, Ardelle
in Akaska and Maud taught what was known as the Liberty school and later taught
in Selby. These two girl spent many happy days on the claims enjoying the
freedom of the prairies and shared the hardships, too, of homesteaders life
with their parents. They had many pleasant friendships in Akaska, which was a
thriving little town with several very substantial businesses. The first real
big event was the coming through of the telephone and the crew boarded with the
Cooks. Next came the railroad in 1907 which passed within 60 rods of the Cook
home. Those things along with the prairie dogs and rattlesnakes and all the
other things that go with ranch life for two city girls, including getting
dumped off the pony into the pond, and their many experiences in church, school
and social life of the early history of this thriving little pioneer community
have left many happy memories in the mind of the sole survivor of the Cook
family, Mrs. Chas. Hinckley, who came to live on a farm in Potter county in
1915.

Mrs. Hinckley tells the following interesting story of her early life:

It is interesting to look back at those early doings in the Akaska area. They
were the beginning of living in that section of the country. The land was an
unbroken prairie, -- we plowed and cultivated about half of the claim area and
fenced the rest for pasture. The prairie dogs had established their type of
civilization before we came and so the battle raged between us and we finally
conquered.

The school in Liberty township which I taught was also a beginning, -- new
building, new furniture which did not arrive on time, -- consequently we were
about a month late commencing school. I wish to mention the pupils who were in
attendance as some of them are still in the vicinity. They were of the Ritter
family: Fred, Mary, Jacob and Regina. The Flemmer family: Fritz, Jack,
Christian, Daniel and Christina. The Goetz family: Rosa, Katie and Chris. The
Goetz Sr., family: Christian and Simon. The Taylor family: Lloyd and Pearl.
Florence Petherick and a girl whose last name was Larson (only there for a
short time), and Harold Gibbs. A couple of years later three more pupils came.
They were Anna Sternhagen, George and Joe.

We were a very happy group and grew to love each other as time proved, since
later there were four marriages among these pupils.

Fred Ritter married Christina Flemmer; Fritz Flemmer married Mary Ritter; Jack
Flemmer married Katie Goetz, and Lloyd Taylor married Florence Petherick. A
unique record for one small school. During that time I met my Prince Charming,
Chas. Hinckley and 6 years later we were married in Yakima, Wash., and I came
back to live in Potter county on a farm. We now live in Gettysburg.

One experience I remember which shows our ignorance of the country and its
surroundings. Only a few days after I came to Ardelle's claim in 1907, she and
I went out to the stable ;to harness the team. I knew how to put on the double
harness, but she knew only about a single harness (by the way that proved true
of our lives) so we were having some fun. Suddenly we heard a loud shouting and
screeching. We looked through a crack in the door and saw horseback riders
coming at a frantic pace down a hill waving their arms and yelling. We were
petrified with fear as we thought they were Indians and when we looked again
through the knot hole, we saw they were coming toward the claim shack. As our
mother was there alone, we decided we'd better brave up and go in. When we got
there they had already dismounted and gone inside. We found our mother
laughing and talking with them and giving them a lunch of milk and cookies. She
laughed at our foolish fears and she had not been frightened at all. They were
city boys visiting at the Haynes and Tofflemire ranch north of Akaska. The
Indians never visited us.

THE CRONIN FAMILY

Tom and Mary Cronin were born in a small town in Iowa in approximately 1866.
They came to South Dakota in 1875.

In 1890 Tom filed on a homestead in Sully county south of Gettysburg. Later on
his sister, Mary filed on a homestead in the northwestern part of Potter
county. After Mary had proven up on her homestead she moved to Gettysburg where
she worked as chief cook at the Bates Hotel. While she was working there her
brother Tom broke up a few acres of land and erected a barn and several other
small buildings, hauling all the lumber and supplies from Le Beau.

After Mary returned to her homestead they started raising cattle and horses and
a few pigs. They always kept their sows until they were 5 or 6 years old. They
were also great lovers of horses and purchased several high priced stallions.

One time Mary was caught in a prairie fire between Le Beats and her home. The
fire burned one wheel off the buggy. One of the horses was burned so badly that
he had to be shot. Mary only got her hair singed.

One time Tom bought himself a car so one Saturday they started for Akaska.
After setting the eggs in the car, Mary got in beside Tom, who started driving
up a big hill which lay just north of their house. When he was about half way
up the hill the engine died and the car started rolling right back into the
house. This ordeal caused Mary to become very angry at Tom as it broke her
whole case of eggs. That was the last time Tom ever drove his car.

Today Tom and Mary's land lies in the Bill Lender and Anderson pastures. Mary
died when she was 83 or 84 years old. Tom died of tuberculosis in the Black
Hills. He was around 77 years old.

One of Tom's great sayings was: "What one man makes, another man tears down."

Tom and Mary were rough and tough people. They lived in a 3-room house with
maybe chickens or pigs walking in occasionally. Their food consisted of tea,
biscuits and soup but anyone who stopped by was a welcome guest.

Today Tom and Mary are both gone but many people remember the 6 foot, 2 inch
frame of Mary walking along with her hands behind her back with perhaps 4 or 5
dogs following behind. Or, maybe they remember Tom with his pipe filled with
George Washington tobacco, his hair and mustache of steel gray, and always
walking at a slow pace.

Tom and Mary were liked by everyone who knew them. They both read the papers
and kept up with the news even in their last days. They loved life on the
prairie and lived in its true tradition.

EMANUEL EITENEIER

Emanuel Eiteneier was born January 25, 1877 at New Nassau, a small village in
South Russia. There he grew to young manhood, working for his father on the
farm. At the age of 21 he was drafted into the Russian Army and served for 4
years. After receiving his discharge he was employed by the water department
at Odessa. While there he met and married Maria Wacker on January 25, 1904. At
this time it appeared that Russia and Japan might be involved in a war so, to
avoid being drafted again, they decided to come to the United States where they
had relatives and friends near Selby, S. D. They arrived in Selby on Nov. 4,
1904. Early in 1905 Mr. Eiteneier was employed on the John Himrich Sr., farm
where he worked for one year.

In the spring of 1906 he rented a farm north of Selby and farmed it for one
year. In the fall of the same year, Mr. Eiteneier filed on a quarter section of
land 6 miles southwest of Akaska and built a small house. In the spring of 1907
the Eiteneier family moved to their new home. Here they lived until the fall of
1910 when they again moved to Selby, renting the Adam Himrich farm. There they
stayed for one year and then moved back to Akaska onto their own farm on which
they lived until the spring of 1913.

The family now consisted of 6 children and needing more room they rented a farm
from John Schell. This was Emanuel's last move. A few years later he bought
this farm from Mr. Schell and started to improve it.

During the first years here in Akaska, many hardships were experienced. Money
was scarce and crops were poor. For heating and cooking, wood had to be hauled
from the river or the family went out into the pasture and gathered "cow chips"
for fuel. Mrs. Eiteneier, born and raised in the city of Odessa, did
everything in her power to keep the family healthy and happy. Besides raising
grain they also milked numerous cows but instead of selling the cream, Mrs.
Eiteneier churned butter and sold it in the towns of Akaska and Le Beau.

The Eiteneiers had seven children, all of whom were born in America and all are
still living. There are three boys and four girls. They are: Edward, Hilda,
Olga, Arthur, Alvina, Alma and Henry. They are all living in the Akaska area
except Alvina, who is living on a ranch near Eagle

Everything prospered for the family until the depression and drouth in the
30's. There being no feed, he had to sell all his cattle for very little and
finally lost his land and home. In the 30's Mrs. Eiteneier's health failed and
she passed away June 30, 1939.

Henry Eiteneier rented the home farm for several years and in 1943 bought the
farm back again from the Federal Land Bank. Emanuel made his home with Henry,
Hilda keeping house for them until his death on Sept. 10, 1952.

Four of their children are married and all are engaged in farming and cattle
raising. Those married are Edward; Olga, now Mrs. Max Weiss; Alma, now Mrs.
William Weiss, all of Akaska; and Alvina, now Mrs. Russell Keckler of Eagle
Butte. Hilda, Henry and Arthur are not married. Henry owns and farms the
Eiteneier home place and he also owns the homestead quarter. Arthur's health is
poor and he is staying at Henry's place.

FLEMMER FAMILY

Mr. and Mrs. Christian Flemmer Sr., came from Russia in 1906 and came directly
to Akaska although the town was not yet started. They lived 2 miles north of
town for only a year and then homesteaded 3 1/2 miles south of Akaska. Here they
farmed for 18 years and retired and moved into town. Emil and Jacob took over
the home place, Jacob later moving away and Emil is still operating it. Mrs.
Flemmer died in 1930 and Mr. Flemmer in 1949. The Christ Flemmers had a family
of 9, of whom 6 are still living. Daniel died in 1909, Jacob in 1953 and Christ
in 1955. Those remaining are: Fred Flemmer, Mrs. Fred Ritter (Christine), and
Emil Flemmer of Akaska; John C. Flemmer, Selby; Geo. Flemmer, Sacramento,
Calif.; Mrs. Relva Rogers (Maggie), Sturgis. Emil and Christine have lived for
50 years in the Akaska area.

The first home erected on their farm was a sod house and it served them well
for many years for it was cool in summer and warm in the winter. After Mr.
Flemmer built the frame house, the 3 younger boys preferred to have their
sleeping quarters in the old sod house, where accordion playing and a bit of
roughhousing did not disturb the rest of the family.

They can remember when one year, the horses were used to thresh out the wheat
crop by driving them round and round on the piled up grain and also the 12
horsepower thresher owned and operated by Martin Kubier. This machine was
placed between 2 stacks and these 12 horses were driven around threshing out
the grain which was fed into the hopper by everyone available, -- men, women,
boys and girls.

GOTTFRIED HEIER SR.

Gottfried Heier Sr., was born February 23, 1847 in Kloestitz, Bessarabia, South
Russia, and his wife, Louisa Diederle was born the same day in the same town.
They were born, baptized, confirmed and married on the same day. They were
married June 22, 1870. They had ten children, two dying in infancy. They were
tired of Russian militarism and left for the U. S. A. in 1897. By then the
oldest son, Gottfried already had served five years in the Russian Army where
living conditions were bad, -- poor food, and so on. The second son, Samuel
served two years in the army and he and his elder brother, Gottfried were
determined to come to the United States. The parents not wishing them to go
alone, decided the whole family would come and the family ail moved to the free
country. They came to Bowdle Dec. 27, 1897. Mr. Heier had a sister living two
miles north of Selby, Mrs. John Wagner.

GOTTLIEB HUBER

Gottlieb Huber, son of Mr. and Mrs. John Huber, was born July 16, 1880 in the

being baptized and confirmed there. At the age of 19 he came to America with

friends and relatives who were taking up their homesteads in that same area.

On Nov. 24, 1904 he was united in marriage with Magdalena Schock and 4 children
were born to them. In the fall of 1954 the children arranged the celebration of
their 50th wedding anniversary. The first home of the Huber's was in Walworth
county where they farmed for 32 years. It was during the first winter of their
married life that Mrs. Huber became so homesick for her parental home that Mr.
Huber took her back there and she remained for a three weeks visit. While he
was batching during her absence, tiring of what he had been cooking, he decided
to prepare some dried prunes for a change in diet. Placing the fruit in an
ample amount of water on the stove, he went out to feed the livestock and
attend to other chores. Upon his return to the house, tired and hungry, imagine
his surprise to find the house dark as night! Prunes and kettle were so charred
they had to be tossed out. Mrs. Huber was never again homesick!

Shortly after the town of Akaska was organized, Gottlieb Huber and his brother,
Jacob bought and managed a grain elevator there for 3 years. After that, Mr.
Huber returned to his farm until they moved to Mobridge where they lived for 16
years before he became ill of an incurable bone disease. The last months of his
life he suffered greatly before he was summoned to the better abode on July 19,
1955 at the age of 75 years and 3 days. He is lovingly remembered as a pioneer
homesteader and also for his noble work in the development of the Pilgrim
Congregational church of Lowry and Gnadenfeld of Mobridge. He was survived by
his widow, one son and 3 daughters, 9 grandchildren and 3 brothers.

JACOB OCHSZNER SR.

The Ochszner family in common with many of the inhabitants of South Russia, who
were of German descent, failed to approve of the drastic military training
required by the Russian government at that time. Their ancestors had pioneered
in this area and the period of freedom from military service which had been
granted, had now expired. In this family were 6 sons who would all eventually
be required to take this training.

So, in 1884, the parents, Jacob and Katherina nee Berreth, and their family of
2 daughters and 6 sons, left their home in Worms, South Russia and set sail for
the U. S., arriving at New York in December 1884. From there they went to a
farm 10 miles from Menno which belonged to Andrew Berreth, a brother of Mrs.
Ochszner, who was now studying for the ministry and on this farm they remained
for several years. Because of the scarcity in that area of land which could be
acquired by homestead rights, and hearing what there was offered in this
northern area of the state, they journeyed to a preemp northwest of Java where
they lived in a sod house. In 1889 they came to Walworth county and homesteaded
on what is now a part of the Ed Oxner land. That fall they built a mud brick
house which stood there for many years. Their 2 eldest daughters were now
married and their eldest sons worked out in the neighborhood when help was
requested. Six more children were added to this family making a total of 16,
two of whom died overseas.

After their children were all grown and most of them in homes of their own, the
parents left their homestead and moved to Akaska where on April 30 and May 9,
1924 they passed away -- 9 days apart.

They had lived to see their children grow to maturity and to see some of their
grandchildren. While the early years were difficult as it surely must be when
parents rear a family of this size, with little money and small comforts
available, we can not help but realize that had they not been strengthened by
their faith and implicit trust in their Lord, their burdens would have been
unbearable.

The heritage which they left their children was far better than gold, -- sound
bodies and minds, ambition, humor, adaptability, orderliness and their faith.

Family members were: Margaret (Mrs. Geo. Ballensky); John, Jacob, Christian,
Rose, Adam, Philip, Henry; Pauline (Mrs. John Berreth); Edward; Lydia (Mrs.
Christ Schanzenbach); Peter, Carl and Emil.

The Ritter family relates the sad accident which took place a few yards away
from their home the first spring they had moved on their land. Some of them
were sitting on their porch when they saw a top-buggy drawn by two horses turn
from the trail and head directly for the creek which flows through their yard.
In the buggy were the Jim Thompson family, husband, wife and baby boy less than
a year old. Mr. Ritter started shouting and motioned the driver to turn away
from the creek while some one else in the family ran for ropes. The horses
paused on the creek bank but the driver slapped the reins and used the whip to
urge them into the water. When they did plunge in they sank into a 15 ft. hole.
They were all under water except the wife's one arm and hand around which
someone threw a rope and she, grasping it, was pulled to shore almost dead but
in her other arm she still held the baby, whom they despaired of reviving until
Rosa, the oldest girl, who was a deaf mute took the youngster by the legs and
held him upside down, shaking him vigorously and the water came out of his
lungs and he began to breathe again. Mr. Thompson and the horses were drowned.
The wife never knew just why he entered the creek but she thought it was in
order to wash the buggy. Years later this same boy wrote Miss Rosa and thanked
her for saving his life and that of his mother.

DANIEL SCHANZENBACH SR.

Mr. and Mrs. Daniel Schanzenbach Sr., with their six sons and two daughters
left their home in Glueckstal and homesteaded at Eureka in May 1888.

They left Russia to keep their sons out of the Russian Army and also because
they had heard from friends who came here before them who told them they could
take up land here.

At Eureka they built themselves a sod home and followed his old trade, --
farming. Here 5 more boys and a girl were born, three of whom died in infancy.

Here they suffered many hardships and many a scare by the Indians. Many times
the children at play would run to the house at the sight of an Indian even
though the Indian women seemed just as scared of the white people. Finally they
became friendly and came to borrow flour or sugar from Mrs. Schanzenbach. One
can imagine about how they got along, neither understanding the other's
language.

Mr. Schanzenbach worked for other farmers to help with the living and therefore
would be gone for days as he would be several miles from home. Mrs.
Schanzenbach dug potatoes for other folks in the fall. she left home early in
the morning while still dark and would work until dark for $1 a day, -- a big
wage at that time. The children were left alone all day, the eldest taking care
of the younger. Many times when she came home at night she would find them all
asleep. After milking the cow she would cook supper for them and then wake them
up to eat their one hot meal of the day.

In 1897 they moved to Walworth county and settled just North of what is now the
Akaska cemetery a mile north of Akaska. Here again they built a sod house and a
summer kitchen and went to farming. Later in this summer kitchen, with just a
dirt floor, a Miss Myrtle Potter taught school for 2 years and is where most of
the Schanzenbach children got their schooling. Also in this summer kitchen,
Mrs. Schanzenbach built a huge oven to bake the bread for her large family. It
was built of mud and stone to the outside of the kitchen. The base was about
two feet high. The sides and top of the oven were rounded and about 4 inches
thick. This was all mud and the stove pipe leading to the chimney was mud. This
oven was large enough to bake about 12 huge loaves of bread.

Since the family was large and bread one of the main foods, large batches of
dough were mixed and this was done in a doughbox. It took huge quantities of
flour for each baking.

About 7 years later they bought still more land further north and east which is
now the Dave Lemley place. Around this vicinity several of the oldest sons
filed claims of their own.

About 5 years later they retired and bought a home across the Swan creek south
of Akaska. During this time he started a grocery and dry goods store and two
sons, Fred and Jacob managed it. About 2 years later Mr. and Mrs. Schanzenbach
moved back to Eureka.

In 1922 they sold out and went to Elk Grove, Calif., where Mr. Schanzenbach
passed away in October 1935 and Mrs. Schanzenbach passed away in April 1944.

The family included the following:

Rosina Schanzenbach married Simon Gimbel and to this union 5 children were
born. They farmed near Java for a number of years, retiring and moving to Java
where she passed away in 1937. Her husband preceded her in death.

Daniel D. Schanzenbach married Katherine Flemmer and they had 3 daughters. He
homesteaded 3 miles northeast of Akaska on land John Eisemann owned later. He
farmed several years and then sold out and moved to Elk Grove, Calif., where he
had a vineyard and also raised chickens. Later he retired and moved to Elk
Grove where he passed away in 1947.

John D. Schanzenbach married Amelia Koerner and to this union were born 3
children. He homesteaded northeast of Akaska where Fred Flemmer now lives. He
farmed a few years and then moved to Selby. Later he sold out and move to Elk
Grove and then to Sacramento where he passed away in 1957.

George D. Schanzenbach married Lydia Ballensky and they had 2 daughters. George
had the livery barn in Akaska in 1909. Later he homesteaded 2 miles northeast
of Akaska where the Schilling school house is located. He also lived in
Mobridge for a short time. Later he farmed the land where Bill Vandrey lives.
Then he retired and moved to Akaska. In 1955 they moved their house to Selby
where they now live.

Christina Schanzenbach married Samuel Heier. To this union were born 15
children of which 12 are still living and all in the state. They lived on a
farm 5 1/2 miles northwest of Akaska for many years. They retired and moved to
Akaska where she passed away in 1956. Her husband preceded her in death.

Katherina Schanzenbach passed away in infancy in Russia.

Christian D. Schanzenbach married Lydia Oxner and to this union were born 12
children. He homesteaded west of where Albert Kulm now lives. He farmed many
years and later they moved to Mobridge where he passed away in 1951.

Jacob D. Schanzenbach married Lydia Schanzenbach. This union had 9 children.
Jacob and his brother, Fred managed the first grocery store in Akaska. Later he
bought the small acreage on the north side of Akaska where Christ Heier now
lives. He was also a cream buyer in Akaska. He also lived in Bowdle for a few

Fred D. Schanzenbach married Magdalena Keim. He managed the store with his

Then he moved to Lodi, Calif., and had a store there for several years. They

August D. Schanzenbach married Ella Peterhoff and this union had 3 sons. He
farmed 4 miles south of Selby for many years. Later they moved to Selby and are
still living there and he is still engaged in farming.

Gottlieb D. Schanzenbach died in infancy.

Henry D. Schanzenbach married Amelia Aman. This union has 4 children. After
leaving here he moved to McLaughlin. He was Janitor of the school at McLaughlin

David D. Schanzenbach married Mary Kessler and this union had one son, Leo who
passed away when he was 5 years old. For a number of years he had a gas and,
bulk station in Akaska. Later they moved to Aberdeen and he is now employed in

GEORGE G. SCHILLING SR.

George G. Schilling Sr., and Katherina Kelle of Glueckstal, were united in

After the death of his wife in 1921, Mr. Schilling made his home with his son,
George Jr., in Akaska until he was called to his eternal rest in 1930.

Mrs. Schilling was not only a housewife and mother who successfully reared a

know how many babies she helped into this world, how many broken bones she set,
how many vaccinations she administered, how many individuals were successfully
treated for all types of ailments. When any individual came requesting her help
she went willingly, day or night, to minister to them. Her skill in
bone-setting she also transmitted to two of her sons, Jacob and George.

LEONARD STARKS

Leonard Starks was born in Kane county at Starks Station, Ill., November 12,
1888. His father was a farmer and rancher.

In the year 1910 Leonard decided to come to South Dakota to file a claim on. a
homestead. He boarded a train in March 1910 and arrived at Gettysburg 2 or 3
days later. At that time Gettysburg was the end of the Chicago-Northwestern
railway.

After arriving in Gettysburg, he accepted a job operating a well-digging
machine for Dan Baker. The rig was horse-powered and went in circles.

During 1912 he filed claim on a homestead in the northwestern corner of Potter
county along the Missouri river. The town of Le Beau, being only 5 miles
northwest of his homestead, was made headquarters for the purchase of food and
provisions. It was in Le Beau that Len became acquainted with Frank Boehmer, a
cattle rancher, who offered him the job of sharpening sickles for his
hay-mowing crew. Thus the summers of 1912 and 1913 were spent sitting under a
big cottonwood tree, sharpening sickles for $2 a day.

After three years of eating baking powder biscuits and pancakes, Len decided
that homesteading alone wasn't for him. In 1915 he married Frances Lemler of
Hoven. Five children were born to this union: Clyde, Keith, Arthur, Vivian and
Milton.

Clyde, the oldest boy, lives ill Gettysburg. Vivian married a rancher at
Seneca. The other three boys live in separate homes on the Starks Ranch but all
work together.

Some of Leonard's favorite memories are of hauling wheat to Akaska by team,
taking all day to make one trip. His load would be about 45 bushels. He recalls
coming into Akaska from the south with a heavy load and the wagon ran into one
of the horses, cracking its leg. Upon pulling the load into the elevator
driveway, the horse's leg broke completely in two. Many of the trips were made
with Tom and Mary Cronin walking along beside the bobsled to keep warm.

Mr. Starks has acquired additional land through the years, buying many of the
old homestead places but he still own his own homestead. Some friends that he
purchased land from are: Bill Lemler, Hank Glent, Phil Hall, John Frederickson,
Fred McGerns and May Russell. On this property, overlooking the Missouri river,
some 500 head of Herefords graze. He will lose nearly one-fourth of the land
under the waters of the Oahe Reservoir.

Mr. Starks is still active and his main interest is the improving of his herd
which carry the _ S_ (bar S bar) brand. Although he and his family went
through drouth, depression, hard winters, P.W.A., plus the raising of children
on rattlesnake-infested land, he would do it all over again. No Texan could be
any prouder of his state and home than Len is of South Dakota and his ranch
which is located 17 miles southwest of Akaska.

JACOB SULZLE

Jacob and Maria Sulzle nee Seefried left Katzbach Government, Bessarabia on
October 8, 1903 with their five children, Christina, Katherina, Christian,
George and Gotthold. A younger son, John was born in America. They came to
Eureka on Nov. 7, 1903 and moved on a farm northeast of Java, next to her
brother, Emanuel Seefried. In 1905 they moved to a farm 4 miles north of
Pollock where they homesteaded. They sold this farm in 1916 and moved 10 miles
west of Akaska where they lived until they retired in 1921 and moved into
Akaska and built a home.

Land was scarce in the old country and there was no future for their children.
The Gypsies were numerous, too. As long as they had relatives over here they
thought they would try their luck in this country as they could never make a go
of it in Russia.

They lost 6 children in Russia.

Jacob Sulzle passed away on Dec. 4, 1923 at the age of 66. Maria Sulzle died
Jan. 14, 1947 at the age of 84. Christina, Christ and George died in this
vicinity, Katherina in Canada. John passed away in Blackfoot, Idaho. Gotthold
is the only survivor and is still living on the home place.

Jacob was a shoemaker and a farmer in Russia.

The following is a true Gypsy story from Russia as told by Mrs. Jacob Sulzle,
mother of Gotthold Sulzle:

As a young bride and groom, living in Russia, they had great hopes for the
future but the young bride had always been afraid of Gypsies from her early
childhood.

One day a band of Gypsies came through the village. One of the band came to
their house. Mrs. Sulzle was at home alone and being afraid he would put id
"hex" on them, she gave him what he asked for so he would leave. He asked for a
loaf of bread, a jug of sour milk and a bushel of wheat. The Gypsies had not
gone very far when Mr. Sulzle came home. she told him what had happened and he
didn't believe that Gypsies could put a "hex" on anyone so he went after them
and got the things back. The Gypsy, of course, was very angry and said to Mr.
Sulzle, "You will remember me!"

Soon after this had taken place one, of Mr. Sulzle's beautiful horses became
very ill and died. Poor as they were, they worked hard and saved their money
until they could afford another horse. It wasn't long after they had acquired
another team until one of them died. This unusual occurrence continued for
years. They always lost one of their team.

Some years later another band of Gypsies came along and asked for a pillow, a
kuchen, which Mrs. Sulzle was just baking and a chicken. She hurried to get the
things, gave them to him and told them to leave before they had any more bad
luck. The Gypsy asked her what she meant by "bad luck" and Mrs. Sulzle told
about the trouble they had had with their horses. The man then told her that
would be the last one but after the horse was dead, they must cut the heart out
and stick it full of needles and put it in the sun to dry. They never lost
another horse as long as they were in Russia.

Mrs. Sulzle always concluded this tale with a sly chuckle and the remark: "At
least, that was the way it seemed to me!"

JULIUS WAGNER

Julius Wagner's parents and family came to America in 1894 when Julius was 11
years old. The family being large, encouraged them to expand their operations
where land could be had just for homesteading. The family settled 5 miles
southwest of Akaska and Julius attended a school 1/2 mile east of his father's
farm. When of age, he too, wished to homestead as many were doing. Hannah, his
sister chose her homestead to the west of Mr. and Mrs. Martin Kubier and Julius
a half a mile east. Mrs. Martin Kubier was his oldest sister. In this way
sisters and brother were not so far away from each other which proved to be
quite a comfort in those days.

In 1908 Julius married Lydia Himrich, who had attended the same school as he.
Often they missed school because the weather was too severe or help needed at
home. But school was a great experience for youngsters of that day and was
considered a privilege. Eight grades of schooling was not yet compulsory.

After their marriage in 1908 they built a large farm purchasing an additional
160 acres of grassland and working long and hard.

The love of books and better education made them see the need of a school in
the community so they gave the northwest corner of the homestead for a school
ground. "The Little White School on the Hill" as it was often called, served
well until 1930 when lightning struck the chimney and it burned to the ground.

Mrs. Wagner loved gardening and had a lovely large one. There she gathered
vegetables and together with fresh eggs and butter made in the large old
fashioned butter churn, all went into the buggy and over the trail toward the
Missouri river to the lively little town of Le Beau, but Le Beau didn't
flourish long and the little town of Akaska became the main trading center.

Poor crops caused them to leave the farm for a time and Julius was rural mail
carrier in the Selby territory for 8 years.

The farmers were now prospering and they decided to return to the farm but in
the flu epidemic of 1918 Julius had such a severe attack that it took the
Wagners from the farm to seek medical aid. It took much hospitalization and
tender care to bring him back to good health.

By the fall of 1924 the family had grown. There was Esther, Reinhold, Clara and
Susie, -- and Julius decided to return once again to the farm. There they stayed
until it was clear that Julius could not handle the hard farm work. The flu had
left its mark. He returned to Selby, doing only light work.

The children all received grade and high school educations at Akaska and Selby
schools. Esther, now Mrs. Ben Berg, lives on a farm 8 miles northwest of the
town. Reinhold is teaching at Bremerton, Wash. Clara, now Mrs. Arthur Bauder,
lives on a farm southeast of Selby, and Susie, now Mrs. Arthur Oster, lives at
Kent, Wash. Mrs. Lydia Wagner lives at the Selby home and spends much of her
time caring for the sick. In 1933 her husband, who had been on a business
errand, was killed when struck by a train. His life had been an active,
influential and worthy one. Among men he was quiet and conservative, friendly
and kind and scrupulously honest.

THE WESTBERG FAMILY

Otto Westberg and his wife, Christine, with their two children, Elmer and Mary,
left their home in Sweden in the early 80's and settled near the home of his
people at Beresford, S. D. They were there but a short time when he decided to
return to his trade, -- stone cutting.  After moving to Sioux Falls, their
daughter, Gertrude was born to them. The lure of homestead land was too strong
for them and in 1884 another move was made to Potter county between the Ole
Anderson and Matt Halvorson ranches.

Here the family remained in a sod house Otto had built, and he went forth to
search employment. They endured many hardships, drouth and Indian scares
taking their toll.

Many settlers decided to return to their native haunts, Wilmar and Canby,
Minn. The Westbergs chose Canby but it did not satisfy them so they, once
again, returned to South Dakota, this time to Summit and eventually to the old
home in Potter county.  Otto continued to work and invest all possible money
in cattle, hoping that there would soon be enough income so that he might
remain at home with his family. Fate was not kind to him, however, as he died
of a heel infection, leaving two sons, Bernard, 3 years, and Sten, 7.

Now the real struggle began. Christine took in herd cattle from the farmers to
the east. The children attended a school three miles from home. The school was
later sold and Bernard was sent to the Gilbert Johnson school about 8 miles
distant.

The joy in their new home, which they were eventually able to manage, was
short, as it burned to the ground and sparks from the house ignited the barn,
too. The family now moved to Le Beau and lived there until 1918 when both Sten
and his mother died of influenza.

In June 1918 Bernard entered the service and served in France. After his
discharge in May 1919, he and his wife, the former Pauline Seeling, returned to
Le Beau and later bought the Atherton place. Here their daughter, Carol was
born.

During the depression and drouth years they moved to Minnesota but Bernard's
health, which had been undermined by the war, continued to get worse and they
moved to Oregon. He grew gradually weaker and died in California just before
his 60th birthday. His wife now lives with her daughter and family at San
Martin, Calif.

Of the Westberg family only Gertrude, of Aurora, Ill., remains. Mary passed
away at Valley Springs, S. D., and Elmer died at Faulkton.

Six children were born in this country: Henry, Emil, Tillie, Ewald, Mathew and

in action on Okinawa.

        Mr. and Mrs. Gustav Weiss and family

THE WEISS FAMILY

Gustav Julius Weiss was born July 17, 1868 at Posen, Germany. He immigrated to
America at the age of 23 and made his home south of Aberdeen.

On July 26, 1891 he was united in marriage to Clara Augusta Ristau of Aberdeen.
The young couple made their home south of there until 1892 when they moved to
Indian Head, Sask., Canada In 1904 they returned to Aberdeen and lived there
until 1907, coming to Akaska on the first M. & St. L. passenger train. They
settled southwest of town and lived there until 1924 and then moved into
Akaska. Here they resided until 1939, then moving to Mobridge. While a resident
of the Bridge City, Mr. Weiss passed away on October 25, 1948. Mrs. Weiss
still lives there.

There were five children born in Canada, one at Aberdeen anal one at Akaska:
Ernest of Parmalee, S. D.; Max and William of Akaska; Mrs. Gertrude Ballensky
of Akaska; Mrs. Helen Edmonds of Mobridge; Mrs. Selma H. Kosel of Sheridan, Wyo.

Harry J. Weiss of Mobridge was considered officially dead by the War Department
after his ship "The Liscome Bay," sunk in November 1943.

There are now 16 grandchildren and 17 great grandchildren. William Weiss is now
living on was the homestead and Harold Stadel owns the Akaska home.
