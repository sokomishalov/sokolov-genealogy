                HERREID'S DIAMOND JUBILEE  1901-1976

Herreid's Diamond Jubilee and School Reunion, July 2 and 3, 1976, precede the
200th birthday of our country, July 4. Either event, in itself, is worthy of
some kind of history. This book is an attempt to cover the history of Herreid
and include within it some history of the school.

At the time the book project was started, it was already too late to do a
complete history (including pre-Herreid and individual family histories). This
then, is a brief history and includes what could be compiled and printed within
the limited time available.

The first section, "Early Days at Herreid," was written by E. C. Slocum, an
early Campbell County pioneer and son of publisher R. M. Slocum. "The First
Fifty Years" was written by Frank C. Brummund for Herreid's 50th Anniversary.
The following pages consist of various business and organization histories and
pictures contributed for publication.

                EARLY DAYS AT HERREID By E. C. Slocum

The most of the east side of Herreid is built upon my father's homestead, where
as a barefoot boy I drove my father's oxen, swam in the limpid waters of Spring
Creek, and trapped the festive muskrat.

My father, R. M. Slocum, came from Michigan with his family to Dakota in the
spring of 1883. Settling at first on a pre-emption about twenty miles west of
Aberdeen, in Edmunds County.  The railroad was built from Aberdeen to Ipswich
in the late summer of 1883, and the first train reached Ipswich after
Christmas. The towns of Freeport, Georgetown and Edmunds then folded up and
moved to Ipswich.

We spent two summers on the preemption, where my sister Ina was born, and after
"proving up" on it, father in the fall of 1884, came to Campbell County, which
had just been surveyed and opened to settlement. He selected and filed his
homestead on the northwest quarter of section 8, township 127, range 76, the
present site of Herreid, and took the northeast quarter of section 6 in the
same township as his tree claim. In October, 1884, we arrived and settled on
the homestead. We found as neighbors, who had just arrived and made settlement,
E. C. Meagher, John Sandrock, Ludwig Damberger, Alois Damberger, Billy
Atkinson, B. R. Owen, John and Chas. McGough, George F. Covell, Reinhold
P'Forte, and Fred Volzke, Sr.

We brought with us our small one room shanty from the pre-emption, a single
thickness of boards covered with tar paper on the outside, in which we lived
that winter. Father then, with several neighbors, made a last trip to Ipswich,
about 60 miles, for supplies for the winter. While the men were gone, a prairie
fire came within a few rods of our home, luckily passing on one side; my mother
and Mrs. Meagher, and I a child of 11 years, fought the flames along the side
nearest us, but the head fire roared on past, into the great hay bottom near
the lake southeast of town. It was a close call. If the wind had blown in our
direction, we could have saved nothing, and might have been burned ourselves, as
we had no firebreak, no plowing having been done yet.

Every fall and spring, fires swept the prairie, and it was necessary to have
good firebreaks plowed around the buildings. Several years later, on April 2,
1889, a fire coming from the northwest and driven by the hardest wind I have
ever known, burned out Michael Weinzirl, Ed Meagher and other neighbors, and
roared on many miles to the east, where it burned part of the town of Leola.
Meagher was severely burned while trying to save his horses from the burning
barn. He lost everything, and was confined to bed at our home for a month
because of his injuries.

After fire had swept the prairie, the white bones of the buffaloes that had
died in by-gone days, glistened on the prairie, and I used to pick them up to
be hauled to Ipswich and sold. They were shipped away to be ground up and used
in the refining of sugar.

Before the prairie sod was broken up, deep pathways leading to the various
waterholes that had been made by the buffaloes in the past ages, were to be
seen. Live buffaloes had all disappeared on this side of the river, but there
were still some antelopes.

That first winter, father's horses, fed only on marsh hay cut after frost,
died, as did some of the neighbor's horses. No grain was available for feed,
and the hay was no good. Had we only known it, the prairie grass, which did not
all get burned, was a richer feed, and would have kept them alive if they had
only been turned out to rustle, as it had sustained the buffaloes and antelopes
for ages past. That winter, and for several winters afterwards, we burned
twisted hay (my chore to twist), in the kitchen stove and ate a menu of bread
and beans, with no butter, milk or meat. The nearest fuel we had, except the
hay, was cottonwood logs, to be hauled from the Missouri River.

The first child born in our neighborhood, was Claudia Meagher, March 4, 1885.
During the years 1885 and 1886, the land in our neighborhood was all settled.
Among our new neighbors were George and Pankratz Schaefbauer, Michael Weinzirl,
Sr., Mathias and Simon Brandner, Jacob Traxinger, Joseph Damberger, Sr., Fred
Bickel, Sr., Thomas Gallagher and son Patrick, John Hahne, Max Geidl, Alois
Greiner, W. R. Allison (who had a large herd of cattle and located on the shore
of the lake now called McClaran Lake), John and Charles Rossow, John David,
Horace and Robert Clark; and the Hollanders, who came from Holland, Michigan:
Gerrit Wentzel, Johannes Plaggerman, Jacob Vork and sons Albert and Dirk, Peter
Borr, A. O. Van Dyk, the Tinholts, the Van Dyke brothers and many others.

The first settlers, with the exception of E. C. Meagher and B. R. Owen, built
sod houses. Lumber had to be hauled from Ipswich, and sod was convenient, cheap
and warm. Father built a combination of dug-out and sod in 1885, later adding a
frame addition. (This was used for the first school held in the township, of
which he was the teacher). Fred Bickel, Sr. thatched his house with the tall
hollow grass that grew in the lake (cut after the lake was frozen). The people
who came from Russia built stone furnaces to heat their main living rooms, in
which they burned straw, and being fired morning and evening, kept their houses
warm and comfortable.

The first threshing was done with a log roller, drawn by oxen, driven around
and around the stack of grain, which was thrown down by forkfuls to be
trampled. After the grain was trampled out it was winnowed by tossing forkfuls
up into the wind, which blew out the chaff. There was no over production in
those days.

Our nearest town and postoffice at first was LaGrace, at the mouth of Spring
Creek near the Missouri River, about 17 miles distant. In 1885, the town of
Mound City was platted, and a postoffice established there the next year, and
after the election of 1888, the county seat was moved there from LaGrace.
Father's horses having died that first winter, the next spring he walked to
Ipswich and hired out to work on a farm, leaving mother and us kids alone all
summer. As soon as it was allowed, he commuted on his homestead, and borrowed
money to buy a team of four oxen. Many the hours I spent in my boyhood, driving
these oxen.

The township was organized as a school district in 1886, and named "Vienna"
after the old home of George Covell in New York state. R. M. Slocum, Ludwig
Damberger, and Alois Damberger were the first school board. Father taught the
first term of school that fall, in a room of his own house. He had been a
school teacher in Michigan before coming to Dakota. Among the scholars besides
myself and sister Edith, were Katy Damberger (now Mrs. Mike Huber), the
Sandrock children, Will, Eddie and Emma, and Priscilla and Peter Vork.

The year 1887 was dry, and the next spring, father, with Ludwig and Alois
Damberger, made a trip to Bowdle about the end of March, for seed wheat, which
was furnished the settlers by the railroad company, to be paid back in the fall
at the rate of two bushels in payment for one bushel received. On the return
trip, their wagons got stuck in one of the sloughs south of Mound City, and the
men had to unload and carry the sacks of grain to dry land on their shoulders,
wading through the icy water.

It was this spring, I believe, that our neighbor, Mrs. Herboldt, and another
woman lost their lives, trying to cross Spring Creek on the ice, at the time it
was breaking up. There had been lots of snow that winter (1887-1888), and on
January 12, 1888, occurred the great blizzard, so widely known and spoken of
because of the great number of lives lost in it. We lost no one in Campbell
County, because it began before daylight in the morning, before people had gone
out. It struck us with full force and fury before daylight; but in the eastern
and southern parts of the state, people had gone about their usual work,
children had gone to school and many lives were lost.

On the day before, George Schaefbauer and Mary Hahne had been married at the
Hahne farm, northwest of us, and father had played the violin for their wedding
dance in the evening; and walking home after the dance, had just reached our
door before the storm struck, or he would have been lost. The bride's brothers,
Henry and Joe Hahne, had taken the priest home to the Mission after the
wedding, and they were caught in the storm on their way home, as they started
home before daylight; their lives were providentially saved by their horses
coming up to a sod house, in the darkness of night. They took the horses into
the house, found there fuel and something to eat. Three days and nights the
storm roared on, and it was death to venture out in it. Incidentally, my son
George married a daughter that was born to this couple.

A few weeks later, Fritz and Joachim Kornell, who had come from Germany and
taken homesteads near the Clark brothers, southwest of Herreid, were caught in
a storm a few weeks later, while walking home from LaGrace at night, and both
lost their lives.

Reinhold P'Forte ("Richard") was living in a dugout on the side of a hill on
his claim near Sandrock's and in the storm his home was completely covered with
snow, stovepipe and all. After locating and uncovering the stovepipe, Mr.
Sandrock called down it to Richard,who answering, asked "Is it day yet"? "Yes,
it's three days already", replied Sandrock. "Well," said Richard, "I thought it
was a long night, but I smoked my pipe when I woke up, and went to sleep again."

In 1889, a postoffice, called Vedette, was established in our house with father
as postmaster. We got the mail twice a week, from Mound City, being served by a
mail route that had been established several years before to supply Thule,
about six miles north, where A. O. Van Dyke kept a store and was postmaster. A.
F. Miles and W. F. Fossett were the carriers.

Those early years were meager ones; the settlers had very little money when
they arrived, and had to live the best they could until prairie sod could be
broken with the plow, and crops raised. Most families lived in sod houses.
Fields were small, as breaking the prairie sod was a slow job. Most years were
too dry, and yields of grain low. Wheat was cheap, sometimes about 20 cents a
bushel, and if we got a crop, we had to haul it a long distance at first,
Ipswich was the nearest railroad town; after 1886, Bowdle, and after 1887,
Eureka, which became for a number of years famous as the greatest primary wheat
market in the country, some wheat being hauled as far as 60 miles or more. But
if we didn't get much for our products, our expenses were also light--no gas or
car tires to buy, no radios, very little machinery, and taxes were low. We
burned twisted hay some times, cow "chips", wood from the Missouri river when
we could get it, and some who came from Russia burned "Mischt". Until settlers
made "final proof" and got title to their farms, land could not be taxed; after
they got title, taxes were not more than $10 or so on a quarter of land. What
we had to buy was also cheap, for instance coffee was 15 cents to 25 cents a
pound. We got 5 cents to 10 cents a pound for butter, and 5 cents a dozen for
eggs.

THE RAILROAD COMES

The "Soo" railroad was built into Campbell County in 1901, and the towns of
Herreid, Pollock and Artas sprung up. Mound City had been one of the best towns
in this part of the state that was not served by a railroad; but most of its
people immediately moved their homes and business buildings to Herreid and
Pollock. Among those moved to Herreid, were the Picayune, by R. M. Slocum; the
Vroman Hotel, Clyborne & Eckert's Bank, Fenelon's Livery Barn, Mewing's Flour
Mill, Blair's Drug Store, and the dwelling houses of their owners. A. O. Van
Dyke and Tinholt Bros. moved their stores from Thule, about six miles north of
Herreid. A sad accident took the life of a pioneer, A. O. Van Dyke, who was
caught and crushed between freight cars when the train was switching in the
yards. With the coming of the railroad and the building of the new towns, a new
era for Campbell County was begun. Land values went up, crop production
increased as more land was brought under cultivation, and farm buildings were
improved.

PIONEER DAYS WERE OVER.

          THE FIRST FIFTY YEARS By F. C. Brummund

To begin a history of a city like Herreid, and to do it almost entirely from
memory, is a large task. The mind of man is fallible and for one to sit down
and record a glossary of happenings covering a period of fifty years, the
subconscious mind just will not bring back all that happened in the half
century that concluded. It is, however, our aim to record as accurately all the
facts and events as a human memory will permit.

Any omission you might discover as you read these lines is not intentional; the
fact just did not come back to the writer. Fifty years is a long time for a
memory to function, and then to sit down and make a record of everything is an
utter impossibility.

The writer has been a resident of Herreid for the past fifty years and has a
very vivid recollection of the beginning of the city and its progress during
the fifty years now coming to a close. The task has been undertaken and it must
be finished. It shall be our aim to give you all the facts as accurately as
they come to us in the time allotted. We may even discover many errors later in
our own discourse, and if you also detect some, we trust you will be generous
enough to overlook any errors, oversights and omissions.

The Golden Jubilee of the City of Herreid, celebrated June 29th and 30th, 1951,
marks the completion of the first half century of its existence. The townsite
of Herreid was laid out under the supervision of the Land and Industrial Agent
of the "Soo" Line Railroad early in the year 1901, and is located on sections 7
and 8, Township 127, north of range 76 W. lt is located in the northern part of
Campbell County, seven miles south of the State of North and South Dakota.
Spring Creek flows by the city limits just east of it. The City is located in
the "Richest wheat belt of South Dakota." The country was already well settled
and in a high state of cultivation at the time the "Soo" Line built its road in
here. The "Soo" line was the first railroad to lay its rails in Campbell
County. Although Campbell County was settled in the early eighties, and much
grain was grown within its borders, it had to be hauled to markets outside the
county. First to Ipswich, then to Bowdle, and later to Eureka, as the terminus
of the C. M. & St. P. Railroad were extended westward. At the time Eureka was
the terminus of C. M. & St. P. Railroad, it is said it was the largest primary
grain market in the United States.

Thus the coming of the "Soo" Line Railroad into Campbell County and the birth
of three new towns, Artas, Herreid and Pollock, was not the opening of new
frontiers; it was merely penetrating an area of an already prosperous country
badly in need of transportation facilities to move its products to markets with
more speed and greater ease. The farmers in the northern part of Campbell County
had waited a long time for a railroad, and now nearly twenty years after the
first settlement in the county the long awaited railroad was coming in to solve
their transportation problem.

The late John H. Wishek of Ashley, N. Dak. was instrumental and responsible for
the Wishek-Pollock branch of the Soo Line. He secured the right-of-way from the
farmers adjacent to the road and urged the completion of it with all possible
speed. He saw the need of it all these years farmers had to haul their crops to
Eureka and other distant markets.

The grading of the roadbed began early in the spring of 1901, from Wishek to
Pollock, a distance of seventy miles, and by September the rails had been laid
and work trains were moving over the road. Work trains had hauled urgently
needed building material for grain elevators, and by the time the crop was
threshed that fall, the Soo Line was moving grain to market, and Campbell County
farmers in the north were relieved of the long hauls with slow moving teams and
many with oxen at that.

Three Grain Houses In Operation

So, in the fall of 1901 the Woodworth Elevator Company of Minneapolis,
Minnesota had completed its elevator and was taking in grain with O. A. Olsen
as manager. This company operated the elevator for a number of years, and later
sold it to O. A. Olsen and Karl Nickolaus, a partnership, who also operated it
many years, and later sold it to the Herreid Equity Exchange, a Cooperative,
and this firm still owns and operates it.

The Osborn-McMillan Company of Minneapolis, also pioneered here by building an
elevator just east of the Woodworth house, and began purchasing grain also in
the fall of 1901.

James A. Brady, a farmer, lawyer and merchant of Mound City, also built a
warehouse and began purchasing grain and other farm products in the fall of
1901. He later moved his residence here from Mound City. He later built an
elevator, replacing the old warehouse which was outmoded.

Whenever a new town is planned, a crew of surveyors swing into action, lay out
the blocks, the lots of various sizes, the main street, the side streets, the
alleys and the avenues. After this is done, a plat is made of their work, and
the town is given a name. Herreid was not the original name by which this town
was to be known. It is said some suggested that it should be called "Campa."
We do not know who tried to fasten this cognomen upon this town, but it seems
it met with unanimous disapproval and a new name was sought which would carry a
measure of greater unanimity. It was then decided that the town should be named
for one of our distinguished citizens of Eureka, the Hon. Charles N. Herreid,
then Governor of the state, and this met with general approval and thus the
name was changed to "Herreid."

When the first townsite was laid out it covered only a small area of sixteen
blocks of various size lots, some twenty-five foot, mostly on main street and
also on some side streets. In the residential sections fifty foot lots were
platted. As the pioneers moved in it was soon discovered that this original
townsite was much too inadequate to meet the demands of the incoming settlers.
These early pioneers wanted spacious places and "elbow room" and not be crowded
together like people in the larger cities. Land was plentiful, and not very
costly, then why not have some extra room. Women were strong and healthy, why
not have room for a good garden and spacious lawn. Since there was ample land
contiguous to the original townsite limits, the first addition was platted and
laid out into lots for additional room and expansion of a future city. This
first addition was called the Clyborne & Eckert Addition and comprised ten
blocks. Most of this addition was south of the railroad tracks, and three
blocks of it just east of the original townsite limits. The lots south of the
tracks are long and narrow and most of them contained about five acres, thereby
allowing buyers to pursue extensive gardening and other agricultural lines
could also be carried on. Those to the east of the original townsite were much
smaller and were intended for residential purposes only. It took only a few
years before many of the lots were sold and occupied.

As the population was gradually increasing, more people establishing
businesses, it called for additional building space. This led to the creation
and annexation of another addition, known as the "North Addition" which was
comprised of six additional blocks and a tract of land embracing an area of
14.99 acres, reserved by a certain Fred Quenzer. This tract was not platted but
reserved by him for his residence and pasture space for his livestock. This
tract changed hands many times. Henry Pudwill, Sr. purchased it from Quenzer
and built a large house on it, and at present it is owned by Emil Kost. Several
residences are now located on part of this tract and since it has never been
surveyed and laid out into lots, those who have established homes on it have
their space designated and described by metes and bounds and are satisfied with
such a description for their titles.

In 1902 C. E. Eckert, then the banker here, owned the land east of the town,
presently owned by Jacob Schmidt, built the house now owned by W. O. Olsen. He
laid out an irregular shaped figure of land, somewhat in the shape of a
polygon, which contains about three acres, and upon which he built a most
modern and up-to-date residence. He lived in this home until the fall of 1906,
when he sold out and moved with his family, to Vancouver, B. C., Canada. John
Hirning, who had acquired an interest, and with other stockholders, purchased
the Clyborne and Eckert bank, then became owner of the house. A few years later
the house was sold to M. J. Schirber, when he purchased Hirning's interest in
the bank. In the late thirties when Schirber abandoned his residence here and
moved to Timber Lake, W. O. Olsen purchased the house from him.

Highland Addition Platted

A forty acre tract of land adjoining the city limits on the northwest corner
was still unoccupied, and used for agriculture mostly, was owned by Theo.
Wosnuk. This was acquired and laid out into lots and is known as the Highland
Addition. This area contains some of the most modern and beautiful homes of the
city. The addition has been built up in the last few years. The location is
ideal; it lies on a gradual slope and therefore has excellent drainage and has
attracted many home builders in the last few years.

With the annexation of this last addition the city has expanded its residential
section to a great extent to the northwestern part of the corporate limits.

Early People of Herreid

Among some of the early settlers who came here from other towns to venture into
various forms of business were not the people who failed elsewhere, but people
who saw a brighter future in a railroad town. They were not content to remain
in the valley where no news came from the mountain tops. It was not the "Ultra
Montone" for them; they were people of vision who saw opportunities in the
future and were willing pioneers in every sense of the word. There were no
conveniences here to attract them; they came here to help build a city and were
willing to forgo the comforts and accommodations available in the older cities.
The glare of bright lights held no attraction for them.

The townsite was laid out; the railroad station stood in the exact spot where
it stands today and was manned by an agent just as it is now, only in those
days he worked longer hours. The first agent the Soo Line placed here was a man
by the name of Orin E. Sheppard.  He and his congenial wife lived upstairs in
the depot. She was an excellent cook and they had a horde of boarders whom they
fed three meals a day at the price of $3.50 a week. They enjoyed it and so did
the boarders. A person working for wages in those days couldn't afford to pay
any more. Money was valuable in those early days.

There was a great influx of people from Mound City, also some from Eureka and
Selby. Joe Alker and W. D. McFarland had a saloon and in it was also a
barbershop, Alker was the barber. When there was a rush at the bar, the man in
the chair, half shaved, would just have to lie there and wait for the barber to
return and re-lather his face and finish the job. This, of course, was
understood and it was also permissible to sleep in the chair.

Then there was another saloon, owned and operated by Joe Damberger and Dave
Clark. Joe Damberger is now a resident of Mobridge, retired from the active
toils of life. Mrs. Clark is still a resident of our city while Mr. Clark
passed away many years ago.

There was another building next to the saloons, owned by a John Quaschnick who
was a stock buyer; his building was used for card games, wrestling matches and
other events in the recreation line. The building was always available whether
you had money or not.

Two banks were organized that first year. The Herreid State Bank, incorporated
by a number of stockholders, T. A. Fossum, et. al. and the Clayborne & Eckert
bank, which was located in the present Prairie Picayune building and was the
only bank in actual operation. The State Bank did not open for business until
about the middle of the year 1902. Shortly thereafter the two banks
consolidated and the Clyborne & Eckert bank took over the assets of the State
Bank, moved into its building which was more centrally located in the business
district and sold its building to R. M. Slocum, who moved The Prairie Picayune
printing plant into it, where it has been ever since. The Clyborne & Eckert
bank was moved here from Mound City while the State Bank was a new organization
of short duration.

T. A. Fossum also moved his general store here from Mound City and this
building was located on the lot where the R. H. Ackerman Red Owl Store now
stands.

O. A. Van Dyk moved his store here from Thule and this store was placed under
the management of Emanuel Broeckel. Ann, a daughter of the Van Dyks, clerked in
the store.

J. W. Blair moved two buildings here from Mound City, one in which he had his
drug store and the other, next to it, was used mostly for a storage room but
later it was leased for a dwelling since there were not enough houses available
for the increasing population.

John Campbell had a meat market next to the Blair building. He was also a
professional wrestler and since there was not enough meat business to occupy
his full time, he commuted quite a little between the mat and the meat block.
There was always a hunk of meat on the block and beside it was a huge knife
with which you could cut your own requirement if he was absent from the shop;
it was self-service in pioneer days.

Beside the meat market stood a hotel where everybody had to eat but not sleep,
as there wasn't room enough in the building. The culinary department was in the
ground floor, as it is known today, and this also included the dining hall, a
long counter and high stools. You would have to stand up until a stool became
vacant, and if there were more people standing than vacant stools, a fight
ensued for the vacant stool and the best fighter won the stool.

Next to the hotel was a building which C. H. Mewing had rented for a post
office. He had already received his commission but he had no mail to give the
patrons, however, he always sat in the postoffice. He was a good story teller
and could almost satisfy the people with his enchanting stories thereby most of
them forgot about their mail which they had come for. All mail for Herreid was
dispatched to Mound City, the nearest postoffice. Several times a week the
businessmen would chip in on a small purse and hire someone to drive to Mound
City and bring up the mail.  No one had any newspapers in those days; we made
our own news here and that was all we needed, and the Picayune would give a
resume of it every week.

When the spring flood hit us in the early spring of 1902, and the flat south of
town was one vast sea, about three feet deep for a mile or more, then our mail
driver, whom we had hired, had to drive east several miles, thence south and
thence west and that way he found his way to Mound City where he loaded his
mail and then returned on the same circuitous route.

Finally, about the middle of May the Soo Line got the contract to bring our
mail on the train and we then enjoyed daily mail service.

Next to the post office Fischer & Dickman had a general store, and a tin shop
in the rear of the store; Dickman was also a tinsmith. This they operated for a
while and then sold out to Jahraus & Kaul, who continued the business, while
the former owners purchased the Vroman Hotel in Mound City and moved it here to
open under the name of the "West Hotel."

The last building on the west side of main street, in block five, was the Jacob
Jahraus store. The store handled groceries, feed, confections, cigars, and many
other items too numerous to mention.

On the east side of main street, where the Farmers and Merchants Bank building
stands, the original Picayune printing office was located, and where the
venerable K. M. Slocum, then also a member of the Board of Regents, ground out
his weekly edition of The Prairie Picayune. The paper was originally published
in Mound City, but was moved to Herreid with the coming of the railroad. It was
located in that building until the present building was purchased from the
Clyborne & Eckert bank. Thus the Picayune has been published in its present
building for nearly fifty years. In 1905 a German paper was also published by
Mr. Slocum. "The Nachrichten" was published for many years, first with John
Hirning, then County Superintendent of Schools, as the German editor, and later
a full-time editor was employed, and E. C. Slocum, now of Mound City, as
associate editor and co-publisher of the paper. This paper ran for about six
years and then was discontinued.

The Fenelon livery barn was purchased by L. H. Damberger and Nick Mueller and a
regular livery service was installed. This barn was located on the site where
the J. J. Quenzer garage now stands.

The L. Lamb Lumber Company pioneered with a full stock of building material and
was located on the present site of the Lacher blacksmith shop. Traugut Nickisch
was manager of the yard until the first of March, 1902 and was succeeded by F.
C. Brummund as local manager. The yard was sold to the Equity Lumber Co.,
another Minneapolis line yard but the same manager at the time, was included in
the transfer. Two years later this yard was taken over by the North Star Lumber
Co., also of Minneapolis and again the manager was retained by the new owners.
In 1904 the location of the yard was changed to the main street where some of
the building still is in use by the present Herreid Lumber and Hardware Co.

It might be proper at this junction to mention that F. C. Brummund served in
the capacity of the line lumber yards for twelve years, eight of them as local
manager, and four as an auditor, from 1910 to 1914. In was in 1914 that the
partnership of L. H. Damberger and F. C. Brummund was formed, known as the
Herreid Lumber and Hardware Company. In 1940 the O. W. Schultz Lumber Co. was
purchased and is now occupied by the firm.

Lambert Tinholt was another old pioneer, who moved a warehouse into town from
the Thule neighborhood and first purchased grain and other farm commodities.
Later he added a lumber business on his location. In 1912 he sold out to the O.
W. Schultz Lumber Co. and erected a two story brick structure in which he
carried a large stock of hardware in one part while his brother, Bert, had a
stock of groceries in another section. The second story of the building was
used for an opera house. This building was destroyed by fire a few years later.
Mr. Tinholt perished in the blaze.

The Evangelical Church is another old landmark which was moved here from Mound
City. The Rev. E. C. Oeder was the first pastor. The church was the only one in
Herreid during that first year and was not only used by the Evangelicals, but
other denominations were permitted to hold services in it too.

Fred Martin, now a resident of Mobridge, and Peter Heisler, deceased, were also
among the early businessmen of Herreid. They were in partnership in the
machinery business and had a blacksmith shop in connection. The site of the
business was where the present Volzke & Conway Hardware Store now stands.

Another early personality was Andrew Benzenko, who was the only drayman in town
and did all the hauling for several years. He later sold out to Damberger and
Mueller, the livery barn men who then took over the dray work.

There were two carpenters, Knute Sognessand and Charlie Lehr, who woke up the
people in the early morning hours with the sound of hammer and saw. Carpenters
in the early days started work in the early hours of the morning and hammered
all day long until sundown.

Doctor F. C. Poehler of Minneapolis Minnesota, located here in the fall of
1902, and had his office in a building located where the present Cressman Cafe
now stands. Dr. Poehler died in the fall of 1906 and in 1907 Dr. A. R. Richey
of Iowa City, Iowa, located here and served the community until the fall of
1918 when he became a victim of the "Flu Epidemic" and passed away in the
middle of October of that year.

Dr. Earl Richey, a dentist, located here in about 1908 and practiced here
several years. He was succeeded by Dr. W. H.Shannon, who remained here for many
years.

Albert Volzke is another pioneer resident of Herreid. He was the first farmer
who resided in town and operated his farms from the sidewalks. Most of his farm
work was done by hired help. Later he also engaged in the machinery business in
partnership with T. A. Fossum who had disposed of his general store interests
on main street. In 1908 they sold their machinery business to Andrew
Buckenberger and Henry Ackerman, and was known as the Buckenberger and Ackerman
Implement Company. A few years later John Quenzer bought an interest in the
company and it was then known as Buckenberger, Ackerman and Quenzer. This firm,
after being in business for a number of years, sold out to Philip Huber and son.
The firm is still known by the same name but is now owned by Andrew Huber and
Son, Andrew being the son of the original Philip Huber. Thus the ownership
continues in the lineage of the Hubers.

In 1904 the A. O. Van Dyk store was sold to Karl Weber and Emanuel Broeckel.
This partnership lasted for only a short time, Emanuel Broeckel withdrawing
from the firm. Broeckel associated himself with his brother, Fred, and they
purchased a small hand manipulated cement block machine for the manufacture of
cement blocks. It was a slow process of making blocks and it took them several
months to make enough to build the first cement block building in town. This is
the present Kurtz Garage building. The Broeckels, after completing this
building, opened a furniture store and carried a full line of furniture and
other items in that line. About l908, the Broeckel Bros. sold the business to
John Schaap, who continued it for several years.

Emanuel Broeckel then entered the ministry, going first to a seminary in
Rochester, N. Y. for his theological training and following his ordination he
served many churches in both South and North Dakota. At present he has retired
from the ministry and lives in Bismarck, N. Dak.

Fred Broeckel, who was associated in the furniture business moved to McClusky,
N. Dak. and engaged in farming and at this writing is also retired.

First Big Fire

Early in 1910 a disastrous fire broke out in the Karl Weber store on west main
street. By the time the fire was discovered it had gained so much headway that
the entire block was reduced to ashes. The fire department battled all night
but as the buildings were so closely annexed to each other, none of them were
saved, and only the smoldering embers were in evidence at the break of day. The
only building in the block, the W. S. Riggler store which was isolated from the
rest, remained standing. The buildings were all of wood structure and easily
fell before the consuming flames.

This fire led to the enactment of ordinances regulating materials to be used in
buildings on main street. Considerable objection was raised to this at first,
but the ordinances were enforced and all buildings thereafter constructed on
main street, in the business district, were of concrete or brick and were made
fireproof as much as possible. So, if a fire should break out in one building
it could be confined to the one unit and prevent the spreading which resulted
in a conflagration.

Flour Mill Moved Up From Mound City

In the late fall of 1901, C. H. Mewing moved his flour mill up here from Mound
City, locating it on a railroad for movement of grain and flour. He had the
mill firmly set on a stone foundation (cement was not used here in these early
days), and in the mid-summer of 1902 it was ready for operation. He, however,
never operated the mill but it was taken over by W. F. Varnum, et. al. and went
under the name of the Herreid Milling Company, of which the aforementioned W.
F. Varnum became the manager. Herman Drews (now of Ashley, N. Dak. ) was the
miller; A. Trautman, engineer (the mill was driven by a steam engine), and Ed.
Irwin was a general utility man around the mill, and when the mill operated
twenty-four hours a day, Irwin was the night miller, and another engineer was
employed for the night shift.

About two years later F. R. Schilling, formerly employed by the Osborn McMillan
Elevator Company, became the owner of the mill and W. F. Varnum moved to
Shawnee, Oklahoma and became interested in oil wells and from all reports he
became independently well off financially. The mill did a flourishing business
and Ed Over was added to the list of employees as a regular night miller.

About 1908 the mill was sold to F. W. Boettcher of Eureka and Joe Assenbauer
became the new miller. In 1910 a new grain elevator was built just to the east
of the mill and the force of employees was increased and grain was purchased
for shipment and for milling purposes. Assenbauer overhauled the mill
completely. If memory serves us correctly it was in the early '20s when the
mill was destroyed by fire. The grain elevator was saved and a few years later
was leased to McNulty and Brown. They bought grain and cattle for several
years, until 1929, when the Farmers and Merchants Bank failed and a general
crash in the World's markets; they folded up, and the elevator stood idle for a
number of years, until purchased by Emil Perman, the present owner, who moved it
across the tracks and joined it to the elevator formerly owned by G.Brady and
later by the Brown Grain Company of Minneapolis.

After F. R. Schilling sold the mill he moved, with his family, to Linton, N.
D., where he purchased another grain elevator which he operated for several
years, and then moved to Vermillion, where he again engaged in the grain and
fuel business. From 1901 to 1906, Herreid had no government other than that of
the county. People had been too busy with their own private affairs and were
content with the common law of the land. The five years with no kind of an
organization to regulate the orderly building of a future city did not
encourage and promote individual ambitions; that is, everybody doing things in
his own way, but rather frequent consultations on future civic affairs were
held to coordinate improvements of all public concern. The town had sidewalks
built of lumber and were all of uniform height and width.  Streets were
partially graded, at county expense, but were impassable when wet. Fire hazards
were carefully guarded by everyone, and bucket brigades made their appearance
at the sound of an alarm.

The saloons were licensed by the county, and only the county received the
revenue. The sheriff was the commanding officer of all unorganized towns in the
county. The sheriff, of course, had a deputy in each town and they were the
subordinate officers and held responsible for the orderly conducts in the
absence of the sheriff.

The town had no jail and there was not much need for one in pioneer days. When
it sometimes happened that an unruly person had to be disciplined for
misconduct, a body of citizens promptly took him in charge and placed him into
a box car to think the matter over. The Soo Line always kept a few extra box
cars on the side track for such eventualities, and the feat of box car
incarcerations was a mighty deterrent for those who had incorrigible
inclinations. Anyone who had spent a night in a box car came out a better man
the next morning.

After five years of civilian rule and county supervision it was thought the
time had come to form some sort of a government to run the affairs of the town
in an orderly way and also to get a revenue for public improvements. Better
streets, better sidewalks, better fire protection and better building
regulations were needed, so on September 3, 1906, the first meeting of the Town
officers was held at the office of the Town Clerk.

The following men served the town as its first officers, according to the
official records:
F. C. Brummund, Trustee first ward;
T. A. Fossum, Trustee second ward;
Emanuel Broeckel, Trustee third ward;
J. W. Blair, Town Clerk;
John Hirning, Treasurer;
Paul Damberger, Marshal;
E. A. Malloy, Justice of the Peace;
Peter Heisler, Overseer of Highways.
Upon motion of T. A. Fossum and seconded by Emanuel Broeckel, F. C. Brummund
was elected President of the Board of Trustees and thus served as the first
Mayor of Herreid.

While the town was operating under the trustee form of government all the
officials of the town, (Trustees) were paid an annual salary of $1.00 per year,
payable annually. The marshal was paid a monthly salary of $15.00 payable
monthly. The clerk was paid $50.00 per annum, payable quarterly, and the
treasurer was paid 2 percent on all money paid out by him. It can be seen from
the salaries paid in those days that no official lived in luxury from the
salary he received from the town. In those days people did more work gratis
than do officials these days. Everybody was willing to work, regardless of pay,
to make a better town for people to live in.

In 1908 the town's first and only jail was built at a cost of $1352.55, and
which has now served its purpose for forty-two years. Very few people like to
inhabit the jail and it is probably for that reason that there has been such
few inmates. Once they have spent a night in it, they never come back for
another imprisonment. That is why our justice department has so little work to
do.

During the hobo days the jail was frequently occupied by transients of the
road, particularly in cold weather when the marshal would give them free
lodging for a night. No transient was allowed more than one night of free
lodging. The next morning he was politely told to march on as others might come
along to ask for this free service.

Emanuel Broeckel, first Trustee of the third ward, was engaged in the furniture
business in his own building, now the Kurtz Garage.

John Hirning, first treasurer of the town, entered politics and followed a long
career of political assignments, first as State Auditor, Superintendent of
Banks of South Dakota, and later as Liquidator of a string of National banks in
this state, and finally engaged in the banking business in Lead. He is now
retired and spends most of his time around the state and in California.

All of the other first officers of the town, with the exception of the writer,
have passed on to their final resting place in the Great Beyond.

On July 11th, 1911, at a special election, the Government of the Town of
Herreid was changed to an Aldermanic system and a city of the third class. This
step was taken to give the governing body broader powers and elect more
personnel to the board and spread the responsibility of Government to a larger
body and thus become more democratic in the administration of city affairs.

Under the Trustee system the outgoing officials were J. W. Blair, President of
the Board; D. C. Leidle and Albert Volzke, Trustees, and A. J. Suess, Clerk, M.
J. Schirber, Treasurer, and E. C. Meagher, Justice of the Peace.

When the Aldermanic form of Government was inaugurated it brought in an almost
entirely new set of officials to the helm of the city administration.  Olaf A.
Olsen was the newly elected Mayor; Albert Volzke and Andrew Buckenberger,
Aldermen of the first ward; L. H. Damberger and Henry Ackerman, Aldermen of the
second ward; Jacob Fischer and Peter Mauch, Aldermen of the third ward; M. J.
Schirber, Treasurer; Henry Herbold, City Justice and Paul Damberger, Police
Justice. J. B. Merscher was the first Auditor.

After the Tinholt brick building had been destroyed by fire the city purchased
all the brick and built the Council Room and Fire Hall for the City's fire
fighting equipment. Wm. Enze of Eureka was hired to do the brick laying. This
building has served the purpose for over forty years for council meetings,
polling place, public meetings of all sorts, and in general a meeting place for
everything.

After the change of government in 1911, when the town was incorporated as a
city, many spirited elections followed and many different people have held
office during the past forty years. Everyone who sought office did so with a
view of giving the city a better administration than his predecessor and
generally after every election when the smoke had cleared away, perfect harmony
prevailed again until the next election. Every official worked for the interest
of the city to the best of his ability; democracy works that way and it should
be the aim of all officials to this day.

As has already been stated in a previous paragraph that when Herreid changed
its form of government to the aldermanic system, it was headed by Olaf A. Olsen
as its first City Mayor, and since then the following persons have held that
office:

RECONSTRUCTION

In was on a beautiful night in the month of July, 1910, when Dave Wentz, then
night engineer in the Herreid mill, looked out of the west window, about
midnight and saw what appeared to him to be the start of a fire on the main
street of the town. He immediately blew the whistle of his engine and summoned
the sleeping population from its slumbers. In a very short time people came
running from all sections of the town and found the Karl Webber store in the
full fury of a real conflagration which eventually consumed every building on
the west side, except one lone store belonging to W. S. Riggler.

It was a sad scene the next morning as the people stood around and viewed the
smoldering embers; all that stood unharmed was the bank vault. Owners of the
buildings seemed depressed, and a shadow of gloom hung over the town. Urchins
romped around in the smoky ashes to see what they could find. Businessmen who
had lost all of their earthly possessions were at a loss to know what to do.

The bank vault could not be opened for several days, until it cooled off so
that the contents could be opened in safety.  When the insurance men arrived
and the policies gotten out of the vault, estimates were made of the loss, both
of the buildings and goods, and all businessmen recovered part of their losses,
and this brought encouragement to those who had suffered the most. All the
buildings that had been consumed in the fire were of wood construction.

Now came the thought of rebuilding, but no more wooden buildings could be
erected on main street. Ordinance No. 5, relating to used in the construction
of new buildings stated that they had to be of fireproof material. This
ordinance was enacted in June, 1909,just about one year before the fire. The
only building then on main street complying with the fireproof regulation was
the Farmers & Merchants Bank; it was built of brick and concrete, and was built
in the fall of 1909. This ordinance was passed to get better buildings in the
business district, and have a better town with fewer fire hazards.

Now the question arose, who could build brick buildings, concrete buildings and
the like. It required expert concrete men and good brick layers and a
contractor who understood such work. It required more money and experienced men
to do the job.

After the bank vault had been opened some of the businessmen and the bankers
went into a huddle to see what could be done in the way of financing some of
the building projects for those merchants who had intentions of rebuilding.

A. Laurant, a contractor from Wishek, N. D., was contacted and he came down to
talk the building matter over with the merchants. He had very good ideas and
had the men and equipment to do the job, and do it in a hurry.

Edward Dickman and L. H. Damberger were the first to give him a contract, and
after they had the basement dug, the concrete was poured for the footing, the
building went up in a hurry, and in a very short time they were set up in
business again.

J. W. Blair, owner of the Blair Drug Co. was the next in line and his drugstore
also went up in a short time. He too, was soon reinstated in business.

Albert Volzke and W. S. Siegele had come to an agreement to each build and use
one wall between their buildings. This was the third and last building put up
by Mr. A. Laurant.

The State Bank of Herreid had decided upon a larger building and wanted to
build of brick, and S. W. Jonason Construction Co. of Aberdeen was given the
contract.

All these buildings were constructed to meet the requirements of the ordinance,
and all are still standing and in good condition. Karl Webber did not rebuild.
He loaded his family into his Model "F" Buick and headed west; he landed first
in Idaho and later in Oregon to get a new start in life.

The W. S. Kiggler building was later purchased by A. K. Deibert who had a store
for several years, and this building also burned. He then rebuilt it of tile
and concrete. This is now the City Liquor store. Joe Wolf owned it for many
years and later sold it to the Arbach brothers.

Lambert Tinholt erected a fine brick building on the east side of main street,
which in a few years went up in smoke and was never rebuilt.

This ended the building boom for several years. So the fire that laid the town
in ruins in 1910 gave back to the city better looking and more permanent
structures. Fire, sometimes, while destructive give back to the city
improvements that are better looking and more enduring.

                HERREID - ELECTRICITY

As soon as the mill was moved up from Mound City, we had electricity in town
but not everybody had electric lights, only the mill and the mill residence
were lighted by its small dynamo.  Many of the citizens prevailed upon the
owners of the mill to furnish them electricity but the generators were not
large enough to take on this extra load to furnish everybody with lights. So
only one institution and one house enjoyed the use of electricity.

When Lambert Tinholt built his new building on east main street, he also built
an electric light plant in the rear of his building. Some years later the plant
was sold to the Woodruff Light and Power Company of Bismarck, N. Dak. and a
franchise was issued to this company. The company continued in operation for
quite a number of years under Dr. L. A. Schipfer, also of Bismarck, who finally
acquired complete ownership of this company, until the rate change became
prohibitive (22 cents per KW hour), and the City purchased the plant and
installed an extra large gasoline engine, and employed C. W. Smith to operate
it. When nearly every business place and most of the residences were wired up
for electricity service, and the residential area constantly expanding, it soon
became evident that the plant with its "direct current" was entirely inadequate
to push the juice to all parts of the city, particularly the Highland Addition,
which had only a glimmer of light when a full load was carried. Something had
to be done as people became dissatisfied with the service the city was
rendering. It was either installing a larger motor, and switch to the
alternating current or dispose of the plant to some outside firm which had the
finances to effect such a change.

The Northern Power and Light Company then purchased the plant from the city,
rebuilt the lines and installed the alternating current which could push the
juice to all parts of the city. This company owned the plant until about 1948,
when it was transferred to the Montana-Dakota Utilities Company, which now owns
it. This company maintains its lines with a resident serviceman and good
service is given to everybody.

                WATER AND SEWER

The greatest need for a city from a standpoint of sanitation and for the
preservation of healthful co-habitation of its citizens is a good water and
sewer system. But it took a great many years before this was accomplished in
Herreid. Many of our residents had installed their own water and sewer system
by using septic tanks for disposal and automatic pumps for bringing in the
water from shallow wells. These systems had been getting numerous and there was
fear of contaminating the surface water and bringing about unhealthful
conditions in general. The numerous recurring floods in the springtime did not
improve matters much but had a tendency to pollute one well after another and
was every reason to cause alarm.

In the spring of 1929 pressure was brought upon the City Council to call a
special election to float a bond issue for $25,000.00 and with another
approximate $25,000.00 in the city treasury, it was estimated that $50,000
would be sufficient to put in a water system. The bond issue was carried by a
vote of 131 in favor and 60 against. The City Council did therefore determine
that the election was duly carried in favor of the issue and also by a special
act of the State Legislature passed an act "Legalizing and Validating" all acts
relative to the $25,000.00 bond issue of the City of Herreid, Campbell County,
for the specific purpose of constructing, extending and maintaining a water
system in said city, voted for at a special election on the 18th day of
February, 1929. It should be noted that this undertaking did not, however, meet
with general approval despite the fact that the bond issue was carried in that
it was only for a water system and did not include a sewer system; it did not
answer the purpose. Water could have been run in but there was no outlet
provided and thereby must be taken out again in pans.

The bonds were then offered for sale but about that time a general depression
descended upon the country and the bonds could not be sold. Banks failed right
and left and the whole project exploded and our dreams faded. The City treasury
was also depleted of its funds through bank failures and we had to be satisfied
with our surface wells, outdoor toilets and septic tanks until a recovery could
be made, financially as well as the moral support of the project.

About the time we had gained our equilibrium the W.P.A. days set in and it
required astute management to pilot the ship safely to shore. Our recovery was
slow, taxes were delinquent and the demands on treasuries were great.
Gradually, as times got better and people began paying their taxes, the
functioning of cities improved as the years passed.

Again, in 1945, the movement for a water and sewer system was undertaken. By
that time the city had accumulated a substantial sum of money which had been
invested in Government bonds. The city's credit rating was good and another
bond issue was submitted to the voters in the sum of $38,000.00. This bond
issue was carried by a large vote, but was far from enough to finance the
project. Prices on material were constantly rising and so was labor and once
again the installation of the system looked very dubious. However, the
insistence of the people kept growing and the urgency increased.

The City Council was then petitioned to create a municipal liquor store to help
finance the project. This was submitted to the voters and was carried by a
substantial vote. The credit of the city was increased in that it could issue
revenue bonds to help finance the completion of both the water and sewer
system. This gave relief and plans were then entertained to proceed with the
project.

Arthur S. Mannesm, consulting engineer of Aberdeen, was then employed to
prepare the plans and specifications. This was in 1945 when the council saw its
way clear to proceed with the project. After the plans and specifications had
been completed, bids were called for and the bonds were readily sold, the
Campbell County Bank purchasing the entire stock. The funds on hand, however,
were far inadequate to complete the entire project and then an additional
$100,000.00 revenue bonds had to be disposed of to complete the entire
installation of both water and sewer. Practically all of the revenue bonds were
purchased by local people.

Phelps-Drake and Company of Minneapolis, Minnesota, was awarded the contract
and work was commenced in the spring of 1946. It took about a year and a half
to complete the installation of the water, sewer, and disposal plant.

The City Council in office at the time of the installation of the project
consisted of the following personnel: F. C. Brummund, Mayor; A. R. Bickel, City
Auditor; Edwin H. Rische, Alderman; Labolt Kramlich, Alderman; Alex Kurtz,
Alderman; A. K. Deibert, Alderman; Walter Salzsiedler, Alderman; W. O. Olsen,
Alderman; and Matt Seiler, Treasurer.

                TELEPHONE SERVICE

Valley Telephone Cooperative, Herreid, was incorporated in 1952 and RTA loans
were approved in 1954. Previously, Missouri Valley Telephone had served Herreid
and the formation of the new cooperative brought about some drastic changes.
VTC replaced the old magneto phones in 1956, thus eliminating the need of a
local operator.

Directors at the time of incorporation were: Sam Merkel, Glenham; Dave
Wittmeier, Pollock; Glen Tolstedt, Herreid; Edwin Tronson, Glenham; Henry P.
Ochsner, Herreid; Joe Wolf, Herreid; Theodore Molstad, Glenham; Arthur Thorson,
Pollock, and John A. Kirschenmann, Artas.

Missouri Valley Telephone consisted of Herreid, Mound City, Pollock, and
Glenham exchanges. In 1959 VTC acquired Leola and Hosmer and in 1964 the Long
Lake exchange was added.

Valley Telephone presently serves 2700 stations with the cooperative's
headquarters located in Herreid. Many improvements have been made since the
cooperative's inception and today all subscribers enjoy private line service
and all cable is buried.

Present directors are: Sam Merkel, Glenham, president; Thomas Heibel, Leola,
vice president; John Vojta, Mound City, secretary; Joe Wolf, Herreid,
treasurer; Milton Aman, Hosmer; David Wittmeier, Pollock; Maynard Schmidt,
Herreid; George Dockter, Long Lake, and Ralph Ritter, Mound City.

VTC employees include: Don Hinkley, Herreid, general manager; Lavern Skott,
Leola, area manager; Frank Gisi, Hosmer, area manager; Jerry Graff, Herreid,
area manager; Bob Schuetzle, Herreid, combinationman; Dianna Quashnick,
Herreid, billing clerk, and Ruth Grenz, Artas, cashier receptionist.

Valley Telephone Cooperative, Inc. serves the counties of Brown, Campbell,
Edmunds, McPherson, and Walworth.

                HERREID FIRE DEPARTMENT

Up until 1914 the Fire Department records are not available, but the City did
have a volunteer fire department that functioned at every fire, and did
commendable fire fighting at the sound of the bell. Most of the fires were
fought with chemical engines, and also a bucket brigade went into action.
During that period every able bodied man was a fire fighter.

However, on April 28th, 1914, the first organized fire department was created
by a group of citizens at a meeting called for that purpose. When the call was
issued, everybody turned out to help form the organization on a permanent
basis. Great enthusiasm prevailed at the meeting.

M. J. Schirber was appointed temporary chairman of the meeting, with Walter A.
Kludt, secretary.

The following officers were elected: F. C. Brummund, Fire Chief; Henry
Ackerman, Assistant Chief; O. A. Olsen, Treasurer; and Walter A. Kludt,
Secretary.

The enrollment of charter members was the next order of business and the
following persons enrolled: F. C. Brummund, Henry Ackerman, O. A. Olsen, M. J.
Schirber, C. O. Robbins, Paul Damberger, L. H. Damberger, A. E. Fossum, Walter
A. Kludt, Harry Stoller, Gerner A. Olsen, R. C. Bowman, Fred Bickel, Fred
Fischer, Henry O. Bens, Fred Olhauser, A. J. Suess, J. B. Merscher, Joe
Assenbauer, Nick Mueller, H. G. Barnett, John F. Webber, Alfred Olsen, Earl
Rosenberg, Edward Buckenberger, C. W. Smith, A. E. Richey, Ed. Dickman, W. H.
Kennedy and Henry Herbold.

The constitution and by-laws for the department were drawn up by a committee of
three: M. J. Schirber, J. B. Merscher and F. C. Brummund, and adopted April 29,
1914.

At the April 4, 1916 meeting, Paul Damberger was elected Chief, H. G. Barnett
was elected Assistant Chief and A. E. Fossum was elected secretary. A group was
appointed to approach the City about buying some supplies and a warning system.

The Fire Department has functioned continuously since its creation, first under
the bucket brigade system when everybody brought his own pail to render what
assistance he could. Then the city purchased two chemical engines which were in
use for several years, and later a home-made water truck where a five hundred
gallon tank was mounted on a truck. It had a pump on the front end of the truck
and was driven by the truck engine and this contraption did effective work.
Another thousand gallon supply tank mounted on a trailer would follow up and
replenish the tank on the truck with the motor.

In April of 1948 a new fire truck was purchased by the Fire Department. By
January, 1951, it was felt that a rural fire department was needed. Farmers in
the area were under the protection of the Herreid, Pollock, and Mound City Fire
Departments.

A new truck was purchased in 1968. In 1975 a fire bus to carry fire fighters
was purchased and a crew cab truck with four wheel drive was also added.

The move to a new location also took place and now provides adequate space to
house all the units of the department.

Specially trained firemen operate the Herreid-based Campbell County Ambulance
which replaced an earlier ambulance system. The present unit is one of the most
up-to-date and best equipped ambulances available. Crewmen have completed the
necessary training to operate the new ambulance and provide a valuable service
by being on call 24 hours a day, seven days a week, to the people in Herreid
and the surrounding area.

Dennis Bates is the present Fire Chief and Don Kary is Assistant Chief.  Jerome
Graff is Ambulance Crew Chief and Lloyd Kusler is Assistant Crew Chief. At
present there are 26 firemen in the department.

Over the years, the old fire bell which summoned fire fighters has been
replaced by a siren and of late a new Civil Defense warning system has been
added. The old bell is being used as a monument, located near the Herreid
Swimming Pool.

In summation, the Fire Department has not only kept a fine fire fighting
record, but has developed into a highly trained body of volunteers which
respond immediately to ambulance and other emergency calls. Through the years
of changing fire fighting techniques and medical training, the Herreid Fire
Department has kept abreast of the changes.

     HERREID BAPTIST CHURCH

The beginning of the Herreid Baptist Church was in December, 1897, when a group
of members of the Eureka Baptist Church who lived in the Mound City area
gathered in the home of Brother Friedrich Kramlich, Sr. for the purpose of
organizing a new church. This was done in full understanding with the "mother
church". Pastor Louis Wiesle of Eureka led this organization.

The name under which this new group organized was: "German Baptist Church of
Mound City". In 1908 the name was changed to "German Baptist Church of
Herreid". In 1955 the name was changed to "Herreid Baptist Church."

The new church grew quickly and the following stations were begun: Pilgerheim,
Herreid, Thule, Gnadenfield, Bibelheimer and Pollock. Later there were also
stations at Spring Creek and Artas. As time went on these stations were closed,
one by one, the last being the Artas Station which closed in 1964.

The first church building was built in Herreid in 1902. In 1921 this original
building was sold to the Reformed Church in Herreid and a new church was
erected on the same location. During the years 1930-33 this church was
enlarged. In 1936 the church was destroyed by fire. This loss was a heavy one
for the church but the brethren never lost courage, for in the same year they
built the present church building.

The first pastor's residence was built in 1907. In 1948 the parsonage was moved
to a lot several blocks north and the present parsonage was built.

In 1948 the Church observed its 50th Anniversary at which time a historical
booklet was printed. Throughout the years the Church has always been missionary
minded. Great sums have been given for the local church and for missions. For
several years the church has assumed partial support of Miss Barbara Kieper, a
missionary nurse in Warwar, Nigeria.

     FIRST REFORMED CHURCH

The bell rings out on Sunday morning calling all to come for worship and
fellowship at the First Reformed Church in Herreid.

This church dates back to the first decade of the twentieth century. German
settlers from Russia banded together to worship in accordance with their
Reformed persuasion. On April 2, 1904, they joined the Odessa Charge, Eureka
Classis of the Reformed Church in the United States. The first members listed
in the records of 1909 were Heinrich Ackerman, Johan Bickel, Henry Boehler,
And. Buckenberger, Henry Herbold, Jacob Jahraus, Friedrich Jahraus, Heinrech
Klein, Johan Knodal, Karl Nickolaus, Jacob Suess, and Karl Wacker.

During a ten year pastoral vacancy from 1942-1952, many of the members united
with other local churches, and it looked as though the First Reformed Church
might disappear. However, in God's providence, a few members were received from
the dissolved congregations of the neighboring Missouri and Friedens Reformed
Churches.

In the spring of 1959, the congregation built a new place of worship.
Previously they had used a "hand-me-down" church. Within five months the new
building was constructed and on October 11, 1959, it was dedicated. Rev. N. C.
Hoeflinger (their pastor) wrote in the "Reformed Herald", "For a congregation
of but fifteen families to undertake to build a church may have seemed like
folly to the world.  But the zeal and enthusiasm of these members was based on
a solid faith, and was backed up with sacrificial giving and plenty of hard
work."

This "solid faith" is the conviction that the Bible is the infallible inerrant
Word of God to be interpreted only by itself. The summary of its teaching is
the Reformed faith or Calvinism. The Heidelberg Catechism has been adopted as a
faithful formulation of the Bible's content and is the church's creed.

The congregation of the First Reformed Church has been served by these pastors
of the Odessa Charge:  William Feige (1903-1906), W. B. Wittenberg (1906-1908),
M. Nuss (1909-1920), John Grossmann (1921-1924), O. G. Schmidt (1925-1926),
Karl Thiele (1927-1930), F. A. Rittershaus (1931-1935), Otto Hoffner
(1937-1940), E. G. Pfeifer (1941-1942), Fritz Lierhaus (1952-1954),  N. C.
Hoeflinger 1959-1961), L. Jones (1961-1967), R. Grossmann (1967-1975), and S. E
Work (1975-present).

We are enjoying our 17th year in our church building, and God has blessed us
with 22 families. God's work continues in our church with worship services,
Sunday School, Confirmation Class, Bible Study, and Youth Fellowship.  "To God
be the glory! Great things He hath done."

     UNITED METHODIST CHURCH

Before writing the history of the United Methodist Church of Herreid, it would
perhaps be appropriate to give a brief summary of the beginning of the United
Methodist Church in this country. In the early 1960's talks began in both the
Methodist and E. U. B. Churches to unite the two churches into one.  Originally
when the church was organized in this country it was named the Methodist
Church. In later years among the German settlers there was formed a separate
church called the Evangelical Church but they retained the same doctrine and
form of organization as the Methodist Church to which they originally belonged.
For many years the two churches existed side by side, one serving the German
speaking people and one serving the English speaking people. After several
years of talk the two churches formally united again, forming a new corporation
effective as of January 1, 1969, and the two uniting churches dissolved their
former organization and became a new church named the United Methodist Church.

Now, we will begin with the United Methodist Church of Herreid. Since this
church was a former E. U. B. Church, for the purposes of the description of the
church and history of the church we will treat it by its former name, that is,
the E. U. B. Church. The history of the E. U. B. Church of Herreid, had its
beginnings with other fields or stations in the vicinity of Herreid and in
order to get a true picture of the beginning of this church it is well to
mention these other stations because the Herreid church is an outgrowth of the
same.

In the year of 1892 information had reached the early settlers in the Mound
City area that Rev. G. W. Hielscher and Rev. G. W. Rekow had conducted
preaching services in the home of one Ferdinand Gatzke, who lived at that time
about eight miles southeast of what is now Herreid. This home was probably the
first station where preaching services were conducted by any Evangelical
preacher in Campbell County. An invitation was extended to those ministers to
also preach to the early settlers in and near the Mound City area which
invitation was accepted and from the years of 1893 to 1897 they preached to
these settlers at some of the stations known as Mound City, Gatzke's, Schell's,
Hiddenwood, and Sutley.

In the year of 1897 some of the early settlers organized a church in Mound
City, S. D., and built a church building at a cost of $600.00. Some of the
known charter members were Charles Rossow, Sr., one Moewing and Fred Martin,
Sr.  After the church and parsonage were built at Mound City, G. A. Rekow
became the first resident minister living at Mound City, to serve the Mound City
church and surrounding stations.  He was followed in the same year by Rev. H.
A. Machaelis, who served until 1899.  From 1899 to 1900 Rev. B. H. Fischer
served that field.  In 1900 Rev. Samuel Finkbeiner was assigned to this field
and probably served until 1902.  In May 1902, the Dakota Conference purchased
Lots 4, 5 and 6 in Block 14, Original Townsite of Herreid, for church and
parsonage purposes. In the same year the church building and parsonage located
at Mound City, were moved to the new location at Herreid and the Mound City
station was abandoned.

The first resident minister of the Herreid Church was Rev. E. C. Oeder.  The
Evangelical Church was the first church to be established in Herreid and served
the spiritual needs of the early settlers in this newly established town. The
morning worship in this church was conducted in German and the evening services
were conducted in English for the benefit of all other faiths and those who
could not understand the German language.

Other ministers who followed Rev. E. C. Oeder and resided at Herreid, were Rev.
A. Zunkel, Rev. Samuel Finkbeiner, Rev. J. Brockmueller, Rev. B. H. Fischer,
Rev. E. Jensen, Rev. Adam Geise, and Rev. Jacob Herning. These ministers served
Herreid and the surrounding stations from the years of 1902 to 1920.  After 1920
Herreid was discontinued as a separate field and was united with the Eureka, S.
D. circuit. The first minister serving Herreid out of Eureka was Rev. Samuel
Finkbeiner, from 1920 to 1923. Then followed Rev. W. H. Krueger, from 1923 to
1924, Rev. G. H. Kowalke from 1924 to 1925. Rev. J. A. Hochstatter from 1925 to
1926. Rev. G. H. Kowalke served again from 1926 to 1930. Rev. Wm. A. Juedes
from 1930 to 1933, Rev. A. Bloedau from 1933 to 1939, Rev. O. A. Goehring from
1939 to 1944 and Rev. E. T. Ehresman from 1944 to 1949.

In the year 1949 Herreid was separated from the Eureka circuit and was joined
to the Bowdle, S. D. circuit.  Rev. Elgin Boegler, who resided at Bowdle served
Herreid out of Bowdle from 1949 to 1952.

In the year 1952 Herreid and Artas, S. D. were united into one field and the
first minister serving this new field was Rev. Walter Schlecht, who resided at
Herreid and served this field from 1952 to 1955. Rev. Rudolph Rieker followed
Rev. Schlecht, receiving his appointment in 1955.

Rev. Rieker served the Herreid and Artas field until June, 1962. During his
ministry the present building was built.  Next followed Sterling Wenzel, who
served the field until October, 1963.  Johnny Walters, of the Mennonite faith,
applied to serve and on October 1, 1963, was appointed by the Dakota
Conference.  Walters disapproved of the union of the two churches and did not
complete his 1967-1968 appointment but served only until December 31, 1967.
After Walters left many of the E. U. B. Church members also left, greatly
weakening the local church. On January 1, 1968, Rev. Olney White served the
Herreid Church out of the Selby Methodist Church. Rev. Orland Sloat followed
Rev. Olney White, also serving the local church out of the Selby Methodist
Church. During the ministry of Rev. Sloat talks were started to have ecumenical
services held among some of the churches in Herreid. It was finally decided to
have one pastor serve the Herreid United Methodist Church and the Herreid
American Lutheran Church.  The American Lutheran Church was to make the first
call. Pastor A. E. Loken accepted the call to serve the two churches and was
installed November 29, 1970, after which date he served both the United
Methodist and the American Lutheran Church of Herreid, until September 1, 1975,
when he left to accept a call in Philip, S. D.  The United Methodist Church and
the American Lutheran Church were temporarily served by Pastor Loren Odland of
the Pollock Lutheran Church until January 8, 1976, when Philip Strain was
installed as the pastor of the United Methodist and the American Lutheran
Churches.  Pastor Strain is now the present pastor of the two churches.

     PEACE LUTHERAN CHURCH

In writing a short history of an organization such as a church or congregation
it requires compiling facts and data. It is impossible to recapture the spirit
that motivated the organization.  One cannot recreate the joys and the sorrows
which were part of existence and part of its struggle in its life. Suffice,
then, that important dates and events be gathered as they took place.

On July 26, 1908 a group of Lutherans met with Pastor Bruegel, as leader, to
explore the possibilities of organizing a Lutheran congregation.  This group
decided to go ahead and organize. The name, Peace Lutheran Church, was adopted
as its official name. Two men were elected to serve as temporary deacons until
the group could adopt a constitution.  The men elected were David Wenz and Fred
Ohlhauser.  The following were the charter members of the congregation: David
Wenz, Fred Ohlhauser, Christ Ottenbacher, Sr., W. S. Siegele, Karl Haney,
Gottlieb Erlenbusch, Ludwig Boettcher, Jacob Borth and Martin Ohlhauser.

On June 23, 1910, under the leadership of Pastor Mall, the permanent
organization took place. A constitution was adopted and steps were taken to
legally incorporate.

A secretary and a treasurer were elected. That same year the group decided to
make plans to build a church structure the following year. Money was collected
and in the spring of 1911 the present building was erected at a cost of $3,000.
The pastor at the time was the Rev. Mayer. He was the first pastor to serve
Peace Lutheran. The small group extended itself valiantly and managed to
maintain itself and also pay its church debt. For a time the congregation was
unable to have a resident pastor and was served from Hoffnungstal Lutheran
Church.

In 1920 a resident pastor was again secured for Peace Lutheran in the person of
H. Werth. Because the congregation found it difficult to find a suitable house
for its pastor, the congregation made plans to build or buy its own parsonage.
Not until the fall of 1922 could their plans be realized. At that time the
present site and a parsonage were purchased for $2,500. A garage was added to
the property in 1924. Beginning with 1924 regular English services were held.
Thereafter progress became more rapid.  A ladies organization was effected
under the leadership of Pastor E. J. Hammer. The need for a place to hold
Sunday School and for the ladies to meet brought about the decision to dig a
basement under the church building. The same was accomplished with a minimum of
expense, the members doing most of the work. During the pastorate of E. J.
Hammer a young people's organization was brought into being.

During the depression and drought years the congregation suffered from some
losses and there were no visible improvements. The group, however, did not give
up, and in spite of adverse conditions, kept up its property and managed to
show some growth.

With the change in economic conditions a new lease on life was taken. In the
summer of 1940, the parsonage was completely remodeled and enlarged.  Necessary
repairs and changes were made. A new garage was built in 1945.  In 1947 the
parsonage was modernized and the necessary remodeling to bring this about was
done.

Members of Peace Lutheran desired a new house of worship, so in 1951 they began
making plans. Once the pledges were realized and a building fund was
established, work began.

The joys of their accomplishment came when the new church was completed and
dedicated to God on November 29, 1953 with Pastor Julius Roehl serving the
parish from 1939 to 1954.

At the request of a group of Lutheran families, Pollock started investigating
the possibility of establishing their own congregation. It was at this time
during the pastorate of J. Mayer that Pollock Golgatha, which had been
affiliated with the Herreid parish since 1911, dissolved and formed their own
congregation known as Pollock Lutheran, in 1955.

In the fall of 1958 Peace Lutheran Church, now under the leadership of Pastor
Lowell Green, who succeeded Pastor J. Roehl, observed its 50th anniversary. The
main feature was a mortgage burning ceremony. This marked the congregation as
being debt-free.

Mrs. Lydia Robbins of Herreid, who served as organist for forty years, was
honored at this event.

At the time of the merger in 1960, the Peace Lutheran Ladies Aid became known
as the American Lutheran Church Women. This organization contributes much to
the congregation.

The Luther League consists of young confirmed high school youth. They are a
valuable part of our congregation.

In 1959 another event made church history when Peace Lutheran built and
dedicated a new parsonage on December 13, 1959.

A son of the congregation, Junior Roehl, was ordained into the holy ministry by
his father, Pastor Julius Roehl, on May 30, 1965.

Hoffnungstal, a country church west of Eureka, and Peace Lutheran, were served
by Pastor Marvin Ketterling.  This two-point parish dissolved March 24, 1969.

Again, in 1970 with Pastor A. E. Loken serving Peace Lutheran and United
Methodist formed a two-point parish. The United Church of Christ (First
Congregational) joined this yoke in the spring of 1971 to make it a three-point
parish. Following five years the United Church of Christ secured a pastor and
dissolved from the yoke.

Pastor F. Philip Strain is presently serving the yoked parish following his
ordination in December of 1975. He was installed at Peace Lutheran January 18,
1976.

Peace Lutheran feels they have been showered with many blessings. "The Lord
hath done great things for us."

     ST. MICHAEL'S CATHOLIC CHURCH

According to an old parish record book, the Rev. Claudius Ebner, stationed at
St. John Church, five miles north of Zeeland, N. D., was the first priest to be
assigned to take charge of the spiritual needs of the Catholics of Campbell
County. The Irish or English speaking Catholics from the eastern part of the
United States were the first to arrive and had settled in the northwest corner
of the county in the Missouri River bottomlands. Some of the men married Indian
women. About 1884 the Germans from Bavaria started homesteading and settled
along Spring Creek, the territory which later became Herreid. Soon people from
Austria, Hungary, Russia and Switzerland arrived. Now the parish is composed of
parishioners with backgrounds of almost every nationality.

The settlers were served by these missionary priests: Abbot Martin Marty,
O.S.B., who later became Bishop of Dakota Territory; Rev. Claudius Ebner,
O.S.B.; Rev. Jerome Hunt, O.S.B., Rev. Chrysostom, O.S.B.; Rev. Bede Marty,
O.S.B.; Rev. Bernard Strassmeier, O.S.B.; and Rev. Nicholas Dahlmanns of
Hillsview, S. D.

In the beginning these missionaries had to walk from place to place so visits
were only once or twice a year. Later, they were able to come more often as
settlers began to acquire horses. The settlers would go to get them for
services and return them to their mission center. After services, all the
babies who had been born since the last visit were baptized. During that time
(1884-1903) Mass was offered in private homes. A cemetery was located one mile
east of the present site of Herreid.

In the summer of 1895 a sod church was built on a hill one mile west of what is
today the town of Herreid. The land was donated by Michael Weinzirl, Sr. and
all but the carpenter work was done by the parishioners, who made and laid the
bricks. The church was placed under the patronage of St. Michael the Archangel.
The sod church remained standing for many years, being torn down some time in
the 1930's. The cemetery east of town was moved to the new location east of the
sod church. The tombstones and markers on the early graves were made by Max
Buck, one of the original parishioners and pioneers of the area. Father Louis
Brunner, pastor of St. Michael's for thirty-seven years, who died June 17,
1968, is buried in this cemetery.

In 1901 the Soo Line built the railroad through Campbell County and the City of
Herreid came into being.  In 1903 the Mission of St. Michael was constituted a
parish.  A rectory was built in the town and Father Joseph Schell became the
first resident pastor with missions at Pollock, Selby, Java and Evarts.
Services were still held in the sod church until 1906 when a frame church was
built near the rectory. That also was a parish project, with the farmers
bringing in the loads of rock needed for the foundation and all helping with
the carpenter work. This church was dedicated by the Most Rev. Thomas O'Gorman,
Bishop of Sioux Falls.

In 1926 the present parish house was built. In the fall of 1945 the
parishioners started a building fund which was to be used toward the erection
of a new church, as the frame church was now too small for the growing
congregation. The cornerstone was laid in June, 1956. When the new brick church
was completed, it was dedicated by the Most Rev. Lambert A. Hoch, Bishop of
Sioux Falls, in June, 1957.

The ladies of the Altar Society observed their fiftieth anniversary in 1975.
They have sponsored various social activities, especially a bazaar in the fall
of each year. In 1969 a Parish Council, consisting of six lay members to advise
the pastor, was organized. The young people have been very active in the
Catholic Youth Organization (CYO) which was established in the spring of 1975.

AMERICAN LEGION

In 1920, a group of ex-soldiers got together and after some discussion it was
decided to form a Legion Post.  That same month, an organizational meeting was
held and application for a charter was made. The charter was granted February
16, 1920 and the post was named the Roosevelt Post No. 170.  Some of the
leading lights in the venture were: Henry Bens, George Schaefbauer, Harold
Blair, Andrew Huber, Martin Bickel, Emil and Ed Buckenberger.

George Schaefbauer was elected the first Commander but since the entire
National Legion Body was in its infancy, there was no special or detailed
program to go by, thus very few meetings were held and interest gradually died.
The next year Harold Blair became Commander and another year elapsed without
much being accomplished.  The net result was that the Post died a painless
death, however the charter was not canceled until May 14, 1926.

For a few years the veterans who joined the Legion did so at Eureka. A few
joined the Post at Mound City and later on most of the veterans in this area
signed up with the Melvin Horby Post No. 54 and this post, in effect, became a
County Post.

Meetings were held in rotation in the four towns of the County. Memorial Day,
and other patriotic exercises were also held under that plan for several years.
It became increasingly evident that regular attendance was impossible to be
obtained, so a few years later the veterans in Pollock decided to form their
own Post. This was followed in the year 1947 by the veterans of Herreid.

A meeting was called at the City Hall on September 22, 1947, for the purpose of
organizing. The sentiment was unanimous in favor of organizing a Post and under
the guidance of District Commander H. L. Fetherhuff, the Post was born. It was
given the name "Jacob A. Heinrich" and the State Department assigned it the old
number, 170, which originally was the Roosevelt Post number.

The new Post expanded rapidly and became very active in a number of community
affairs.

After paying rent for various meetings for about a year, sentiment rapidly
crystallized in favor of constructing a Legion Hall, so that the Post might be
housed in its own building and so that its own entertainments and meetings
could be held whenever and in such manner as they might wish.

On March 1, 1948, by resolution of the Post, a committee was instructed to
contact I. T. Rische and draw up plans for a 30'x40' building. This was done
and the Post immediately took up the problem of financing the project. It was
done by levying two $25.00 assessments on the members, two big raffles, and
numerous "smokers".  Most of the preliminary rough work of the building project
was done by the Legionnaires and except for the laying of the cement blocks and
certain woodwork, the members worked whenever they could from Sept. 16, 1948
until the building was practically complete.

The Legion Hall is a fitting monument to the men of this area, who gave their
lives in the service of their country.

Reviewing the minutes and activities of the Post for the past 25 years show
many general activities of the American Legion carried on with no special
effort on any one of them.  Regular election of officers, Boys Stater program,
Memorial Day services, presentation of school awards, baseball programs, some
community service, Armistice Day observances and Christmas candy and free movie
for the small children, are among the projects.  Assistance to veterans has
always been a strong effort in any Legion post, especially for those needing
hospitalization.  Post Service Officers and later County Service Officers
became the rule.

The Legion Home is sort of a general meeting place for numerous organizations
in and out of town.  A lot of the meetings were free to many organizations,
especially ones like 4-H, health groups and many of general county-wide
importance. Oratory and declamation were also recognized but not too much
participation in them.

In 1959, an event occurred that gave the local post a terrific shot in the arm.
One if its members decided to run for State Commander. H. L. Fetherhuff, who
had worked his way up from Post Commander to State Vice Commander, decided to
run for the big office. He was endorsed by the Jacob A. Heinrich Post No. 170
and a state-wide committee was formed for the effort. Due to a wide
acquaintance in this area both from the Postal and Legion circles, the effort
quickly picked up steam and at a general meeting in Aberdeen of most of the
regional leaders, including John W. Smith of Eureka, an aggressive campaign was
started and strongly pushed. The State Convention was held at Mitchell and in a
very spirited contest involving nearly every post in the state, Fetherhuff was
elected.

Coming from a small post with no great political savvy this was indeed a
remarkable feat. As is customary, the State Commander was given a royal
homecoming celebration.  Gov. Herseth, a National Legion Vice Commander, and a
host of other Legion officials attended. The local Post put on a good program
at the school auditorium, followed by a big feed at the City Park. It was
estimated that 750 people were fed at the affair. Photographers from Aberdeen
and other places recorded the event and the results are contained in a fine
scrap book presently in Fetherhuff's possession.

Since the campaign got off to so fine a start, heavy emphasis was laid on the
lifeblood of the Legion--membership.  All previous records were broken and a
record established that was good until 1972 when the Vietnam veterans began to
enroll in the Legion. During this period, the Jacob Heinrich Post was regarded
by many as one of the better Posts in the state.

Interest declined somewhat after 1960 but the Post has managed to maintain its
quota of members for 23 years.

Generally speaking, the Legion has taken the lead in many activities of the
community, especially patriotic ones.  Legion endeavors are widespread and
include child welfare, rehab work, veterans preference in jobs, hospitalization
of the ailing and crippled vets, strong support of a strong U.S.A. and anything
else that would better this country.

HERREID CIVIC CLUB

The Civic Club came to life in September of 1927 in the M. J. Schirber home, as
a community service club.  Mame Schirber was elected president, but resigned in
October on account of health reasons and Lydia Robbins was elected to replace
her.

By-laws were drawn up: all members must be active, dues were set at 50 cents
per year, meetings to be held the first Tuesday of the month--September to May
inclusive, in the basement of the State Bank. Later the club met in the
members' homes.

The School Library was not accredited, so that was the Civic Club's first
project. Books, suitable for children's reading, were bought or donated by club
members. Other things which the club became involved in and some which are
still being done include: serving alumni banquets; chaperoning student parties
in the club room; putting on carnivals, food sales and card parties to raise
money; entertaining basketball teams and their coaches; sponsoring free held
health clinics; serving punch and cookies and the Junior-Senior Prom; made
capes for band members; made a U.S. Flag for the City; hosted the first picnic
at the Linton Park for faculty and members and their families in 1938;
sponsored a boy at State Youth; did Red Cross knitting; provided financial aid
for a back-drop for the stage; with the help of the Commercial Club, achieved
the first skating ring, in 1943; helped organize PTA group; serving Soil
Conservation banquets.

The Civic Club started the City Library with donated books and books from the
Free Library at Pierre (these were kept 60 days and then returned for a new
supply). The club no longer gets books from Pierre but has a well stocked
library in the City Building of donated books.  The past two years, the City
has given the club $200.00 annually to purchase new books, so there is good
reading for young and old. The Library is open every Saturday, from 2:00 to
4:00 p.m. with a club member in charge. During the summer months, a reading
program for children is held.

Other activities have included sponsoring a band student to summer music camp,
entertaining exchange students from Finland, Brazil, Argentina and Greece, and
also sponsoring the Elk's Club Chorus one year.

The club observed its 25th anniversary in 1952 in the Legion Hall and is
looking forward to celebrating its 50th anniversary in 1977.

Through the years, donations have been made to Abbott House, Children's Home,
Elizabeth Kenny Fund, Red Cross, Christmas Seals, Community Fund, Iron Lung,
Crippled Children, Cancer Society, football supplies, band uniforms, playground
equipment for school and park, hospital, junior basketball, and Athletic Club.

This year, members worked on a cook book which is being sold to replenish their
bank account. 21 members belong to the club this year and meet in the School
Home Ec. Room.

Mary V. Brandner is the only active charter member. Rose Bickel, who joined in
1933, has the most continuous years as a club member.

There is a welcoming committee to greet newcomers to town and to invite the
ladies to join the club.

Present officers are: Mrs. LeRoy Sattler, president; Mrs. William Bommersbach,
vice president and Mrs. Ervin Meidinger, secretary-treasurer.

HERREID SENIOR CITIZENS

On April 23, 1974, the Herreid Jaycees took it upon themselves to call a
meeting at the Herreid Theatre for persons interested in organizing a Senior
Citizens organization.  Bruce Brandner, then president of the Jaycees, presided
at the meeting before a group of about sixty interested people attending.
Brandner explained that the Jaycees would cooperate in helping them organize.

Speakers Tony Welder and Ervin Njas of the Mobridge Senior Citizens were
secured to explain their procedures in attaining help.  The Jaycees then
proceeded to appoint a committee of four to act as a planning committee,
namely: Harold Fetherhuff, Matt Seiler, Ramona Werner and Ann Schaefbauer.

A trip to Pierre was made to get information. This proved unsuccessful so the
committee decided to try to get donations from business places, organizations
and individuals and get started on their own program. A good response proved
successful. Surrounding Senior Citizens Centers were visited to secure ideas
which proved helpful in getting started. After considering different locations
the present site seemed most feasible. Inquiries were made and it was found the
building could be secured.  The planning committee then contacted the County
Commissioners who consented to allow the cost of the building through revenue
sharing funds. The building south of the Picayune, formerly used by the SCS,
was purchased.

The first project was remodeling the building and afterwards furnishings were
acquired, some of which were donated.

The first meeting was held in June when States Attorney C. W. Renz presented
the articles of incorporation and by-laws. Directors elected were: Harold
Fetherhuff, Matt Seiler, Ramona Werner, Ann Schaefbauer, Bill Block, Herman
Weisbeck, Conrad Renz, Rose Lang, Ella Perman, Emanuel Deibert and John
Weisbeck. First officers elected were: Herman Weisbeck, president; Matt Seiler,
vice pres.; Harold Fetherhuff, secretary and Ramona Werner, treasurer.

On September 22, 1974, the grand opening of the newly remodeled Center was
held. The building would not hold the over four hundred people attending and
the program was moved to the Herreid School auditorium. Various Senior Citizen
groups participated in the program, emceed by Harold Fetherhuff.

Herreid Senior Citizens boasted a membership of one hundred their first year.

Meetings are held once a week with business meetings once a month. The building
is used daily for those who like to play cards, shoot pool or just gather to
visit and have a cup of coffee.  Monthly birthdays and special occasions are
observed with a pot luck dinner with birthday people and special guests seated
at decorated tables centered with birthday cakes. Slides are shown and various
speakers and other entertainment are secured when they are available.  Bingo is
also enjoyed by all.

1975 proved to be a good year for the Center when the entire floor was
carpeted, central air conditioning was installed, new drapes were hung and the
old refrigerator and stove were replaced with new ones.

The Center has also proved useful for various occasions such as family
reunions, anniversaries and meetings.

All in all, the Herreid Senior Citizens are proud of their accomplishments and
take this means of thanking the Herreid Jaycees who sponsored and made this all
possible. They welcome membership of anyone 55 years of age or older.

                BUSINESSES - PAST AND PRESENT

HUBER & SON

The farm machinery business originally began in the City of Herreid in 1903.
The original owners were Quenzer and Rieker, with a John Deere business. The
firm was located in a small building just west of the livery barn.

In 1917 the business changed hands and was owned by Ackerman and Quenzer, with
Buckenberger as bookkeeper. It was during this period of ownership that the new
implement shop was built a block west of main street.

In 1914 this implement business changed hands, and was owned by Quenzer and
Philip P. Huber. At that time Andrew Huber also worked there as bookkeeper.

In 1918 the business became known as Huber & Son, and that was also the year
that the business received its first International Harvester contract. The
business had many ups and downs during the coming year. It is one of only four
businesses in the City of Herreid that survived the Bank Crash of the twenties,
and also the drought years of the 1930s.  In the late 1930s the business became
a sole proprietorship, owned by Andrew Huber.

In 1946, Wilbert Huber joined the business after returning from the armed
services. Now the business was again Huber and Son.  This partnership remained
in effect until 1962 when Wilbert Huber bought out his father, Andrew Huber,
and became sole owner of the business.

In 1952 the Huber & Son business built a new base of operations, which is now
located on the south end of the City, on Highway 83.

The Huber & Son IHC business is still being operated as such. It is considered
by the International Harvester Company to be one of the oldest family owned IHC
contracts in the United States. The contract with IHC has now been running for
60 years.

HERREID REALTY

Herreid Realty was organized the 6th of March, 1948, at Herreid, by 21 members.
First officers of the organization were: John K. Wiest, Matt Seiler, Louie
Stellflug, Jacob G. Hofer, and Edwin Bollinger. The group incorporated on
February 6, 1948.

The first complete meeting of all the stockholders was held March 6, 1948. At
that meeting the by-laws of the organization were established.

At the April 6, 1948 meeting, contractor I. T. Rische was given authority to go
ahead with the construction of the new Bank building. This proved to be quite a
difficult project, as it was a period following World War II and materials were
very short in supply. The bond for the new organization was also secured at that
meeting.

The new building was rented to the Campbell County Bank on July 12, 1949. The
second portion of the Bank building was leased to Henry Ochsner Jan. 17, 1950
for the purpose of operating a clothing store.

On June 4, 1960, a stockholders meeting was held for the purpose of buying a
building and lot in Pollock, to be used in starting a branch of the Campbell
County Bank, in Pollock. The building was remodeled for a banking operation and
leased to Campbell County Bank on June 4, 1960.

The Herreid Realty organization is still operating with the original 25 shares,
but due to the deaths of some of the original shareholders, much of the stock
has been divided into smaller shares, now giving the Realty Company about 60
shareholders.

The organization's present directors are: Wilbert Huber, president; Herman
Klaudt, vice president; Louie Stellflug, secretary; Matt Seiler, treasurer and
Edward Beck, member at large.

SCHAEFBAUER GROCERY

Edward Dickmann and L. H. Damberger built a new grocery store after the fire of
1910 and in 1913 Dickmann bought out Damberger. Henry Ackerman, A. K. Deibert
and Gottlieb Heinrich purchased the business from Dickmann in June of 1918. In
December of 1919 Ackerman bought out Heinrich and the business operated under
that ownership until 1922 when Ackerman bought out the Deibert interest and was
joined by his son. R. H. Ackerman.

In 1925 Ackerman & Son sold to Boschker & Newton and in 1928 Fred Heinrich
purchased the store. The store closed during the depression, for several years,
and Heinrich re-opened it and ran it for several more years until its closing
in 1939. R. H. Ackerman bought the building in 1943, operating the store until
1965 when he sold out to his son, Maynard, who ran the store for two years
before selling to Larry Martin in 1967. Martin operated the business for two
years before R. H. Ackerman again took over its operation. In October of 1970,
Ackerman sold the business to Eugene Schaefbauer, who has operated the store
since that time.

In 1976, Schaefbauer purchased the building which housed the Herreid school
buses and kindergarten classroom and began remodeling the facility in
preparation for the move to its new location. At the time of the printing of
this book, the building was rapidly nearing remodeling completion.

RIEKER ELECTRIC

Rieker Electric began business in Herreid in September of 1947. Melvin Rieker
opened the electric firm in the basement of the former Farmers State Bank
building on Herreid's west main street.

Rieker Electric installed a 40 ft. antenna atop its building in May of 1953 to
pick up KSTP, Minneapolis, some 400 miles off. At the time KSTP was the nearest
television station.

The business later moved to the ground level floor of the bank building and in
1959 the present building was purchased and the move was made to its present
location.

HERREID CLOTHING STORE

Ochsner Dept. Store, operated by Henry P. Ochsner, opened a clothing store in
the building north of the Campbell County Bank, in January of 1950.  The

from Wenzel's and have operated it since that time.

HERREID EQUITY EXCHANGE

A group of local farmers banded together to purchase and organize the Herreid
Equity Exchange in 1916 and 1917. The first elevator was purchased from Fred
Bickel for $6,000.00 and is still in use at the present time.

The first Board of Directors were: Frank Hellhake, chairman; John Rossow, Sr.,
secretary; Charles Rossow, Sr., John Aldinger, Christian Salzseidler, Gust
Fenske, Adolph Fenske and Martin Winterberg.

Christ Bieber was the first manager of the association, hired for a salary of
$125.00 per month.

The Herreid Equity Exchange, with faithful patronage from its members, grew to
fulfill its patrons' needs.

Following are highlights of the Equity's years of operation:

1920 - Acquired its first storage warehouse.

1938 - Purchased the Sterling Oil Co.

1938 - Purchased what was known as the Olsen Elevator which was used for many
years and torn down in 1975.

1949 - Service station was built.

1954 - 100,000 bushel annex was built.

1958 - 200,000 bushel concrete elevator was built.

1963 - New offices and warehouse were built.

1975 - Bunge Elevator (formerly Perman Elevator) with its bins and quonsets
were purchased.

The Herreid Equity Exchange has been a thriving and successful enterprise. It
is now a cooperative of 525 stockholders, the majority of which are farmers in
the Herreid area.  Assets total $807,337.38 and the firm is engaged in buying
and selling agriculture products such as grain, feed, fertilizer, and petroleum
products.

The cooperative, being one of the largest businesses in Campbell County, pays
an annual property tax of approximately $17,000.00. 1975 saw sales of
$2,927,937.00, with nine full time employees on the payroll.

Managers of the cooperative were: Christ Bieber, a Mr. Greve, H. J. Edinger,
Frank Brandner, John J. Rieker, Hugo F. Lippelt and Delmer Droog.  One of the
longest managers was John J. Rieker who was hired as a bookkeeper in 1925 and
took over as manager in 1929, a position he held until retiring in 1961.

Chairmen of the Board of Directors have been: Frank Hellhake, Christ
Salzsiedler, Charles Rossow, Wm. Hoffman, John P. Huber, George Ritter, Jacob
Schmidt, John F. Rossow, and presently Eugene H. Wiest.

Each one of the present stockholders should indeed be grateful to those first
members who had the foresight to organize this association. A business where
they could buy and sell through a farmer-owned association. Since its
organization, many thousands of dollars in profits were returned to Equity
patrons, in patronage dividends.

HOSPITAL, CLINIC AND GOOD SAMARITAN CENTER

Herreid voters decided July 1, 1963 to construct a 16-bed hospital in Herreid.
The vote was 372 for issuing of bonds and 27 against.

Edward Quaschnick donated a three acre site on the north edge of Herreid for
the hospital site and a full block (300 ft. x 300 ft.) was held in reserve for
a rest home, with an option to buy that parcel.

Holzwarth Bros., Inc., of Mobridge were awarded the contract with a bid of
$126,559, with additions totaling $129,559.  Bid letting was held August 30,
1963.

An attorney general's ruling stating that the construction of a hospital comes
under the 5 percent debt limit, required that another election be held.  The
second election, held October 7, 1963 also passed by a vast majority but with
fewer voters turning out.

Dr. Bedrettin Gunc, M. D., Selby, opened an office at the Huber Motel, on
Monday, Oct. 14, coming to Herreid five days a week.

A five member board was appointed by the City Council to determine what
equipment would be needed and to seek personnel for the hospital. The board
consisted of Mrs. G. Martinez Senftner, John Riedlinger, Mrs. Melvin Rieker,
Matt Seiler, and Henry B. Bickel.

The hospital officially opened on Tuesday, Sept. 22, 1964, with Dr. Gunc as
chief of staff. Dr. M. Sabbagh and Dr. R. Berzins were also on the staff and
Mrs. Bernice Silen, R. N., was administrator. Other personnel included Mrs.
Morris Jacobson, head nurse, and Mrs. Johnnie Walker, secretary.

Mrs. Wilbert Huber, Herreid, was the first patient admitted to the hospital and
Sherri Lynn, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Wilfred Kiefer of Zeeland, N. D., was the
first baby born at the hospital.

Dedication of the City Hospital was held Sunday, October 18 with Wm. Murphy,
president of the S. D. Hospital Association as main speaker.

Along with the City Hospital, another business was added to the list of Herreid
establishments. Denis Svedjan, Registered Pharmacist, opened a drug store on
main street and was operational in December of 1964. The last druggist in
Herreid, prior to that time, had been C. H. Blair who left in the 1930s.

In February of 1967 the first set of twins were born at the hospital. Kenton
Gary and Keven Lee, sons of Rev. and Mrs. Vergil Moos, were born Feb. 15.

Dr. Tacettin Turedi, came to Herreid in January of 1967 and in August was named
to the hospital staff, as a heart specialist.

1967 was a busy year for the hospital with many days of 100 percent plus
occupancy. It was also considered, at one point, that an addition should be

In March of 1968, Dr. Gunc resigned and the hospital closed March 15. Dr.

With the hospital opening and closing so often, the influence was felt by the
drug store and that business closed in July of 1969.

Following many attempts to secure a doctor, the hospital was put up for sale on
bids. The hospital, even when open in its later years, had lost money and bonds
were not yet paid off.

The Evangelical Lutheran Good Samaritan Society was the successful bidder on
the hospital building. Some hospital equipment was moved to the City building
next to the theatre where a clinic was set up. A clinic had been operated in
the hospital following the closing, with Dr. Roland Fleck and Dr. Walter
Peschel, both of Ashley, N. D., operating the clinic. On July 16, Dr. Fleck and
Medex Ray Tangwall began serving the clinic and presently are here two days
each week.

THE PRAIRIE PICAYUNE

The Prairie Picayune was started in Mound City, in 1888. Volume 1, No. 1 made
its appearance July 25, with F. E. Hazen as editor and publisher.

On November 7, 1888, the paper changed hands, Hazen having sold to John Kipp
and J. Schafer.  A. L. Arneson purchased Schafer's interest in the Picayune in
August of 1889. Kipp and Arneson operated the business until February 17, 1890,
when Kipp bought out Arneson.

In September of 1890, R. M. Slocum bought the newspaper from Kipp. It was
during Slocum's ownership that the Picayune was moved from Mound City to
Herreid, in the year 1901.  In 1908, Slocum sold the publication to J. B.
Merscher, then the Soo Line depot agent. Merscher took over the reins and for
41 years was editor and publisher.

Type for the newspaper was set by hand when Merscher bought the paper and after
several years of operation, he installed a Linograph machine to handle the
typesetting.

Following Merscher's death, W. H. Jahraus purchased the publication and
operated it until June of 1969 when he sold out to the present publisher, Wm.
Bommersbach.

The Picayune has been published continuously since its start in 1888, making it
one of the oldest privately owned business establishments in the county.

The Picayune is pictured above as it looked until 1952 when it was moved to
make room for the new building.  The old Picayune building is still in use
today, serving as a workshop for Fred Bauer, local carpenter.

HERREID CONCRETE PLANT

Herreid Concrete Plant was started in the 1940s as a gravel pit where gravel
was removed with horse drawn wagons and loaded with shovels.

In 1948, Frank Ritt built the present building with solid concrete block
manufactured in a wooden form and cast as solid blocks. A block machine was
later installed and loaders were used to load gravel. Culverts were also
manufactured.

In the early 1950s the management was taken over by Jacob Schmidt and the
business was then sold to Jake H. Fuehrer and Art Orth.

In January of 1959, Curtis Kost purchased the interests held by Mr. Orth. In
the early 1960 additions were made to remove some of the manual labor and do
the work by machines. Ready-mix concrete was added, rubber tired loaders, fork
lifts and boom trucks were also put into use.

In 1966, Jake Fuehrer sold his interest to Morrell Fuehrer.

Today a varied line of concrete products are available, including blocks, ready
mixed concrete, culverts, septic tanks, feed bunks and bunker silos.

Co-owners Kost and Fuehrer have also established a ready mix plant in Eureka to
help serve that area,

THE OWL BUFFET

One of Herreid's early businesses was the Owl Buffet which was owned and
operated by William S. Siegele. Mr. Siegele opened the liquor business in 1907,
after operating and working in grain & meat market businesses in Eureka and
Webster, S. D. and Zeeland, N. D., and teaching a country school in southeast
Campbell County.

The first location of the business was north of Lang's harness shop on west
Main Street, and was later moved a block north, where the business burned in
the 1910 fire. Siegele re-opened the business in a new brick building he built
in the 1911-12 period on the west side of Main Street, and remained in business
at that location until he retired in 1938.

He was a member of the Herreid City Band and was one of the six family heads
who formed Peace Lutheran Church.

The Owl Buffet was known locally for its fine collection of stuffed native
animals and birds, and fine Wisconsin cheeses and meats.

BICKEL FAMILY DAIRY

A small dairy business was established by Mrs. Martin Bickel and children,
Minnie, Esther, Ruth and Henry, in 1928, on the southeast edge of Herreid. The
dairy herd was just four cows, which were milked by hand, after which the milk
was strained and cooled in cold water, bottled and delivered.

A new phase of selling the milk was used at that time, as up to then milk was
sold in buckets, to the customers.  The Bickel Dairy sold the milk in glass
milk bottles with paper caps.  These came in quart and pint sizes, as small
families would buy a pint of milk daily but larger families did buy a quart,
and the biggest sale went to the local cafe that would buy as much as three
quarts a day.

There was a morning and evening route; the morning route usually was on the way
to school, delivered by the children, carrying bottles in wire racks from house
to house. The milk sold for 10 cents a quart and collection was made the last
of the month. The business grossed less than $50 a month, which fed and clothed
a family of five.

ROBBINS FUNERAL HOME

Charles Orville Robbins, came to Herreid in 1909 and opened a jewelry store and
watch repair business on the west side of Main Street which burned out in the
1910 fire. He re-opened the business on the east side of Main Street in the
Alker building north of the old West Hotel and added a furniture store to the
business.

In 1914 he bought the Schaap building (later the Ford garage), on the east side
of Main Street and went into business as a funeral director and furniture store
operator.  Mr. Robbins graduated from the Eckel's School of Embalming in
Minneapolis in 1916.

With the advent of silent films in the 1920s, Robbins and Henry Benz formed a
partnership and opened Herreid's first theatre in the Schaap building which
they operated for several years.

In the 1920s Robbins sold the Schaap building and later built the present
funeral home still operated as Kesling's Funeral Home in Herreid. A pioneer
funeral director who started with horse drawn hearses, Robbins operated the
business at that location until his death.

An accomplished musician, he was active in bands and orchestras, and was the
band director who started the Herreid High School Band in the 1931-32 period
and the Pollock High School Band later in the 1930s.

                HERREID SCHOOL

It was in the late fall of 1901 when the Soo Line Railroad laid its rails into
Campbell County, and following the Spring Creek with its roadbed, they soon
reached what came to be the townsite of Herreid. Already in the early eighties
pioneer settlers had reached this area and homesteaded along the beautiful
valley of the little stream whose valley made a broad expanse here in its
winding course to the Missouri River.

As the townsite of Herreid was laid out and platted, the Vienna school house
located here came within its limits, and of course the children of the town
residents attended here for the first season.

As the town arose the name of the school district was changed to Herreid School
District. In the summer of 1902 the people realized the need for a larger and
better school building, and promptly built one that year. The structure was a
two room affair and was at that time, considered a modern building and looked
upon with pride as a step forward in progress. But as each of the rooms in the
building were larger than the room in the old discarded building, only one of
the rooms was finished and used the first year. The upper room was left
unfinished and used for a sort of public gathering place of the new town.  Mr.
W. A. Bentley was the first teacher in the newly erected school. Mr. Bentley
continued in educational work in the county and state nearly 36 more years
thereafter, teaching and serving two terms as County Superintendent of Campbell
County from 1915 to 1919.

In 1903 the population of the town had grown larger, the extra room had to be
finished and a second teacher was added. We now had a teaching corps of two,
which was considered to be quite an up-to-date modern school.  However the town
soon outgrew this and more room had to be provided. A two story addition was
then built onto the north side of the old building. Of course another teacher
was added immediately and the following year a fourth faculty member was
needed. A few years later another addition was built on the south side.  This
provided room for six teachers, and served the purpose for many years.

Three members made up the board of education up to the year 1919 when the law
required a five man board. In the summer of 1914 the board was petitioned by 25
freeholders of the town to institute a high school department. Immediately they
set about to carry out the wishes of its citizens and engaged Sidney P. Morat
to head the school as principal and teach the following subjects: English I,
algebra, ancient history, botany and physical geography, all to be on the ninth
grade level.

Mr. Morat continued to teach the high school work the following year. In 1916
Gardner H. Watkins came to serve as superintendent of the new high school with
Miss Clara Van Beek and Miss Gladys Brakke as assistants. Now a full four year
curriculum was offered.  Supt. Watkins also took care of the janitorial duties
with some extra remuneration. During his second year he succeeded in bringing
the school into the state accredited list. In February of 1918 Watkins was
called to serve in the armed forces, and Miss Brakke was designated acting
principal for the remainder of the season. Dr. Richey served at that time as
clerk of the board, Rev. H. G. Bens was treasurer and William Quaschnick as
chairman.

In 1918 Herreid High School graduated the first class of seniors, consisting of
the following six members: John H. Blair, Alfred J. and Willie O. Olson,
Jeanette Van Heuvelen, Stelline Van Heuvelen, and Erwin Siegele. The next year
the graduates numbered only two and in 1921 none. Subsequent classes became
ever larger, and in 1943 the graduating class numbered 32.

        First Basketball Team

Almost from the inception of high school work, teachers and pupils saw the
value of physical education and set about almost immediately to organize sports
for boys and girls.  Basketball was played on an open outdoor court opposite
the school grounds next to the old Herreid Theatre. "Bill" Olsen, who was a
member of the first team organized, stated, "Basketball in those days was a
rugged sport, and played in daytime outdoors in all kinds of winter weather.
Often, prior to a game the snow had to be cleared from the court."
Nevertheless, the cultural aspects of education were not neglected, for records
reveal that elocution, debate and music became a part of the activities even in
the first years of high school.

Miss Clara Van Beek succeeded Supt. Watkins and stayed for one year.  Then A.
G. Savage headed the school from 1919 to 1921 and E. S. Olsen during the
1921-22 season. John D. Mossler followed and remained through 1925.

The old building was a frame structure and as it had reached the average life
of such buildings and began to look old and dilapidated in spite of the fact
repairs were made annually. It was in the month of July 1923, when the building
underwent its annual overhauling, that fate decided its destruction, while the
boiler was being tested out the building caught fire and was demolished. The
volunteer fire department answered the call promptly, and with the aid of
bystanders succeeded in removing most of the furniture and books from the
building. An early day camera fan seized upon the opportunity and quickly
snapped pictures of the conflagration at various stages. Stories prevail that
some of the citizens with an eye for civic improvement urged the fire
department to attack the fire rather cautiously. One of the writers of the time
penned, "In one way it was a blessing" to the community even though it worked a
temporary hardship upon the district. Incidentally, on April 12 of the same
year an election was held to approve a $33,000 bond issue for a new school
structure but was turned down by a vote of 99 to 133.

Now the board of education rented the Woodmen Hall, and six grades were put in
there, with three teachers. The rooms (divisions) were separated with curtains
which were hardly soundproof.  Several grades were housed in the City Hall,
small as it was. A large vacant house constituted the home of the high school
for a year.

A special election was called and a bond issue of $32,000 was voted for the
erection of a new school building.  Actually, it cost $52,000. The building was
ready for occupancy in September, 1924.

Frank C. Brummund, president of the board, and members Philip Silverman, A. K.
Deibert, Arnold Krause and Henry Ackerman, bore the burden of planning and
supervising the much-needed structure so that it would serve the educational
needs of the community.  With the exception of one year, Mr. Brummund served as
a member of the board from 1918 to 1933.

A publication of the time described the new school building in the following
tone, "Our present building, 62x124 feet, is modern in every way and is
entirely fireproof.  The first floor contains three grade rooms, boiler room
and spacious gymnasium. The gym is provided with a stage on one side and may be
used for programs, literary productions and entertainments of various sorts."

"The second floor" it continues, "has two grade rooms, one large high school
assembly, three recitation rooms and a laboratory. All rooms in use are
equipped with the Univent ventilating system, thus making it possible to let
fresh air into every room so gradually that the temperature is not affected in
the slightest degree. The building is steam heated and kept at a comfortable
temperature at all times while school is in session. Other conveniences in the
building, are shower baths, indoor toilets and drinking fountains."

Dave C. Leidle was engaged as the custodian for the new building and continued
in the position until his resignation in 1942.

Julius J. Elster succeeded John D. Mossler as superintendent in the fall of
1925 and remained at the helm until 1929. The new school building was in use
the second year and had a high school enrollment of 48 students with four
teachers making up the faculty. Not until the fall enrollment of 1930 was the
60 student mark reached.

With the completion of the new school building and its spacious gymnasium,
basketball moved indoors and naturally received a fresh impetus of player and
fan enthusiasm. With the arrival of Supt. Elster came principal Henry Goeken
who also served as Herreid's most illustrious coach. Basketball history was
written during his second season at Herreid. Even though the Herreid cagers were
comparatively newcomers at the District tournament, they astonished all
dopesters by eliminating Java, McLaughlin and Selby to enter the finals against
a much favored Mobridge aggregation, and lost only by a point margin in the
last 30 seconds of play. In the semifinals Selby was put away by a score of 19
to 1.

Goeken's teams continued as the best of the area, but wrote a second chapter of
basketball history the next year, 1928, when at the tournaments they eliminated
all competition at the district and regional, advancing to the state tournament
at Mitchell. Here the Goeken boys defeated Mitchell but went down before a
powerful Yankton team. It should be remembered that in those days the
tournaments included all Class A and Class B school of the state.

In the spring of 1926, track was added to the sports program and continued to
the present time with a temporary interruption during World War II. Girls'
basketball was later replaced by girls' physical education, and six man
football had its beginning in Herreid in the Fall of 1944. Spring baseball came
to be very popular during the war years when track was dropped.

K. H. Cressman followed Elster as Superintendent and held the position until
his unexpected death in 1933. H. L. Fetherhuff served as acting superintendent
the remainder of the season, and the next fall, 1933, E. G. Gloege was employed
as head of the school until 1938. Then A. E. Joachim came to head the school.

The depression of 1929 soon made itself felt in all school finances. In 1931
the assessed valuation of the Herreid school district stood at $493,000, while
by 1939 it had been lowered to $275,000.  Herreid school finances were put to a
hard test during the depression and the drought years to follow. In 1932 the
bonded indebtedness still stood at $28,000, and warrants had been registered
for operational expenses for some time. When R. B. White became treasurer of
the district, teachers had a hard time cashing their warrants, and they
returned to par value. In 1942 all bonded indebtedness was paid and the
district was entirely out of debt. Mr. White continued as treasurer until 1945
and Wilbert Huber then assumed those duties.

The younger generation did not concern themselves with matters of finance and
55 students were enrolled in high school, 17 of those were non-resident. The
following year 73 enrolled in high school and 124 in the grades. By 1935, 90
students were listed in high school, 41 of them were tuitions.

The highest grade enrollment of that period was reached in the 1939-40 school
year when 130 registered. In the fall of 1938 the high school registration
exceeded the 100 mark for the first time with 102 enrolled, 56 of those were
non-resident students. The peak high school enrollment was reached in 1942 when
132 students registered, 77 being non-resident.

Members of the boards of education ever visioned the need of educational growth
and progress. During the thirties numerous federal projects were set up to
improve the school plant. First the grounds were landscaped, the low places
filled in, trees planted and water channels curbed with stone masonry.  Later
the lots south of the school block were acquired, mainly through the suggestion
of E. E. Clapp, then president of the board.

In the late thirties and early forties a federal N. Y. A. project was
instituted at Herreid with I. T. Rische as supervisor. Rische was also a member
of the board at the time. He served some 18 years before he retired in 1949.
With N. Y. A. labor, numerous improvements were made within the school
building.  Considerable furniture and things were made at the shop including
many folding chairs, tables and cupboards. A sidewalk was laid along the school
property all the way to the printing office lots.

Ever increasing attendance necessitated expansion of the curricular program.  A
fifth teacher was added to high school in l910 and with the addition of manual
arts in 1950 and the veterans on-the-farm program, a sixth and seventh teacher
were needed.

The first literary publication edited by the students and faculty appeared in
1917. It was printed monthly for a season under the editorship of John H. Blair
and was called "The Sirius." Records do not disclose that it appeared the next
year. In 1921 another monthly news bulletin was printed under the name "The
Enthusiast." Five years later, after the completion of the new school building
a bulletin was issued giving detailed information about the new school, its
facilities and the courses offered. This was for distribution to new and
prospective students.

In the early thirties, "The Yellow-jacket" took form and has been published by
the students under faculty supervision nearly continuously.

Music has always played a leading part in the community life of Herreid.  In
the early twenties the town boasted a fine city band composed of the leading
musicians of the area. But it was not until 1929 that Oliver R. Dahl was
employed to teach instrumental music in the public school.  During his two
years in the system he laid the ground work for a school band. In 1931 C. O.
Robbins, a local musician, was engaged to take over this work and immediately
set about to organize the players into a school band. From that time on to the
present, the school has never been without a band. Almost at the outset Robbins
led the band of 16 members to a first place win at the Hickory Stick Music
contest at Mobridge in 1933.

From 1933 to 1938, Superintendent Gloege taught and directed the school hand,
bringing it to still higher levels of perfection, and during the five years
received three district superior ratings in Class C competition. Following
Gloege, A. E. Joachim assumed directorship of the band.  Another director who
helped keep the tradition of the prize-winning band was Joe Thompson. The
present director is Delroy Jordahl.

Added to the athletic activities were both track and football, in the early
1940's.

Although a well-rounded academic program had been developed, the community felt
that there was a need for industrial arts. In 1951 a shop was added and the
program has been offered since then.

The home economics department was added during the 1954-55 school year. To
provide space, a room was added above what was known as the old boiler room.

A hot lunch program which conforms to government standards was added in 1957.
It is now served in the area which was the gymnasium of the original school.

Because of reorganization through-out the state, the enrollment continued to
rise. More space was needed and in 1958 an addition was begun. This addition
which was put into use during the 1958-59 school year included an auditorium
with dressing rooms, a science room, three class rooms and a furnace room.

Also, due to reorganization, buses were needed and began operation to bring in
students from the rural area. The school year 1961-62 found them fully
operational.

Special Education classes were held in 1958-59. These were discontinued and a
remedial reading program was begun in 1971-72 and is functioning at present.

Kindergarten was begun in 1966-67 and is being continued at the present.

A guidance program was begun in 1969-70. It too, still offers its services to
the students.
