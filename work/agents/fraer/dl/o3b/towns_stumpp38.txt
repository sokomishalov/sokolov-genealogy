Karl Stumpp:  Original Homeland and Emigration of Germans to Bessarabia.(1)

If one wants to research emigration and homeland data, there are two options to
proceed with retrieving information.

The first option requires searching in Germany through the archives, church
records, etc. as to who emigrated from these certain towns and to where.  In
this type of research one finds only where they emigrated to without more
specific mention of towns. In this case it is extremely difficult, if not
impossible, to find the emigrants' original homeland and to tie them back to
their people.

The second option entails beginning with  the origin of emigration. Through the
course of research,  one will find that in these German villages, which were
founded a hundred or two hundred years before,  the land of origin and sometimes
the towns are indicated in their personnel data and church records.(2) Which
ever route is taken, you would have  to follow one or the other completely.  To
research the origin of homeland , undoubtedly the information in the German
villages they emigrated to, is of more value and leads more surely and quickly
to success.

We do not totally need to get involved with statistics and numbers, as worthy
and interesting as  they may appear, nor do we need to know, that in some cases
in Wuerttemberg, as many people emigrated out of towns as lived in those towns.
The main goal is to establish a connection between the emigrant Germans and
their tie to the homeland... to build bridges and to connect the bloodlines.
With that in mind, the work should be concentrated  in that area. (This finding
is a result of several years of research.)  I started this in 1930 while working
as a professor in Bessarabia.  Many have helped me and I want to thank all at
this point.

The data was extracted from church and personnel records, which the original
communities (mother colonies) kept and are marked with a star in the index of
communities.  From these 24 mother colonies derived 119 daughter colonies.
Among them, 42 poor share-cropping communities are marked by two stars.   Data
is missing from the colony Schabo(3), which consisted mainly of Swedes, a lot of
which still speak French today, and from the Catholic colony Krassna.  There is
not much to say about the colony Krassna from the original homeland.   An old
farmer indicated that the inhabitants of the village Kehl could have come from
Baden(hausen?).  Perhaps the family names like Erkwein, Utz, Obetwein, Heineke,
Hoppe, Grams, Novak, Schreiner, Mirsprecher, Seiwelt, and Schindler may give a
focal point.  The polish names like Jankowsky and Wotowski point to a Polish
midway location.

The index is set up as Family name, First name, Birthdate, Town and Country of
origin, Village of Emigration and Year.  Where there is no emigration year noted
it is usually that the year of immigration is that same as the year the colony
was founded.  I have sent copies of indexes to the appropriate governments to
correct the spelling of towns names (Prussia, Baden and Bavaria).  The overseer
for the town names of Wuerttemberg was Dr. Spaeth from the Research Center of
Schwabians in Foreign Countries.  The difficult correction of spelling of the
Polish towns were done by Dr. Hans Hopf and Director Breyer-Sompolna.  I want to
thank these gentlemen here.

I am aware that this work contains mistakes, because the entries were done
during the settlement by the writers through spoken affidavits and handwriting.
The data from towns in Poland, Prussia and Hungary are especially full of
mistakes and inaccurate, as well.  It will be a hard task to determine by the
church records of Poland and Hungary from which towns or parts of the country
the pertaining families emigrated.  That task will require a lot of time.
Possibly, one could be able to prove whether or not the available data is
correct.   I believe that the publication of these results should not be held up
any longer, in consideration of  the Bessarabian Germans to whom this work is
dedicated.  It will be the responsibility of  the individual families to
research their country of origin.  The relevant authorities will surely assist
them,  especially the Hauptstelle fuer die Sippenkunde of the Germans in foreign
countries.  Many entries do not indicate a town and, where there is only mention
of the country, it could be impossible to find the home of origin.  At any rate,
this is the first job which embraces a closed German settlement as complete as
can be.  I just hope that my people from Bessarabia will put this publication to
good use.  Since there is a family connection between the Germans from Russia
and Bessarabians, this work will also be of use to the Germans from Russia.
This is much more important since there may not be another chance for such
research in Russia because a lot of important data was destroyed by the
Bolschewiks.
