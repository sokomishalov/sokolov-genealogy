GNADENFELD MOLOTSCHNA

as translated by Mary Lou Garbin March 1998
Coordinated with GRHS Village Research Clearing House
Coordinated with GRHS Translation Committee Chairman

Gnadenfeld )1)

In order to escape the frightful oppression of a Polish Nobleman, a number of
Mennonite families in 1765 moved from the area of Schwetz in West Prussia, which
was at that time Polish, into a moorlike region covered with meadow shrubbery on
the right bank of the Netze (river) in the present province of Brandenburg and
founded - with superior privileges granted by King Friedrich II under the
leadership of the Royal Privy Council Franz von Brikenhof )2) - the two colonies
named for him Franzthal and Brinkenhofswalde and a third, named Neudessau.
Initially battling in this swamp with indescribable hardships, the inhabitants
finally achieved through untiring industry, crowned by God's blessing, a truly
comfortable and well being life. They would not have left their accustomed and
well-earned situation, if King Friedrich Wilhelm II (though it was difficult for
him to go against his own word) had not seen it politically necessary to
partially remove their privileges, so they could inherit more land )4). Then the
congregation cast it's eyes towards southern Russia - where, on the banks of the
Molotschna, thousands of faith-brothers had found innumerable examples of
protection and kindness under the septer of the Emperor - and chose exodus.

Believing that nothing prevented immigration, they sold most of their land in
1833 and prepared to travel. Their shock was  great when the general counsel of
the Russian Empire in Danzig denied their request to immigrate. )4). Then the
spiritual representatives turned on behalf of and in the name of the
congregation to his majesty the Emperor with the request, for 40 families to be
most mercifully allowed to immigrate. Soon the permission under the legal
stipulations lay in their hands. )5).

In two groups, one under the leadership of church elder Wilhelm Lange, the other
led by a church teacher, the train was set in motion. As much as possible while
underway, they did not forget that they traveled as a Christian congregation.

Daily, before the departure, a short morning blessing, and on Sundays a prayer
hour was held. Without remarkable incident the groups arrived in the Molotschna
at the beginning of autumn, and each family sought a suitable habitation for
winter.

However, this cost much money because food was tremendously expensive due to the
previous year's extremely poor harvest.  Autumn and winter were spent finding
and securing a suitable place to settle. They finally decided upon (a spot) high
up on the steppe, where the first traces of the Apanlle steppe river valley were
noticeable; where, however, no shrubbery, or stone quarries existed, nor any
other peculiarity of land is present.

On January 10, 1834, the congregation received the report that their immigration
was permitted under the following stipulations:
1. display of a consent to emigrate from the Prussian government,
2. Immigration of families consisting of at least 5 persons,
3. deposit of a sum of 800 rubles, to be returned at the settlement location,
cf. Mennonite Lexicon V.1, pg. 263

In 1835, the number of 40 families was no longer attainable, some Lutheran
families (Klage, Lenzmann, Klatt) were accepted by way of baptism on confession
of faith (cf. Friesen a.a.O. pg. 80)  to found a colony of 40 landlord, )6) 4
craftsmen - and 30 renter sites.

Settlement took place in 1835, with the exception of some building sites,
settled in 1836, and three which were first occupied in 1840.

The colony consists of an almost 1-werst-long rectangle, whose long sides face
south and north and whose ends, east and west. Each of the two rows of houses
contains 20 (farmstead) sites, across from which are 40 renter sites.

Along both sides, between the farmsteads and renter sites, runs a wide street
heavily traveled in autumn, because the transportation of grain to the 70 werst
distant district and port city of Berdjansk, by many colonists and Mennonite
villages was routed through here )7).

The relatively large gardens of the householders would join together between the
two rows of the household sites, if a four Faden-wide grass-covered foot path
(named) the "Kirchensteig" (church path), lined both sides by forest trees, did
not separate them.

The so-called Mittelgasse [Middleroad] traverses the middle of the colony, on
whose sides, [and] upon the Kirchensteig [Churchpath]; stands the school to the
east; and, to the west, [the spot] where the church should appear. It would be
completed (??) if many unexpected obstacles had not been placed in our way.

The "Mittelgasse" [Middleroad], which goes in a straight line northwards towards
the churchyard, divides the colony (along with church path) into equal quarters.
Behind the renter sites runs on both long sides the planted woods, - and one may
well boldly assert that in regard to the woods very few colonies can compare
with Gnadenfeld.

'Gnadenfeld'! This name was given to the settlement by the leaders of the
congregation for the following reasons. 'Feld' (field), because it was situated
not in a valley, but high on the steppe, that is in a field. 'Gnaden' (grace),
as a remembrance of the Emperors grace, which in spite of the denied
immigration, afterward readily granted to the congregation the requested
permission, and also, because they were imbued with the fervent wish that they
would receive a large measure of God's grace on this spot. The congregation
readily agreed to the name.

The colony had, until then, been able to celebrate God's blessings, because
although they received no support from the Crown and many immigrants had little
means (because cattle, household items, farm implements etc. had to be sold at
ridiculously low prices before they emigrated, the trip and the first winter, as
well as the settlement itself meant significant costs) . . . one still has to
say that with each year, the situation improved. From the fruitful and (if God
does not withhold his blessing) very productive soil, we have usually good, and
sometimes very good harvests, which increase prosperity and result in the
construction of houses of more pleasing appearance and farm buildings which are
more complete and more functional.

Many gardens are (in spite of the relatively young settlement) already fully
planted, and several farmstead areas in the forest plantation, which was started
2 years ago, are fully occupied and surrounded by hedges.

Up to the present, God's grace has protected us from disease, fire and other
special evils, and we hope, under this protection and umbrella of our dear
authority, to continue to lead a peaceful and quiet life in Gnadenfeld, April
30,1848.

Mayor: Voth.
committee members: Jantzen, Goerz

gave their agreement, cf. 11 PSZ Vol. 7, no. 5684, October 18, 1832.
