                 Wetonka - Little Town on a Railroad

Under the guidance of a resident lawyer, Charles W. Herried, *McPherson
County had been organized in 1884 with Leola as county seat. The town
courageously rebuilt after the destructive fire of 1889, but could not
convince railroaders to build a line to it.

Charles Herried served as McPherson County's first register of deeds. In
1901 he became the governor of the state. Always friendly to the Leola
community, it's possible he helped focus attention on the plight of the
farmers without shipping facilities because by 1906 a spur line track was
being built from Aberdeen to Leola. Also at this time the idea of railroad
builder, James Hill, to add branch lines into settled areas with marketable
produce (or into locations with potential resources) had proved beneficial to
the established east-west transcontinental systems. Previously they had been
pulling too many empty cars on their eastern trips.

Eureka in the western part of McPherson County had grown as the terminal
of a Milwaukee spur line completed in 1887. Westport, fifteen miles northeast
of the new townsite, had rail connections with Aberdeen even earlier. Our
grandparents brought their household goods and farm animals in immigrant cars
from Illinois to their new farmlands near Westport in 1883 and 1886.

*McPherson County was named for Brig. Gen. James B. McPherson, a Union Officer
killed during the sedge of Atlanta, p. 9, 1969 McPherson County Atlas, History
Section.

The prosperity of Eureka had exceeded the expectations of the Chicago,
Milwaukee and St. Paul Railroad which established it. **By 1892 this wheat
market included forty-two grain buying firms and required the services of two
hundred men to handle more than three million bushels of sacked grain. The
railroad station showed earnings of $100,000 a month For that year. This was
said to he greater than that of any other station of the Milwaukee system of
the time. It is understandable that other railroad companies knowing of the
area's record wheat production would seek to share in the profits of marketing
it.

**The organization which belatedly began construction of a line between
Aberdeen and Leola in 1905 was designated in some early papers as The
Minnesota, Dakota and Pacific Railroad but was part of the Minneapolis and St.
Louis system later. The terrain offered few obstacles for grade building so
the construction of the thirty mile spur proceeded rapidly. The first train
reached Leola on Christmas Day of 1906. Enough space had not been left at the
ends of rails so with expansion in the heat of the following summer
adjustments were needed.

Based on the horse powered transportation of the farms, railroad towns at
intervals of ten miles seemed proper to planners for trading centers. Two
sites between Aberdeen and Leola were selected. The first became Richmond and
second Wetonka. Too near the large town of Aberdeen, the first station was not
expected to become much more than a shipping center for grain and a market for
coal. One grain elevator was built there.

The railroad builders obtained land for their right-of-way and railroad
yards but the land for the second town was acquired indirectly by Floete, Cory
and Bemis Company who drew plans for a village sold lots, and established
their own business there

The land had been George and Matilda Kernon's claim for which they held a
"patent" from the United States dated May 9th, 1905. Described as the S. E.
1/4 of Section 22, Township 125, Range 66, it was their homestead of 160
acres. A few months after receiving title to it the Kirnan's sold the land to
William Henry Colyer of Whiteside County, Illinois, for $1,200. Colyer and his
wife, Lucy, sold it after five months to C. C. Bender for $2,000. Mr. Bender
evidently bought it for the Floete Company because he and his wife, Georgia,
later deeded it to the firm for the same price. In some old records Mr. Bender
is named as secretary of the Floete Corporation of Clay County, Iowa.*

*Old plots of the town show a neatly laid out village northeast of the
tracks. The railroad right-of-way included the land fifty feet on either side
of the centerline of the track as it entered the townsite but the railroad
yard within the town area extends one hundred fifty feet on either side. The
commercial lots along Main Street are twenty five by one hundred forty-two and
the home sites fifty feet by one hundred forty-two. Eighty feet is given as
the width of Main Street and sixty for the streets in the residential area. An
addition to the plotted district must have been required almost immediately
because the school, the first church and several homes were soon built north
of Moulton Avenue, the north extremity of the layout. The grain elevators,
coal sheds and stockyards were necessarily built adjacent to the siding, or
switch track as we called it, to facilitate loading and unloading directly
from box cars. The depot was centrally located between the main track and the
siding at the west end of Main Street. No provision was made for an engine to
turn around in the new towns so it must go on to Leola and use the "Y" in
order to pull the cars back to Aberdeen. Of course, the usual run included
Leola and additional "Y's" would have been little used. No water tank was
needed in Wetonka or Richmond but the railroad had its own artesian well.

Several local farmers were attracted by the opportunities almost certain
in the "brand new" settlement. Our parents, Ed and Arline Friel, had sold
their Westport general store only the year before and bought a farm seven
miles to the west. They had invested in an artesian well for the new home but
the actual construction of the long sought railroad only five miles away was
too exciting! And it touched their lives: Dad was postmaster at this Bern Post
Office and farm, and the mail service to the farm would be discontinued! He
had been appointed postmaster for the new town, so why not accept and move
with the mail? Bern would be quite dull with no stages stopping at noon from
Leola and Aberdeen, and no neighbors coming for mail or with butter and eggs
to trade for items of the supplies we kept.

It must have been a hard choice to make because Bern which had been
developed by a Von Eschen family from Switzerland included a large garden area
entirely surrounded by trees and shrubs--some bearing fruit. The attic of the
house delighted kids. It still held the product of the senior Von Eschen's
hobby--bird cage building. Some of the cages had been too large to remove
through the small attic door. Our nine-year-old brother, Osie, made use of the
cages but the occupant I remember was an unhappy gopher. Probably our Dad's
fondness for people influenced his decision to move, but he kept the farm for
several years.

He had been teaching the nearby Murray School, so called at the time
because the Charles Murray family of boys furnished a good share of the
pupils. This was Carlisle District of Brown County. In March Dad gave up
teaching and started his contribution to the new town. Each morning he and a
carpenter or two from the area, George Esser and/or Hans Harkeson, left for
the townsite where they built a combination structure for family living and
for operating a small store with the post office. It must have been a very
busy spring because a crop must be planted at the farm, livestock must be
cared for, and the stages still came with passengers who expected a warm meal.
The five mile drives provided some rest but the work on the building included
heavy labor since full basement was scooped out and rocks hauled to be part of
the cement foundation.

The teacher who came from Aberdeen to finish the term of school was a
brother of N. A. Brothers, an Aberdeen photographer, and quite good with a
camera himself. Because of his ability we have an interesting picture of the
pupils of that school and another of the four children in our family at that
time.

In April of 1907 we moved from the farm to the new place. My sister,
Zola, and I had not been to the site before. In the quite level country the
three grain elevators soon showed on the horizon. "Look at the big cream
cans," I said, but the older children remembered grain elevators from Westport
and set me straight on this.

With our arrival, the town had a population of at least thirty since two
other families, C. J. Kindred's and Emil Von Eschen's, already occupied their
homes, and a number of construction men employed by the railroad, grain and
lumber companies, or private individuals were still there.

A 1967 Atlas of McPherson County states that our home was the first in
the new town, and it may have been started first but our day of arrival surely
depended upon the date set for the first transportation of the mail by the
train.

Dad's selection of lot five on Vivian Avenue indicates he may have
started the building before the town survey was complete. This home plot had
room for a barn too, but the post office should have been on Main Street. Lots
seven and eight across the avenue were our garden and later small trees brought
from the Elm River marked its borders. Eventually, along the south side, a
shed protected the buggies and some of the McCormick machinery for which Dad
had an agency. After our 1910 fire a new building was built on Main Street
east of the First State Bank.

A good reason for the first location had been its nearness to the Hawkeye
Grain, Coal and Lumber Company. This company with its elevator, coal sheds,
lumber shed and yard and already quite complete, had hired Dad as manager.

Mr. William Brearton of Morrison, Illinois, became the Pacific Elevator
agent and at the McIntyre Engold, Mr. Von Eschen had charge. The three
original elevators were painted red. At least two sold coal and had long sheds
with separate bins for several grades of bituminous coal and one for
anthracite. Locally called "hard coal", anthracite was needed for the
decorative "hard coal burners" of the time. Sometimes there were even clean,
man-made, coal dust briquets for sale. All these substantial buildings and
innovations were enough to convince us country children we had a town!

Right off the town had to have a name. There had been no lighthearted
Christening of the station by railroad officials. Somehow a list of suitable
names was received from Doane Robinson. He served the State a long time as
State Historian and if not named to that position in 1907, he was actually a
leading state authority who had written a lengthy book on state history and
also a grade school text. The name Dad and other men of the town chose was
"Wetonka", said to mean great in the Sioux language, and to be the name of a
great Sioux chief. If its true the penmanship was not too plain when the name
was submitted and the choice had been "Wetonka", at least it indicated positive
thinking on the part of the first citizens.

A town grows through the opportunities it offers. From Bern the A. S.
Baker family wrote a daughter, Lydia Kindred, of the new railroad and station
to be started so Lydia's husband, Canus, came from their home in Winona,
Minnesota, to do carpenter work. He received a contract to build the first
elevator and also several other structures. On Vivian Avenue he began a snug
little home for his family. Because of his knowledge of building materials and
able carpentry, The Floete, Cory and Bemis Company hired him as agent for
business they established under the simpler name Floete Lumber and Coal
Company. This occupied several of the lots along the south side of Main Street
which were well fenced with neat grey pickets. There was a lumber shed on the
west and an office building with living quarters across from it but facing on
the street. The shed, mostly open on the east side, had a central covered area
for finished lumber and a completely enclosed and locked department at either
end for precious items like doors, window frames, and materials like lime,
cement and plaster which must be kept dry. In the open yard and east of the
driveway the yard was filled with stacks of building timbers not hurt by open
storage. The scales for weighing wagon loads of bulk items were inside the
office behind the big window where the weight could be read from the street or
by the agent in the office. The platform for the wagon scales was in the
street, the scales mechanism in a pit below it.

One can see that this early development determined the course of the main
thoroughfare as the Floete Company intended it should. The store built by
other neighbors from Bern, just east of the Floete Company, indicated this
area would be the trading center. There was no doubt about it when the hotel,
the hardware store, Heil's bank and large store were built on the lots across
the street and the second bank established west of the lumber yard.

Louie Ludwig Heil, his wife Pauline, and her brothers, Jake and Adolph
Preszler, came from Menno, South Dakota, the first summer of the town's
development. They built a small bank and a big store (comparatively big). The
bank of cement blocks had a city type facade with plate glass window and a
corner entrance. An outside stairway led to the pool room and barber shop below
where Knute Lee and later Bill Alexander were the first barbers.

Heil's were German Lutheran and not Mennonnite as one might assume. They
brought a lot of new things to the new town like style and money! And the
first automobile--a red Rambler. The big house on several lots north of the
store seemed the very center of the town. With two stories and a long porch
wrapped around the north and west sides it drew the attention of any
newcomers. It lacked indoor plumbing but the outdoor facility was up to village
standards and neatly painted to match the small barn. The town's new artesian
well was drilled just south of this home.

The good quality construction Heil's sponsored was done by a carpenter
team, Walton and Jamison, and probably Bert Birdseye who married Vinnie
Ferguson of the pioneer Ferguson family south of town. Jake Preszler, cashier
in Heil's bank, married Vinnie's sister, Belle, the bank clerk. Dick Jamison
married Wetonka's second teacher, Frances Ladd, another Scotch girl from
Edmonds County.

The pool hall and barber shop usually frequented by men and boys had a
monopoly on pop. We children visited this recreation area to spend occasional
nickels. Our good friend, Mr. Ledingham, a salesman, provided five cent pieces
with some regularity. For us Friel kids he had started this nickel largesse
when he visited our Westport store about once a month. For a time his route
remained unchanged and he still called on storekeepers in Westport and then
came to Wetonka and on to Leola in a livery buggy driven by Mr. Morrison. Dad
liked him but not because he spoiled us with money; he had delightful new
jokes to tell on each trip. When the Aberdeen paper brought the news of his
accidental death we hated to believe. Evidently he had fallen between the
railroad coaches as he boarded a train carrying his heavy cases. Dad, saddened
by the tragic death, wrote a poem for the paper in an attempt to show the
character of this kindly man who shared his warm personality as he recommended
and sold quality goods.

Heil's were not the only German people in the community. There were
several families like Doll's. Doell's, Schile's, Kruger's and Lorber's who had
come to the bare prairies and obtained their land by filling and "proving up"
on claims. I don't know when Esser's, Franzen's, Raetzmann's, Mass's, Schick's
and Bielke's came. Sometimes an old-timer might say, "Bielke's farm used to be
the old Harmon place," and I'd assume they were the original owners. In 1908
if we'd known more German's were coming we'd have been interested and pleased.
Those were different times from the approaching World War I period when
Wetonka had a home guard drilling in the hall with home made practice guns of
wood, and two men on duty each night patrolling the streets to protect our
town against possible German invaders!

Joe Lynch, the vanguard of the Maple Lake, Minnesota, Germans came to
take over the time consuming job our dad had as agent for The Hawkeye Grain,
Coal and Lumber Company. Dad welcomed Joe as a fellow Irishman which he may
have been, but his name was that of the family who had adopted him. His young
wife, Lena, turned out to be an outspoken German girl with a noticeable accent.

Joe and Lena caught the fever of gambling on the possibilities of the new
town and they had what this required: ideas, building materials, and Lena's
carpenter brothers. Soon in some order or collectively, Lena's mother, Mrs.
Rachel; three brothers, Will, John, and Tony; a sister's family, the Felix
Hartman's; all came from Maple Lake. They built the hotel, a temporary home
for Lynch's, a home for Will Rachel's, a later and larger home for Lynch's
with out-buildings, also the Hartman home, and several more. Mrs. Rachel's
place was just west of the Catholic Church which they probably built too. All
of this amounted to a new section of town on what the village plot designated
Moulton Avenue.

To help Lena Lynch in the hotel, identical twins, Nellie and Nettie
Coombs, came from Maple Lake. Two other Coombs sisters arrived later and three
of these Minnesota girls stayed as wives of farmers--Mrs. Guy Inscho, Mrs.
Oscar Broadbent and Mrs. Charles Heagley.

When Joe and Lena had their first baby, Sylvester, and moved into their new
house, the Thomas Doell family from southeast of town ran the hotel, at least
until they enlarged the Duncan home across from the Presbyterian church. Mr.
Doell replaced Mr. Duncan as drayman and Mrs. Doell kept lucky, well fed
roomers. A Mr. L. C. Robinson owned the hotel long enough to paint over the
sign and advertise it as "Wetonka House, L. C. Robinson, Prop." John Meade
owned or rented it and his "woman-of-all-work", Ada Thomas, had her own ideas
and spoke her mind! She said of us and our home fire which
two-and-a-half-year-old Ambrose started in the upstairs and attic, "That kid
burned their house and they didn't even lick him!" (The poor baby did well to
get down from the inferno--no one else was able to enter the stair well.)
George Kirnan's owned the hotel and occupied it on several occasions. A
Goodrich family of a father and several sons were there for a time before Mrs.
Kindred bought it and refurnished it. With new outside paint and a sign
reading "HOTEL KINDRED" the town could be proud of the efficient place again.
In the late twenties when Kindred's moved to La Verne, California, Lucille
Kruse Wilson kept the hotel in operation but during the winter of 1928-29 our
brother and his wife, Oswald and Floss Friel, were the renters. I believe it
remained closed after that.

I have some good memories of the hotel: Mrs. Kirnan was a friendly, witty
woman, tolerant of kids, and Frank Goodrich's wife (for a time) occasionally
hired me to help her. She gave me a neat brown cross (celluloid? amber?)
because, as she said, "You're Catholic and they're mostly worn by Catholics."
After sixty-three years I still have it. (I think I had it with me in 1975 on
Ascension Day at the Vatican when my sister and I heard Pope Paul VI speak and
bless all visitors and religious articles which they carried.) Then there were
the dance suppers at the hotel when we were teenagers, and the Kindred girls
who were our longtime friends.

The first get-together of the children of the original three families had
taken us to the hill northwest of our homes. Von Eschen's cattle had gone
there to graze and we went to bring them back on a pleasant sunny evening. The
hill, actually a knoll, offered a noticeable variation in the landscape where
the terrain was so level. Covered with lavender Pasque flowers it became
"Crocus Hill" to us, a place to go for a walk as popular as walking up the
railroad tracks as far as Fyke's, Vincent's, or Calder's. It seemed steep the
day my cousin, Adeline, and I borrowed a hired man's too big, flat tired
bicycle for practice. Surely we could learn to ride if we pushed the bike to
the crest and headed it down grade. After spills and bruises, we did. We could
manage bicycles after that but had none of our own. Grazing reduced the number
of flowers growing on the hill, but gravel caused its downfall. When its
stoney heart became known it grew scarred and disfigured as gravel was dug and
hauled away for use elsewhere.

Because of Dad's job as first agent for the Hawkeye Company we thought we
had a right to play there. The elevator though interesting was not suitable
for games! Its outside stairway did attract older children and our year-old
brother once had to be rescued from a high landing where he had stopped to
rest and nap, but kids, my age, liked the lumber yard! It seemed a wonderful
place to explore. Hawkeye's shed, almost a duplicate of Floete's, but painted
red like the elevator, had the distinct advantage of being at a distance from
the office. Mountain climbing over the stacks or hide and seek behind nicely
arranged boards at Floete's could alert the manager. The hiding places were
like secret little rooms formed when stacks of long lumber were on either side
of a stack of short lengths. One had to enter such a cubbyhole from the top
and climb out carefully using the stack separators for foot holds. Knocking
over some piled lumber could call attention to your presence, and get you some
place--like sent home and told not to play in the lumber where you might be
hurt. Not play there? Of course, we came back. The overhead walkways built to
get at the high stacks had an irresistible fascination for prairie kids who
never saw high bridges or viaducts.

Some years later when the Hawkeye property had been bought by Lorber's
and Lynch's, I saw my first bat on a stack of lumber. Joe Lorber appeared from
somewhere, so my chum and I asked about the bat hoping to explain why we were
there. Joe said, "Do you want it?" I hadn't considered ownership, and I don't
know that I even attempted an answer before he quickly struck the quiet little
creature with a lath and handed it to me. I felt very bad I hadn't wanted a
dead bat but I accepted the beautiful black furry flier and learned what it
was really like. Mr. Lorber, a good farmer, evidently did not know of the
service bats render as they feed on cut-worm moths. (Of course, I didn't know
about bat diets either at that time.)

The railroad stockyards made another good play area. There was little
chance of hurting anything. Built to withstand cattle the heavy construction
easily withstood kids! What a jungle gym it made when empty and dry with all
those separate pens and fence-like partitions for climbing. The box cars were
for kids too, but not when the train arrived and cars were being moved!

Mr. Weed, the first blacksmith, made a visit to his shop interesting. A
teacher suggested this when we read "The Village Blacksmith." He showed us
about the forge and bellows and we felt welcome. His successor, Olamp Durand,
equally kind to children, let us heat iron and try to use some of the
equipment. As a friend of his older girl, Bernice, I watched this competent
ironworker at special horse shoeing jobs where he dug out infected hooves and
put leather protection under the metal shoe. Sometimes he heated metal rods
and made good kitchen tools like a two prong fork with an attractive handle of
the twisted steel. One of the strong forks Mrs. Durand gave Mother is now in
Dakotah Prairie Museum in Aberdeen. During the time the town water system no
longer served because the underground pipes had frozen and burst we On a quiet
Sunday when the baseball team played away from home a sudden electrical storm
offered a special diversion and the Pacific elevator was struck by the
lightning. With no one else about, Dad and Harold Palmquist rushed for the
fire engine. One manned the motor and one climbed the narrow stairway inside
the elevator with the hose. They extinguished the fire! They had got to it
quickly and it was not a time when the air was full of grain dust.

The improved water system of 1918 provided pressure better able to cope
with fires, but circumstances always decide the outcome--how advanced the fire
is, how well the equipment is used, and the weather. When three buildings
burned on Main Street in 1931 the windy weather was severe and the fire well
under way when discovered.

Southwest of the tracks, the Holmes Addition became part of the town in
1909 when Lew Holmes sold his farm to Harvey and Ina Selser of Red Oak, Iowa,
and bought this acreage from Floete, Cory and Bemis.: He paid about $2,900 for
it and had it surveyed for home sites but kept over sixty acres for a farm
type home for his own family. This included a large house, barn, silo,
well-lighted chicken house, and fuel shed. With its good paint job and nicely
planted and fenced yard, it made an attractive view from the train.

Mr. Holmes had an artesian well drilled to furnish water for his use and
that of Mrs. Sears, his wife's mother, who bought one of the residential lots
and built a good home on it. A sizable triangular piece of the Holmes Addition
at the south end where the railroad crosses a county road, was sold to a little
understood easterner, C. J. Hill. For some reason, health or economy, this
studious man, his younger wife, and little girl came to our prairie town to
retire! They became valuable assets.*

Lew, hired by the farmers who had organized to build the Farmers'
Elevator, bought and shipped grain for them and sold coal. Reorganized later-
the name became Wetonka Farmers Equity Exchange and survived for years.
Another interest of Mr. Holmes--cars--led him into garage ownership where he
sold Dort cars. Friendly and enthusiastic, he was too generous to be a shrewd
business man but he contributed greatly to the spirit of the young town and
its activities. The Holmes children, Billie, Genevieve, Lester, and Dwight
were our close friends for years. After 1919 the family lived in Aberdeen
where Juanita was born, and in Veblen before going to Amberg, Wisconsin, to
make their permanent home.

Charlie Francis, the first railroad crew foreman, lived in the north half
of the section house when it was ready. Mrs. Francis loved children and often
produced a gift or a goodie for a young visitor. My combination book of
Gulliver's Travels and Puss In Boots came from the Francis home. It may have
been discarded by teenager Ada but it survived at our place for at least
twenty additional years. Once I must have arrived at Francis's at an
inconvenient time. I received a paper roll of brown sugar candy to take home
with me. Sticky and possibly one of Ada's failures in cooking, it was well
received by me. I appreciated it and hurried away, but like other
five-year-olds I have known, I felt my departure had been graciously speeded
up, though it was no unkind "bums' rush."

Lundeen's who lived in the other half of the railroad quarters at that
time lost a baby girl. I think we understood about death but wondered what our
parents did at the home when they spent part of the night there. Mother said,
"We kept fresh cloths which had been dipped in formaldehyde on the little face
so it would not become discolored. Coins were placed on the eyelids to keep
the eyes closed as in sleep." I remember nothing about a funeral. Of course,
there was no undertaker in the new town, no church established, no minister,
no graveyard, so the child may have been buried in Leola or in a often
borrowed a well balanced pushcart Mr. Durand had built. It was just right for
kids to use in hauling small barrels of water from the well. (We were better
at borrowing it than taking it back so: Mother bought it.)

The artesian well which the railroad builders had provided early for
their crews, and which was the only source of water for the first residents
did not seem to be a good well and did not last many years. Impatient with its
weak flow and the task of carrying water in pails or hauling barrrels of it on
"stone boats" must have driven the first householders to contract for their
own well. It was drilled in the central area south of Heil's house and was
completed toward the end of 1907. Ambrose Kimmit and a Mr. Chase were the well
drillers. The choice of the location and the quality of the pipe used or the
depth reached might explain the difference in the wells. This second well has
continued to furnish an ample supply of water over many years. The water is
considered "soft" for washing clothes, and although strangers have disliked
the taste, the minerals it contains seem to have given local children good
teeth. One of these, our December 1907 baby brother, was named for Mr. Kimmit
because our dad liked the man and because Dad knew about St. Ambrose. Although
the baby did not grow up to be a well driller, he and his pals enjoyed the
deep ditches for play areas and the high water tank for climbing when these
features were added to the renewed system in 1921. Actually, Ambrose's
youthful association with the tank caused me to record his experiences as "A
Boy and His Tower."

Some of the grain elevators were not finished at the time of the arrival
of the first families The depot, section house for railroad track repairmen,
and the stockyard were not built, or not completed. By 1909 an organization of
farmers had built a fourth elevator, The Farmers' Elevator, the different
elevator, the elevator that wasn't red but covered with galvanized metal
siding. The Pacific had already been rebuilt, by 1910! It burned one morning
during Frances Ladd's 1908-1909 term of school. A student returning from an
outdoor errand reported the fire and the students rustled from the building.
As many as two of us beginners stayed with Miss Ladd. We hadn't been told what
to do in case of fire and it seemed safer to stay with the teacher. After all,
the school wasn't on fire. If Frances had wanted to go to the conflagration,
she didn't have a chance--she was stuck with us. So the primary department
missed a big show, but later I did benefit some in watching construction when
the Pacific was replaced. I saw a horse traveling in circles to hoist timbers
to men working on high scaffolds. Painting an elevator can be something too,
when professional painters work in pairs on a plank supported by ropes with
pulleys hung from hooks on the roof. They must balance each other and operate
the ropes to lower themselves to new positions.

I missed most of another big fire in 1911. On a toilet errand one night I
discovered a group of people apparently enjoying a bonfire near the McIntyre
Engold elevator, but the building was not there! I awakened my parents, "The
elevator has burned down!" Unbelievingly, my mother said, "Goodness, Sylvia,
did you sleep through that!?" It seemed our place had been a prime source of
water for the bucket brigade, with the faucet on full, banging pails, running
feet, and excited voices. Our parents must have just got to bed. Again the
water pail fighters had made no progress against a fire, and the group still
watching were there to see that final glowing timbers and smoldering grain did
not flare again to spread to another area.

The McIntyre-Engold was not rebuilt but a Mr. Kagel acquired the site for
a smaller elevator he built about 1915 when he brought his family to Wetonka
and the former home of Mrs. Sears where they lived.

The destructive fires prompted the town to purchase a push cart sort of
fire engine with hose from which a chemical could be sprayed on a fire.

Harrington's and Hunts were the next two families I remember living in
the section house.

When George Walker became section foreman and his crews repaired the
tracks in both directions, an improved work car with a gas motor took the men
to their work. The hand powered car had fascinated our young brother but the
gas car was irresistible. Sometimes he spent long hot days out with the crew.
George's mother who kept house for him resembled Mrs. Francis in being kind to
all young people. She had come from Virginia and we liked the kindness in her
soft voice. After George married our pretty primary teacher, Olga Belseth, he
transferred to Aberdeen where they lived the rest of their lives.

Joe Esser, one of the local men, who worked many years "on the section"
sometimes misunderstood town plans but he could be determined and he'd speak
up about what he believed. When the need to incorporate the community as a
town seemed proper to a number of the leaders, there was argument. Joe had
negative ideas on the "Coperation" as he called it. To arouse his temper and
hear his views on "dis coperation", the roustabouts produced surprising
reasons in favor of the change in status for Wetonka. They dreamed up more
benefits than were acceptable to Joe and he angrily told them off. One night
the progressive measure to be gained was a second post off ice. "When we get
this 'coperation,' Joe's nephew said, "we can have another post office like
we've got two banks." Joe blew up, but bless him, he was loyal and on our
side, "If dey do, I stick by Friel. He always been good to me." (I think
incorporation was necessary in order to finance the construction of the water
tower and improve the water system which had failed.)

One summer three attractive young men from Sweden worked on the tracks.
it seemed unsuitable work for them but could have been the job on which they
could save the most money since the "grounded" box cars where they lived were
rent free and their expenses only those for food and clothes.

Of the southern European workers, Mike and Vinie stand out in memory.
These happy men from Bulgaria and Serbia also lived in the box cars and saved
their wages for a better life on their return to their families. They
interested town children by baking their own bread in an oven dug into the
"cut bank" and lined with rock. It held heat enough for the baking after a
wood fire had first burned in the oven to heat the rocks. One of these men had
some acquaintance with Chicago. Our tiny brother's name, Marshall Friel,
amused him. "Marshall Field", he would say. "In Chicago he be a big man."

When the depot had been finished a dance was held there. I don't suppose
I stayed long at the dance, but I recall George Kirnan playing violin
accompanied by his young daughter, Mae. She got a lot of chords out of a small
pump organ that had folded out from a container no larger than an old
fashioned suitcase. Because of Mae's instrument I remember that music but do
not know who played at the first dances held in our own upstairs before Heil's
store and hall were completed.

Fred Brearton, a son of the Pacific Elevator grain buyer, came from his
work at the Minneapolis and St. Louis station in Aberdeen to be the first
depot agent. He and his wife, Rozina, lived in the town several different
times. Sometimes Fred helped at the farm his father owned just south of the
village. He invented a potato digger--a horse drawn machine which dug the
potatoes, elevated them on a moving belt to a level where workers on either
side sorted and sized them. Potatoes were raised and the invention tested and
improved, but no satisfactory purchase offer was received by Fred and attempts
to organize a company to market it did not work out.

There were depot agents and depot agents but the agent to end all agents
was Sargent. He grew to fit the town as one who had been born there. He
married Margie Drake, a primary teacher, and though they made their home in
Aberdeen, Sarge lived in Wetonka during the week. In the thirties when the
railroad no longer maintained daily train service but used a truck for mail,
express, and light freight, he was the driver. Arriving before noon he
delivered the mail to the post office first and then the express before going
on to Leola. He came back in the afternoon to pick up the mail and shipments
for Aberdeen and other areas.

Sarge acquired some lots on Main Street which he watered and kept mowed.
This helped the street's appearance as more and more areas were abandoned and
became unsightly. After retirement he often came to town with Tom Lohner who
had farming interests including the old William Brearton place which Lohner's
kept as a second home for overnight stops. Towards the end of his life Sarge
became convinced he had acquired a portrait of historical importance. He
bought it from an artist who claimed to have had a vision of the subject,
Chief Wetonka--a great chief from whom the town received its name. Revelation,
also part of the vision, indicated the area was still designed for greatness!

The train service provided seems almost unbelievable in the realities of
the 1970's when again there are no trains and even the railroad tracks have
been taken up. At first there were two round trips to Leola from Aberdeen each
weekday--four exciting breaks for watching passengers and two times to check
your mailbox or four times if you expected mail from Leola, too, as on days
when The McPherson County Herald came. This made the post office work, if not
heavy, at least often. One could go to Leola on a big errand, like seeing a
doctor, in the forenoon and return on the afternoon, or arrive in Leola on a
quick errand at the courthouse and be on the way home again in less than thirty
minutes. One could make a forenoon trip to Aberdeen and return that afternoon.
This amounted to incredible transportation after the twenty mile horse and
buggy trips to the doctor, to legal offices, lo banks, and to large stores
before the railroad came.

Transportation played a big part in the celebration the enthusiastic and
high spirited early residents put on in 1909 or 1910. I'd guess 1909 for the
year since the Fourth of July, a Sunday, made the special train necessary.
That "Special" caused excitement as did the big rented tent set up in the
street in front of the hotel. The tent furnished shade and a place for the
program. A speaker may have come--at seven or eight I would not have cared
about a speaker. At least the "Declaration of Independence'' must have been
read. Our dad would have done that himself if no speaker had been available.

Besides some visitors many treats came on the train--tall wood tubs with
lots of ice around ten gallon containers of ice cream, and crates and crates
of pop, and special orders of fresh bread. There were stands set up where one
bought cones of ice cream and bottles of pop or competed in throwing or
spinning techniques to win prizes. There were firecrackers for sale--any kind,
dangerous or not.

If the inevitable ball game for the afternoon included the Westport team,
a good representation from that town came. This would mean relatives and
friends of our parents attended--Uncle Floyd and Aunt Mary Friel Johnson in
their high wheeled, chain driven International and Uncle Abe Neer in his E.M.F.

Our thirteen-year-old sister, Inez, had two new dresses for the Fourth, a
rose colored cotton for the day and a lovely one of white embroidery for the
dance. The dance I don't remember but the white dress I recall well--after it
was Zola's, it was mine for several years. It escaped our 1910 home fire when
practically all our clothing burned except what we were wearing on the hot
July day.

The train brought a chapel car in 1908 which remained on the siding for a
week before moving on to Leola. It had accommodations for two priests and a
negro cook as well as the chapel for Masses. Mother, our baby and I were
baptized with Mr. and Mrs. J. H. Doll as sponsors. The older children had been
baptized in Westport. Mother, too, as a Methodist, then.

In 1914 Wetonka had another special train service for dog owners who
brought their animals to the territory for competitive work outs. It was an
event entirely foreign to the community and not too well understood by the
farmers on whose land the dogs flushed out the birds. A Scotchman, Gordon B.
Currie, a substitute minister in the Presbyterian Church, made the
arrangements for the hunters and the hotel, not really well staffed at the
time, attempted to care for the aristocratic eastern and southern men who came
during the three day contest. We often wondered if the visitors were
disappointed in the venture or if they anticipated primitive housing and found
the open fields and plentiful prairie fowl sufficient for the tests in which
their dogs competed.

After the train service had been reduced to one trip each weekday there
were occasional specials to take people to Aberdeen on circus days. A Sunday
"Special" in the late twenties took passengers to Watertown to hear Governor
Bulow speak at Lake Kampeska. My sister, Inez, and I made that trip. We were
guests of the family of one of my college girl friends at their picnic in the
park, listened to the Governor's talk, and enjoyed a swim in the lake before
the evening train ride back to Wetonka.

Mr. Maloney, the M & St. L's conductor on the Aberdeen-Leola spur line,
an efficient and kindly man, is the only conductor I recall. I think he served
over thirty years. As long as I rode the train Mr. Maloney was there. On the
trip to Aunt Susie's in 1908, he was there; on the special trips on circus
days, he was there. When we went to the Birth of a Nation, when Mother brought
me home after a tonsillectomy, on the many trips when I attended school in
Aberdeen, and on Christmas vacations after I left home--always Mr. Maloney. So
there was something about the train which did not change for a long time.

And there was something about the town which did not change for a long
time--"Friel's" post office. Our dad, Ed Friel, served as postmaster from 1907
until 1915 and Mother from then until her retirement in February of 1945.
Mother had always kept the records and accurate as she was with bookkeeping
and reports, I'm sure the office was efficiently run. The hours were surely
convenient--at midnight if the post office door had been locked one could
knock at the kitchen entrance for mail or for a money order.

The James McIntyre and Fred Kruse families were town blessings in the
early '20's. Mr. McIntyre, as an associate of L. B. Geisler who had purchased
the old Hawkeye elevator from Lorber and Lynch in 1923, bought grain and later
served in the old Wetonka Mercantile which Lou Geisler also acquired. Kruse's
came from North Dakota to replace Jim McIntyre in the store work which would
be impossible to combine with grain buying in threshing time. Popular little
Margaret McIntyre matured and taught in the local school. McIntyre's remained
as residents until after Margaret's marriage and their Wetonka baby, Jimmy,
had become a school boy.

Earl Kruse became postmaster when Mother retired. He bought our old
building and kept the office in the location until he accepted the mail
carrier job and his sister, Cindy, Mrs. Albert Bielke, became postmaster.
Cindy and Albert moved the office to the store building east of the old
location which they modernized for more comfortable living quarters and where
their post office and store could be larger.

The Kruse family, like Sargent, had the good of the town in mind. Earl served
until his death in 1968 and Cindy until retirement in the 1970's. Earl owned
and occupied Kindred's durable little home but our old place was vacant when
destroyed by wind. Fortunately Albert, employed in trucking and road work, was
able to bulldoze the remains into the basement and level the scarred area.

The locally made cement blocks were said to be defective because the sand
and gravel used needed to be more free of clay. The three structures built of
the blocks did not survive as well as those of timber. Our building developed
cracks, and Heil's bank, too. The first church, Reverend Latchaw's Presbyterian
Church, was saved because the members succeeded in replacing the blocks with
timbers and siding. What might replace the town and the people?

Who needs a train when most of the year a one man truck can easily haul
the mail and express on good highways provided by the State? Who needs a town
when personal transportation is taken as a matter of course? Who needs small
post offices and obscure addresses when routes from cities can be extended? I
hope the vision of greatness Sarge's artist reported may come true. At present
the address is well known as that of the Hutterite community located at the
former Ed Hamilton farm and as the range of Ora Erdmann's prize Black Angus
cattle--Ora Erdmann, the baby of whom his mother said when we first met her,
"Yes, although Elizabeth is older, he is already the boss." No one could have
predicted his success as a breeder of superior cattle, and no one can predict
the future of our old townsite. Of the three original families, only
descendants of Von Eschen's remain. If needed as a center of future
development it would be a new town with new construction and new people.

                            KINDREDS

While our dad and his carpenters were going daily to the townsite of
Wetonka to build a combination store, post office and dwelling, Canis Kindred
from Winona, Minnesota, built a home across the street and a block east. His
wife, the former Lydia Baker, and he became interested in the new town when
her parents wrote about it. The Baker farm was about two miles north of Bern
Post Office where we lived. Mr. Baker, a Civil War veteran and his second wife
had come to South Dakota to make their home. Their family of six included
Lizzie, Laura, Elmer, Delilah, Floyd, and Martha.

Lydia Baker Kindred and the Kindred children came from Winona when their
house was complete enough to occupy. Mr. Kindred also built the town's first
grain elevator and several other structures before he accepted the manager's
job for the Floete Lumber Company. This company's office, shed, and large
fenced lumber yard were on Main Street west of The Wetonka Exchange (Stone and
Lindley's general store). Kindred's did not need the living quarters provided
above the office but Knute Lee, the first barber did and later Mr. Weed, the
first blacksmith, lived there.

Kindred's family matched ours for size, sex and the order of births but
each member was some years or months younger. The three older children had the
advantages of good town schools including kindergarten. Much of the
Anglo-Saxon heritage of children's sayings, songs, and games might have been
missing from our childhood without the influence of this family.

I vividly remember a party they had for one of their summer birthdays.
The games were played in the small barn where low chairs were arranged in a
circle and many delightful games introduced besides the too familiar "Drop the
Handkerchief" and "London Bridge." The oldest girl, Genevieve, directed the
play and knew when to make a change. There was much laughter before some of us
younger children caught on to "Book, Book, Pretty Book" where one must say,
"Ahem! Book, Book, Pretty Book" the second time the book comes around the
circle. Another game, "Puppy", is a version of "Drop the Handkerchief." The
puppy runs around the players patting heads and saying, "I won't bite you,"
until a child is selected to race for the vacant seat. Then the puppy says,
"But I will bite you!" and the race is on. Of course the loser is the new
puppy.

These children often had their version of "Cricket" going in the vacant
lot beside their home which also served as the calf pasture. It could be an
exciting and competitive game for a large group. No equipment needed to be
bought. A small hole was dug in the ground and a short stick placed across it.
A team member used an improvised club to lift the stick into the air and bat
it. The club length then became the unit of measurement for the distance that
counted as the individual's score.

Music was a part of the Kindred family life and although Genevieve had
organ lessons, little Marvel who had learned at home, could play sprightly
tunes with both hands before she entered school. Lucille, the middle girl, was
able to sing correctly the phrases of music given out by one of our teachers
and this won my respect! I had to take mine home to the piano and memorize the
tune.

Clearly, I recall a fun time a group of us had on Kindred's vine-covered
porch on a hot afternoon. We sang lustily, hymn after hymn from their Church
of the Brethren song book. I had never heard "The Old Time Religion" before
and it seemed slangy and carefree to me after our solemn Catholic music and
Mother's Methodist "work songs." (She'd sing, "Tho' our sins be a-scarlet,
they shall be as white as snow," as she rubbed clothes on the washboard, and
I'd think, "She must mean the clothes--she's no sinner.") That afternoon we
laughed and laughed and sang, "The Old Time Religion" verse after verse.

The first year in Wetonka we had a baby brother, Ambrose. Later in the
year Kindred's had Ivan. Von Eschen's didn't need a new baby. They already had
two boys besides three girls and their Leonard was still very young. Heils had
come and they had Walter. They had lost their beautiful little boy, Arthur,
and Lundeen's who were employed by the railroad lost their baby girl. Jack
Ellis's, a mile and a half northwest of town, lost their first child, the only
girl they ever had. If this seems an uncertain way for a town to grow, it was.
The surer way was for a family like Alldritt's to move in with a sturdy group
of nine. The years of 1910 and 1911 were good growth years. Both bankers,
Hamilton's and Heil's had babies in the fall, our baby arrived early Christmas
morning, and Kindred's, Lew Holmes's and Von Eschen's babies came toward
spring. There were other births in the school district just outside the town
boundaries, and later families who had children of that age moved into the
area. These children made up the largest class ever to go through the local
school.

A few days after the arrival of a new baby at the home of one of our
classmates we were allowed to stop in to see the infant. The Kindred baby had
dark hair and when I saw her she had a little rash on her face. She became a
beautiful child with dark curls and violet-blue eyes.

I could appreciate how much Kindred's loved this little girl because at
eight I was enough older than our small brother to think he was the greatest
gift a family could have received and as beautiful as Kindred's Vivian with
his blond curls. The following summer the dreaded "Cholera Morbus" struck -that
fearful threat which had Mother reaching for castor oil and orange juice at
the first sign of fever or irregularity in our baby. Von Eschen's Albert and
little Vivian became ill. Albert lived but with the best care known at the
time Vivian did not get better. Her illness went on for weeks, so it seems
with modern methods she surely could have lived. At the time of her death the
fall term of school had started and we were dismissed to attend the funeral in
a group. The flowers at the church were from local gardens and the odor of
sweet peas prevailed. Always the fragrance of these flowers brings back the
memory of Kindred's loss and the little casket at the Presbyterian Church.

After this time Mr. Kindred was not well. It seemed he had injured
himself while helping to unload and store the carload of ice he and our dad
had bought that summer. Then we children were shocked to learn that the family
meant to move far away to Greeley, Colorado. It was sad to go to the afternoon
train where their household goods had been loaded and tell our school friends,
"Good-bye." Someone explained, "Mr. Kindred has relatives in Greeley, there
will be doctors there, and the climate will be better for him." But the
doctors and the climate were not enough and the family also lost their kindly
father.

By 1916 Mrs. Kindred and the five children were back. She was ill but not
pessimistic. With help from a brother-in-law, Otis Gilbourne, she started a
restaurant in the old Floete Lumber office and the family lived upstairs. The
lumber business had been discontinued and the long covered shed removed. Our
dad had been able to buy that part of the lot and planted trees and shrubs
there. Finally it became a park-like area which permitted more sunlight in our
east windows.

Mrs. Kindred recovered from serious surgery, organized her family and
made a success of the small restaurant. It helped the town to have a clean
place to eat after several years of a sketchily run hotel. Genevieve and
Lucille taught rural schools at Bradley, South Dakota, when they were of age
and Meredith, the older son, had a job "on the section" for the railroad. He'd
had a birth injury and was not well enough coordinated for all types of work.

About 1920 Mrs. Kindred bought the old hotel building which she repaired
and refurnished. This also benefited the town by adding overnight
accommodations and more dining space for farmers and business people in town
through the day. Here also the family continued to serve the midnight lunches
on dance nights.

Genevieve became Mrs. Rooke of Bradley, but Lucille or Marvel, or both
assisted their mother--at least during school vacations. Eventually there was
a Star car for the trips to Aberdeen and a good piano for Marvel who had early
shown talent in music.

Always capable of making a hard decision when it seemed best for the
future, Mrs. Kindred rented the hotel in the late twenties and moved to a
California town where her elderly parents lived in a home for Civil War
Veterans. Here again the family worked together and obtained beneficial
rewards. Ivan received a good education and Marvel a teaching certificate in
piano. They always had jobs and outings. Mother Lydia, in her late seventies,
still enjoyed a cross country trip or rock hunting at desert land they owned.
At the home they had equipment for polishing the stones and tools for making
the art work for gifts or for sale. There was a better piano for Marvel who
continued to teach after hours from her dental office employment.

Genevieve and her husband also moved to California and Lucille and her
husband remained near the family after their marriage. They were killed in an
airplane disaster more than twenty years ago. Mrs. Kindred and Meredith are no
longer living, but Marvel is still busy and not alone with Genevieve's and
Ivan's families not far away.

In 1977 the seventy-year old home, the first family residence completed
in Wetonka, is occupied. It has just been sold to a great-great-granddaughter
of Emil and Anna Von Eschen.

                          VON ESCHENS

The Von Eschen family didn't wait for carpenters and a new home. For
temporary use they moved a granary to their lots on the north side and at the
west end of what the townsite plot said was Vivian Avenue. Their home as I
remember it was an L-shaped, two story house very similar to the one at Bern
farm, the former home of Emil Von Eschen's parents. It wasn't the largest home
in Wetonka but, without doubt, the most occupied. At first in 1907 there was a
family of seven--Emil, Anna and their five children: Mable, Clara, Clarence,
Florence and Leonard. "Em's" job was to run the McIntyre-Ingold grain
elevator, and he, like our dad, continued to farm.

When the telephone exchange was completed Von Eschen's accepted the
switchboard and this work became part of the family chores. It took time and
patience because then one called "Central" for every local call unless the
person one wanted to reach happened to be "on your line". In that case one
could ring the long and short combinations which made up the neighbor's
"ring", himself.

A few years later Em bought Heil's general store which must have been a
mistake. This error and the high occupancy of the home are related. The
generous family couldn't say, "No."

Mrs. Von Eschen's parents were the William Walkers from Kankakee,
Illinois, the county from which our mother's family came, but I don't know
that they knew each other in Illinois. When Emil married Anna Walker he
acquired a great many relatives, and these relatives had relatives. With that
many family credit customers at a store, a bad year is not just a bad year,
it's a catastrophe. Then at home--Anna was a good cook and hospitable. All the
relatives and their relatives were always welcome. Later the grandchildren
enjoyed staying at Grandma's. Grandma, long suffering Grandma, was never
cross.

A baseball Sunday will illustrate how out-of-hand such hospitality can
get. When a game was being played on the local field, the cars and horse drawn
vehicles began arriving early at Von Eschen's from the farms and neighboring
towns. It was the natural thing; Anna's brothers were ball players and their
in-laws were ball players, and the ball diamond at that time was part of Von
Eschen's pasture. One Sunday the casual gathering for dinner at Anna's
included forty persons.

In the 1910 - 1911 baby boom at Wetonka, Von Eschen's had Albert, their
third boy and sixth child. He must have been four years younger than Leonard.
Albert survived the infant cholera when Kindred's lost their little girl. Anna
often called a neighbor, Mrs. Doell, in time of sickness and her timely help
may have favored Albert's recovery, but even Grandma Doell's skill with the
sick could not make things right again for Leonard when his trouble came.

On August twenty-eighth, 1913 Em Von Eschen and others were "putting up"
prairie hay: Anna's nephew, Lloyd Walker, twelve years old and small, was
thought able to drive a gentle team on a hayrake, and Leonard teased to go
along to the hayfield. His mother opposed the idea since he, at seven, was too
young to help. Em, appreciating how much the child wanted to be with the older
cousin, reluctantly consented. As the warm day wore on both boys, sharing the
single iron seat, were riding the rake and the quiet team showed no concern.
The boys talked and laughed happily, proud to be real farmers doing men's work.

Suddenly things were not right at all in the field. A nervous team one of
the men was driving on a mower broke from control and raced wildly over the
hayground. The boys' team continued their faithful work but the run-a-ways
headed toward them with their deadly sickle still in cutting position. Lloyd
pulled on the lines in an attempt to turn, and off balance, the impact of the
colliding machines sent him backwards out of the path of the rapidly cutting
edge. Poor Leonard, the crash threw him forward across the sickle bar which
instantly severed both his arms--the right just below the elbow, the left only
a few inches from his shoulder. He was painfully hurt otherwise from the fall,
the weight of the machinery, or the horses' hooves.

Emil racing to the bleeding boy could see immediately the horrible
severity of the crippling injury. Somehow he avoided panic. He made strips of
his old handkerchief and put tourniquets on the stubs of arms. He gently held
Leonard as they drove the miles back to town. They had phoned ahead to ask
that the afternoon train be held until Leonard could be prepared for the trip
to an Aberdeen hospital. Mrs. Doell came with antiseptic and fresh bandages.
No doubt her clean-up job was a factor in the progress the little boy made.

The townspeople waiting with the train were hushed and breathless, even
in shock at the horror or the ghastly affair. We felt the relentless finality
of the sickle's work and despaired of the boy's life, or wondered silently
about his future.

Em, of course, accompanied Leonard to the hospital. It was a time of no
blood transfusions and no antibiotics but the boy lived. The greatest injury
besides the loss of his arms was a badly broken leg. His care required weeks
in the hospital with the doctors not too hopeful at first. Then he improved and
enjoyed the hundreds of postcards the newspaper reports brought from strangers
as well as family friends. The father had been told by the doctors, "We are
pleased with your son's progress but want to warn you children so badly
injured so early in life often do not live to adulthood." It seemed as though
that could have been left unsaid, but it did not destroy the parent's optimism.

School had started before Leonard returned home. On his eighth birthday
in October the entire school was invited to his party. I had not been to such
a big party before--so many people all through the house; school kids talking
to Leonard in his bandages and cast or reading his many cards; grownups
helping the family serve the complete meal with beautiful cakes for dessert.

It was said Leonard would get hooks to wear as he grew and then
artificial arms to use as an adult. The Odd Fellow Lodge to which his father
belonged would educate him to do whatever kind of work he chose. When he
returned to school he had a hook for his right arm which he learned to handle
well because the elbow had been saved. He might have become a demanding
pampered boy since he had been the youngest child for at least four years and
now crippled there could have been a tendency to spare him. There was no child
specialist in the school, the town or in the Von Eschen family, but in the
family there was great love and kindness! Then there was great need when the
father suddenly died. The older brother could not do all the farm work and
hold a paying job too. The mother could not do all the homework and adequately
operate the switchboard. Mable clerked in Lindley's store and Clara was
married. Leonard helped where he could. One had to be present to realize how
well: at the switchboard his hook turned the manually operated bell and made
the connections unless two hands were needed. In that case he pushed in the
plug with his teeth. His resonant voice was also an asset. Sometime operators
from other towns asked, "Who's the man at central in Wetonka? He really sounds
nice."

Leonard was growing up. Actually we didn't know who "that man" was
ourselves. He felt no need for artificial arms--he could use the hook
skillfully. He had a good mind but did not want to go away to finish high
school. He did not seek the special training his father's lodge had promised.
He felt needed at home and seemed content. Strongly built and well formed he
could use a pitchfork or long handled shovel by holding the end under the
stump of his left arm and managing the tool with the hook. He had started this
early and done it so much his muscle development and coordination were good.

Baseball was another part of his therapy. It was the consuming interest
of town boys of all ages. They started young and played early, late, during
play periods, and throughout the school noon.

                              LINDLEY'S

It didn't start out as "Lindley's" but became that in time with the
popular use of the name. It came into being because George and Tabitha Hose
Stone also grew interested in the new railroad town. Jay had been teaching the
Doll School south of Stone's farm. That was Jay's last school. Thirteen years
later it was my first school. With no more of the large Doll family in
attendance it had become the Bielke School. Now it's a farmer's shed, but a
Doll granddaughter who visited the old site said she found a porcelain
doorknob which she prizes.

Bachelor Jay had a tall, narrow body with a big stomach--a really big
stomach! It surely put a strain on his legs to support such a mass. Adults
said he also had a milk leg, whatever that was. It didn't show. Someone said,
"It's a sore that doesn't heal because of poor circulation. Mrs. Esser has one
too, that's why she wears the pad and bandage on her foot instead of a shoe."
The affliction didn't stop Mrs. Esser from walking to Mass on summer Sunday's
nor keep her from growing beautiful flowers. Jay Lindley dared to go into
partnership with the Stones to build and operate a general store where he'd
need to spend a lot of time on his feet. He must have thought it would be
better than walking to a country school in snow storms and he must have hoped
to make more money.

George Stone and Jay started their building east of the Floete Lumber
yard after we were already living in the town. Their farm home not far from
the Bern Post Office had probably been George Stone's homestead. The house, a
unique structure in the mostly flat area, was built into a small hill for
protection in storms and extreme weather.

Each morning the two men drove to their future store to supervise or help
with the construction. Each evening they drove back to the farm--the buggy
sagging a lot on Jay's side. I think they continued to make the drives after
the completion of the store until their two-story square home next door to
Kindred's was ready.

When the store was painted it also got a beautiful sign done in Old
English lettering, "The Wetonka Exchange." Our Irish friend, Billie Earle, had
done it carefully, and I admired it. Some said, It isn't right for a
store--too hard to read." That didn't bother me; at five I let other people
read except for my specialties c-a-t and r-a-t and "Home Comfort" from the
oven door on our Wrought Iron Range.

Myrtle Inscho, a niece of George Stone, clerked in The Exchange before
her marriage to Will Doll. She assisted me in making my first independent
purchase on February 14, 1907. I had what it took to buy a Valentine--one
cent. These were actually Valentine cartoons and about the size of business
stationery. We called them "Homely Valentines" and they were, but I had to live
within my means. Brightly colored and gay, and more insulting than funny, they
were commonly known as "Funny Valentines." One could pick out a picture and
message likely to irritate an acquaintance and send it unsigned, by mail for
another cent. I had no intention of parting with mine--even a non-schoolage kid
needs a Valentine. I didn't ask what the cartoon said and Myrtle didn't
volunteer that information. She had been a teacher and would know how to
divide her time between a one cent sale and counting a pail of eggs packed in
barley some farmer wished to exchange for groceries. And she might have needed
to go to the back room to fill a gallon jug with vinegar from the wooden
barrel or even out behind the store to the oil shed to fill a kerosene or
gasoline can from a big tank. In February gasoline would have been an unlikely
item since it was used mostly for gasoline cook stoves in hot weather.

When Mother read the words on my Valentine I was disappointed and
probably didn't understand it. I did understand a post card some unknown
person sent to Maude Evans, the clerk in Calder's store. It showed a damsel
followed by a small bear and carried the notation, "Maude with her little bear
behind." This was questionable mail in 1907 and our parents debated if it
should be delivered to the attractive girl!

Well, the Exchange sold a lot of useful things besides Valentines and
trade was good. There were hitching posts in front of the store and along the
east side which were needed for the customers' teams. The storage space had to
be opened up into the sales area and more storage room added. Sometimes there
were four clerks. Mrs. Stone's niece, Iva Broadbent, worked there and Clarence
(Rusty) Ayres from Frederick, South Dakota. These two "kept company" and
married but they weren't the only sweethearts in the store. Mr. Stone had died
about 1909 and after a proper waiting period and the proper words said, it was
Uncle Jay and Aunt Tab Lindley. Despite the sign the store became "Lindley's".
Who is going to say, The Wetonka Exchange" in six syllables when two will do?
Besides it might be confused with the name of the fourth grain elevator, The
Wetonka Equity Exchange, but that became "The Equity" in four syllables.

I liked to go to Lindley's because from our new location on Main Street
there was continuous sidewalk--Dad had a cement walk and the Floete company
had built one of plank along their frontage. For several years Mabel Von
Eschen was their clerk--always pleasant, and patient. She was also a relative
of Mrs. Lindley--a great niece.

Mrs. Lindley had so many nieces and nephews among Broadbent's, Walker's,
Von Eschen's, Ellis's, Morrison's, Ayres's, that many others adopted the
practice of calling her "Aunt Tab". I'm sure she didn't object. A sharp, witty
woman, she continued to be alert past the age of ninety-five. She headed the
Red Cross work in the town during World War I, and we turned in our knitting
to her and got more yarn when we needed it. I felt relieved when she accepted
by first pullover sweater because I had fought a grim battle with the needles
and yarn on the lower half of the front and that part was noticeably looser
and larger than the upper part. The back was a uniform job. I thought, "She
will rip it out and do it over, but she didn't want to discourage me."

It just occurred to me that it might have looked right to her--just the thing
for a soldier built like Jay.

Mrs. Lindley always helped in the activities of the Presbyterian Ladies'
Aid, a live organization, which managed through many years to raise a big part
of the money needed to keep a minister in the town. She also wrote the local
news for the Leola paper, "The McPherson County Herald." With store customers
and so many relatives news items were readily obtainable but faithfully
mailing them in, year after year, is something else. We could be critical and
offended when we "got left out" but Aunt Tab's column was the first thing we
looked at on Thursday's when the paper came.

Uncle Jay profited by faithful care too. During a long serious illness
Mrs. Lindley was able to attend to his needs until his death in the late
twenties.

                       SCHOOLS OF COURSE

The first school in Wetonka created no problem. Lincoln Township where
the town developed had several rural schools. One of the buildings with Mrs.
Will Leighty as teacher became Wetonka's first school. At five years I
couldn't attend, but Zola in the second grade, had a high opinion of Mrs.
Leighty. For some reason, pregnancy or the need to be at her farm home during
spring work, she gave up the school before vacation time. Mrs. James Calder
whose husband had started a hardware store, finished the term. The school
board must have decided a town should have nine months of school instead of
the then acceptable eight months for rural communities where farm parents had
work for big children by April. The farm students in the new town had work to
do too and had dropped out. Because of the reduced number of pupils I could
go. This may have marked me for life! I think I made Mrs. Calder an extra
class so not much time could be given me when she still had all eight grades.

The traumatic experience I haven't been able to forget came one afternoon
when it occurred to Kate Calder I should be busy. She brought a handful of
words in longhand and said I should arrange them in a sentence. A sentence!
Some printing I knew but these slanty, connected letters I could not read and
a sentence! I'm quite sure I knew I was to make the words say something, but I
didn't know the words.

The next year Miss Frances Ladd whose Scotch parents had been Edmunds
County pioneers south of town, taught the overcrowded, one room school. She
seemed tall to me and may have been. She looked neat in her shirtwaists and
long, red, dark skirts. I thought she spoiled her looks when, perplexed, she'd
draw her lower lip over the upper one and make her chin "all puckery." I
believe she loved little children, so we first graders fared very well.

When the Alldritt family moved from near Mina it was exciting for us but
I wonder how Miss Ladd felt when she had seven new students to stow away
somehow in the well filled room. No wonder she married Dick Jamison one of the
good carpenters who had been employed in town. They lived in Aberdeen after
that but we used to see Frances occasionally when she arrived on the train to
visit her parents at their farm.

One day when I was twelve or thirteen and helping Mrs. Frank Goodrich at
the hotel Mrs. Jamison came from the train to eat there. I thought she
wouldn't know me since I'd "grown up" but she said to Mrs. Goodrich, "I think
I know the young girl who is helping you." So I dared to visit with her and
her first little child.

Mr. Corrington, a violinist, accepted the teaching job in 1909 and 1910.
With our dad, Mr. Kindred, Mr. Calder or Mr. Von Eschen on the three member
school board Mr. Corrington could have been hired on his penmanship alone, and
they didn't know he could do beautiful Old English Printing. Mustached. very
adult and dignified. he managed the big school capably but even the parents
knew it to be too much to expect of one teacher. Another rural school building
not far away and not in use was moved into town and placed just east of the
first. Louisa Arnold, an experienced instructor from Wisconsin, began teaching
the first four grades. Just think of the progress: Wetonka, only two years old
and already two schools!

Mr. Corrington knew the "modern" requirements for seventh and eighth
grade students--that they didn't continue in grade school as long as parents
could spare them or until they married. He prepared upper grade students to
write seventh and eighth grade State examinations. Emory Kindred, a cousin of
the Canis Kindred's, and our sister Inez passed both of these tests and the
teacher made their graduation special by presenting large diplomas with their
names in English lettering. His beautiful signature was no slight addition to
the document either. George Hickman, the old time county superintendent, wrote
a proud, careful signature also. Inez's treasured paper or parchment last
reposed in a "Sweet Cuba" metal tobacco box of keepsakes, but stored in the
attic it surely was buried with the rubble left when our vacant post office
and home collapsed in a windstorm.

Of course Mr. Corrington qualified for better employment and the town
lost him. Louisa Arnold became the upper grade teacher the next year. We
younger pupils were again fortunate when Olga Belseth from south of town
became our teacher. The pretty, trim, shirtwaisted girl with the halo of
golden hair arranged over a hair form called a "rat"--we worshiped her!

Mr. Corrington's efforts toward progress caused Louisa more work: With
some eighth grade graduates there had to be a ninth grade and English,
algebra, ancient history and physical geography added to her schedule. Because
of her apparent youth she had some difficulty with the young adults but
discipline was a small matter compared with the loss of both school buildings
on a cold winter night.

Our brother Osie age twelve, Pete Geiberger or Louie Doell did school
janitor work. They were all quite young to sweep, dust, bring in fuel, and
"bank" heater fires for overnight without supervision. When coal is added and
then covered with ashes and the stove drafts closed before proper slow burning
has been established, an accumulation of coal gas can cause an explosion
forceful enough to blow open the heater door and scatter burning fuel.
Whatever the cause, the fire was real and well advanced in the upper grade
building before its discovery. The aroused townsmen did well to save desks and
contents. With pails and no water except at a distance, they could not save the
building or prevent the fire from spreading to the second one.

Miss "Belsit's" pupils had school the rest of the year in the good sized,
well lighted Odd Fellow's Hall above the First State Bank. The big kids were
taught in the never painted, one room building west of the hotel which had
been Frank Miller's harness and shoe repair shop and our temporary post office
after our building burned the previous summer.

With the complete loss of every building in which fire got a good start
the townspeople lost all faith in bucket brigades and began to think more of
fire resistant materials. Our parents used cement blocks which Mr. Fessenden
made at his farm for our new building on Main Street. The Presbyterian Church
and Heil's bank were of this material also.

For the new school the board selected good quality brick. They also
anticipated town growth and included such good supporting timbers that another
floor could have been added for future classrooms. The structure as finished
included two large teaching areas, a daylight basement with two playrooms,
quarters for a janitor, indoor toilets, a furnace room, and an adjoining coal
storage space. Ready for use in the fall of 1911 the new school was a source
of pleasure and community pride.

Olga Belseth again taught the younger pupils and Elbert McKenzie of
Elwood, Iowa, probably the most outstanding teacher the town ever had,
accepted the principalship at $75 a month! He was not a rugged person, but
good looking, well qualified in subject matter, and so much in control of his
emotions, a glance discouraged a country boy intent on mischief. A young woman
inclined toward romance would be so impersonally, but so well, treated she'd
accept the professional relationship, do her very best school work and hope to
find someone like him sometime in her own world.

What a contrast a middle-aged, old-school type substitute made the year
Mr. McKenzie had to be hospitalized for an appendectomy. To discipline the
quite grown-up boys, Mr. Smith felt fit enough to manhandle them. This
encouraged them to try to even scores with more infractions. When our
fourteen-year old brother was thrown down the entrance stairs even our dad
thought Mr. Smith had lost control. A young woman, Miss Walters, then came
from the normal in Aberdeen and peace prevailed--partly due to her good
personality and partly to the relief that Smith had been terminated!

Our pride in the brick building undiminished in September and October of
1911 reached its height on Halloween when a sort of dedication and party took
kids and parents through all the rooms and attic for entertainment and
Halloween games. Then with the first severe weather the hot air furnace proved
inadequate, the water pipes froze, and the toilets were useless. The nice
hinged storm windows blew off in the winds, and we were always cold. Large
jacketed room furnaces were added to heat the two big classrooms and no matter
what the discomfort good teaching prevailed.

Another local girl, Etta Ferguson, taught The second year with Elbert
McKenzie. I was one of Etta's fifth graders. I recall good programs and the
extracurricular activities Mr. McKenzie sponsored for his students and the
community lectures and entertainers of a lyceum course, a speaking contest to
select a representative at district competition in Aberdeen, and always the
monthly "literary society."

I liked all the lower grade teachers we had but I felt sorry to miss Mr.
McKenzie's teaching when Zola and Inez appreciated him so much. I did,
however, come under his discipline one day. It was partly Miss Belseth's
fault. She had introduced us to charcoal sketching. Then there'd been another
fire which left just a foundation and charred wood where Alldritt's butcher
shop had been. I noted the useable charred particles and gathered a sack full
on my way back to school after lunch. I thought only of using them in drawing
but I had the sack with me when a group of us went to a downstairs playroom.
In a recent program I'd taken a negro part in a dialog and the idea of trying
the charcoal on my face--just a few lines and crosses--came to me. The idea
caught on and it seemed to us it would be fun if all of us decorated our faces
for going into the classroom. We finally convinced ourselves it would be even
funnier to go all black. The bell rang and Miss Belseth was startled when her
fourth and fifth grade girls came in colored! She allowed us quite a bit of
freedom but she did think this behavior required the principal's assistance.
I'm quite sure he had seen us too as he stood by his classroom door
supervising students returning to his room. Miss Belseth said she'd ask Mr.
McKenzie what he thought of our behavior. I really believe both teachers
struggled to keep straight faces. He said, and not unkindly, "Now, this was
fun, but we don't want it to happen again. You'll need to go where you can
clean up before returning to your room."

So we went to Von Eschen's which was close and where children were always
welcome. Cleaning up, not an easy job, took some time but finally with some
black still showing about our ears and hairlines we returned to school. Like
Mr. McKenzie said, "It was fun" but we had no intention of trying it again.

Wetonka had three teachers for the 1913-1914 term. Floss and Ferne
Heagley, daughters of George Heagley, the lay minister, of the Church of the
Brethren in the "Dunkard" community north and east of town, taught primary and
intermediate grades. A widow, Mrs. Murphy, from Iowa taught seventh and eighth
grades besides whatever high school subjects required that year. She was
talented in music and Floss a young beginning teacher but we intermediate
grade pupils were most fortunate. Fern Heagley, experienced and dedicated,
followed the prescribed courses and enriched the program with supplementary
booklets, singing and skipping games, and even a special luncheon one noon for
the sixth grade to which we brought food we'd made ourselves. Table setting
and etiquette were stressed and Ferne said a blessing to begin the meal after
donning her neat little white net prayer cap. She became a missionary in the
Orient the next year. Mrs. Murphy's style and music won over the older
students, the entertainments were superior, and one would say we had a good
school year.

Not so the next! Although Clara Schuchardt from near Leola and Elga
Carlson from south of town were efficient primary and intermediate teachers,
there had been no one found to teach the advanced grades at the time school
should start. There would be no eighth grade class until farm boys returned to
school but seventh graders must be taught so we were assigned to Miss
Carlson's room. Everything was pleasant for us until the board decided to fill
the teaching vacancy by hiring Gordon B. Currie a substitute minister who had
served the Presbyterians during the summer. Somewhat charming a good speaker
he had been convincing enough to be accepted as an educated churchman so why
not accept him as the needed teacher? Then we had a teacher and a few high
school students ready to leave fall work and enter school. That's when
"Reverend" Currie got us seventh graders. The high school students were
disillusioned early when they found Gordon B. couldn't solve their algebra
problems. The seventh grade was not enthusiastic about Mr. Currie's math
either. If fractions bothered him, what did he know? Well, he had his good
speech flavored with his Scotch accent and a high regard for some
poetry--Gray's Elegy in particular. When we didn't memorize a suitable portion
of the poem we were impatiently reprimanded and kept after hours until each of
us could recite our assigned two stanzas.

Evidently we complained enough about the man to discourage any leniency
on the part of the school board, or they were obliged to replace him when he
could produce no teaching credentials. He had been less than discreet as a
minister too and for all his shortcomings he was free to leave town, but we
had to stay and put up with G. W. Courts from Highmore who replaced him.

At least Mr. Courts knew his subject matter, and quite a bit that was not
in the books! When he reported to a high school class about a doctor who had
so skillfully operated upon himself that he gave birth to a child, we not only
disbelieved him but thought the subject obscene. I don't know how the large
boys received this educational material--I think they were more interested in
being men than in becoming mothers.

Unlike the muscular substitute of 1912 who had ideas of subduing young
huskies, Mr. Courts overlooked obvious misbehavior on the part of young men
and was severe with girls. He had a way of trotting softly on the balls of his
feet and arriving unexpectedly behind or beside us. When he slipped up behind
my sister, Zola, and pulled her braid for no apparent reason, she didn't
attend the remainder of the term. Quite a few other high-school students had
dropped out by that time. Only Jewel Fyke remained at last of the ninth and
tenth grades.

With the year nearly finished school should have been bearable. It hadn't
been pleasant and there are no memories of good programs or activities so our
surprising behavior on a warm, sunny day may be understandable. We seventh
grade girls who went home for lunch happened to meet on the way back and we
were not hurrying! We stopped at Alidritt's for Lepha shortly before the bell
rang. Mrs. Alldritt asked, "Isn't that the one o'clock bell?"

"Oh, no, it's the first bell," we lied, apparently agreed on being late
with no previous planning. When we left Alldritt's we went by way of Von
Eschens straw stack instead of directly to school. The stack was no longer
high and fresh as in the fall. Disappointed, after a bit more time in the sun,
we decided to go on to school. We met Jewel Fyke coming out of the building
with her lunch pail.

"What's the matter, Jewel?" we asked.

She explained, "No one came back but Lester and Lloyd and I wasn't going
to be the only girl with the old Ogre."

Lester and Lloyd were the small sized seventh grade boys and "Ogre" was
our pet name for Mr. Courts. I think Zola had thought of it. We agreed it
fitted much better than "George W." That might even have been "George
Washington" and unthinkable.

I said, "Oh, Jewel, come back with us. We're all going in now."

Inside I went as far as our room's second door where I could see the
clock. I could see G. W., too, at his desk and his face was very red, really
somewhat purple as Zola had indicated when she said, "He turns purple with
rage."

I said, "Oh-h-h, it's after one!" as though I hadn't known it. I think
this boldness convinced Mr. C. I had been the leader in our taking the
extended noon hour. We tiptoed to our seats and the afternoon classes went on
as usual. I don't recall any punishment assigned at this time, but we may have
lost the afternoon recess. However, in our last period when we were reading
selections aloud I was stopped abruptly with a "That will do!"

Surprised, I said, "Oh". Now "Oh" can say a lot of things and it was not
a simple aspiration as the students reported it. I'm sure it must have been an
insolent, smarty "Oh" with a shrug and head toss, because I hadn't liked being
interrupted in mid sentence. G. W. said I was to stay after 4 p.m. which I
did. When he dismissed me I left through the door to the hall closet to pick
up my sunbonnet or hat. All the kids including the boys had stayed and my good
friend, Genevieve, banged the hall door shut after me. I knew that to be an
error since Courts at his desk would surely believe I'd done it. He moved
quickly through the door behind his chair and grabbed me, propelled me back
into the room, and pushed me into my desk. Then he had many things to say to
me with a lot of accusations. I had so little respect for him tears were no
part of my reaction. I felt only anger and replied with an old-fashioned
expression I'd heard Mother say once, "You know I was not the only fly in the
gravy." He became more angry and the scolding continued for a time before he
again said I could leave.

This time most of the kids had gone. Those left said Lester and others
had gone to get my father. Bless Lester! He was an outspoken defender, but I
doubted this being a right move. I wasn't afraid of Courts but I thought his
punishment unjust. Dad also thought that after listening to the students. He
waited for the teacher to come to Main Street where he had a room in the rear
of "Hamilton's Bank". There Dad had been verbally severe with the man! I think
it's just as well I didn't hear that. I preferred Dad's strap corrections to
his voiced opinions and I'd heard him set some adults straight on things
before. What a switch if I'd turned out to be sorry for Courts! I don't
remember any discussion of the incident at home, but I think the teacher kept
all in his mind for further action.

The last week of school we wrote the South Dakota examinations prepared
by the Department of Education for promotion to the eighth grade. In our
school and in most schools of the area other students taught in the same class
room were given the two days off, but must attend the remaining days of a
term. Seventh and eighth graders were not required to return to school after
writing these finals. G. W. announced we were to come back to school! I
suppose we might have complied but for the lovely day which followed our
tests. Lepha and I were given permission by our parents to ride with Mr. and
Mrs. Alldritt in their little Metz car. Sitting on the hard little back seat
built over the gas tank we went from farm to farm south of town where Mr.
Alldritt bought cattle for the St. Paul market. At Noah Bloomster's there was
a new piano and I played "Robins Return" which I had just memorized.

That day at school Mr. Courts gave tests of his own making to our
classmates. He graded the papers and entered his grades on report cards,
passing some who hadn't done well on the state examinations. The next day,
Saturday, just before train time, he turned in the school ledger at the
Alldritt home since Mr. Alldritt was the school board clerk. He then hurried
to the train. When Mrs. Alldritt looked through the ledger she was startled to
see after Lepha's name and mine, "Final examinations not taken." He had,
however, dutifully mailed all the test papers to the county superintendent in
Leola but on Lepha's and mine he had written, "Unworthy pupils should not be
promoted."

One can only speculate what conclusions the man hoped the superintendent
would reach from the notation of our unworthiness. We had surely reviewed the
years work at home and prepared ourselves for the drawing test when that
subject had not been taught at school. Lepha's brother who had been teaching
in the county called on the superintendent and described the character of the
teacher and the background of this particular vindictiveness. In due time the
reports came with seventh grade certificates and the grades were good.

Then we had a carefree time before another worry developed: Mrs.
Liggett's step-father had been hired to teach the 7th, 8th, 9th and 10th
grades! After Old Courts another old man with one foot in the grave! Why he's
a grandfather! At least a step-grandfather.

When William P. Barrett arrived from Iowa for school he brought a
beautiful daughter about twelve years old. His wife and their young son had
remained in Iowa at her mother's home. The other teachers that year were Clara
Schuchardt, again for the primary room and Dora Belseth, the younger sister of
Olga who had taught the lower grades from 1910 to 1912.

We went to school in doubt but daily our respect grew for the trim older
man with the lively ideas. We lined up to march into the classroom, we marked
time like veteran soldiers with our knees rigid, we took turns playing for
marching and singing. All who could play had a chance but Winnifred Barrett
had not had many lessons and on her days we marched to our own drums. However,
Winnifred could sing. At one of the early programs she sang, "Has Anybody Here
Seen Rover?" an old popular piece we had. She couldn't whistle, and I'd been
trying to, so Mr. Barrett made it a duet. There had to be some sound during
the whistling part.

At the end of Mr. Barrett's first year he asked Lepha, my cousin Adeline,
and me to learn poems for the afternoon program which would be attended by
mothers mostly. We got "Heart Throbs" from the bookcase and made copies of
poems we already knew quite well. Adeline recited "The Last Chord" in her
pleasant voice. I learned "Beautiful Hands", Ellen M. Gates' verses honoring
her mother, but I found out I could not get through it without choking up. I
hoped Mother wouldn't be able to leave her post office work that afternoon but
she came. She was only thirty-eight but I knew I'd never get past the third
stanza:

"Such beautiful, beautiful hands!
They're growing feeble now 
And time and pain have left their mark
On hand and heart and brow."

I begged Mother to go home after lunch. I told her I'd never get through
my recitation if she stayed. I think she was hurt but she left, and I did get
as far as the last four lines before the tears came and speech became
difficult:

"When crystal streams through endless years
Flow over golden sands
And where the old are young again
I'll clasp my mother's hands."

I don't think anyone could have heard the last line.

We never had reason to question Mr. Barrett's qualifications to teach the
subject matter required in all courses. After many years of teaching he could
add background and related information to our textbooks content, and could be
criticized only in his use of time. Like many teachers he'd find dismissal
time approaching and need to hurry through or skip classes scheduled for the
last periods. We worried eighth grade girls gathered all the old state test
questions our brothers and sisters had saved to prepare for civics, South
Dakota history, and drawing which had suffered the most for time. We knew
about the president's cabinet, about Sitting Bull, and that a centered,
conventional, circle design was wanted if the drawing test said, "Draw a
rosette."

Mr. Barrett had a strong speaking voice and a rather quick temper. A firm
reprimand usually sufficed to control students, but when I disturbed him one
day I had intended no offense and had to struggle to hold back tears.

Our hardcoal (anthracite) burner had leaked carbon monoxide gas in the
night. We were sick in the morning! (We could have been dead.) I stayed home
from school but Adeline who had slept with me went. She reported at noon that
she had felt better outside in the fresh air. I dressed for school then and
arrived without difficulty except for being somewhat unsteady. Mr. Barrett
came up from his basement rooms just as I entered the building. He said, "How
are you?"

I said, "Well, I'm all here." I didn't mean to be rude. I didn't want
sympathy, or to complain about being dizzy.

Annoyed, the teacher said, "Doesn't a civil question deserve a civil
answer?" I was hurt but also aware of concern. I think I would have
understood, "Doesn't a serious question deserve a serious answer?" better. The
question itself points up the difference between the two middle-aged men
instructors: G. W. Courts and W. P. Barrett. Mr. Courts would not have asked,
"How are you?"

The first year in Wetonka, Winnifred and her father lived in the simple
quarters provided by the Floete Lumber Company for their agent. Since the
first floor office space was no longer in use and there was no heat there or
water piped into the building, the upstairs was neither comfortable nor
convenient. I stayed overnight with Winnifred and appreciated Mr. Barrett's
good tasting potatoes. In the morning we had crumbles--the first of that dry
breakfast food I'd had. The next fall Mrs. Barrett and the handsome small boy
came for a time and the family lived in part of the school basement.

Clara Schuchardt exchanged time with Mr. Barrett and taught Palmer Method
penmanship in our room. We enjoyed all the practice ovals and push-pull
exercises. Then Mr. Barrett who could write badly with either hand ended the
exchange teaching and took over the writing drills himself. After all he had
an older set of loosening up pen movements and we enjoyed these, too. Just any
old spare time we were apt to cover a fresh sheet with Spencerian doodles and
offer it for display.

The second year of Mr. Barrett's principalship a pretty red-head, Ila
Ritchie, taught the primary grades and the numerous 1910 - 1911 babies
including our youngest brother entered school. It was the first of many terms
for the beloved Ila who taught first as Miss Ritchie and later as Ila Ferguson
when she married Bill Ferguson from south of town.

During the years 1915 through 1917 Miss Belseth (Dora) started our Camp
Fire organization. For some reason the group felt a need for capital and put
on the play, "Deacon Dubbs" for profit. Since our "one-night stand" came at
the end of school Mr. Barrett helped at the final rehearsals as he could so
ably do, and there needed to be no other school entertainment. The years had
been pleasant and at our ages the skating, sleigh rides and occasional dances
were important. One didn't go to the dance with a boy friend, but the one who
invited you to midnight supper at Kindred's restaurant walked home with you at
the close of the dance. That was like a date. For my sisters, cousin and me the
walk was just across the street and around to the back door.

Dora Belseth married Harold Alldritt after school ended and went to live
on his claim at Watauga, South Dakota. Mr. Barrett could not be hired again
because he needed a better South Dakota certificate to continue in upper grade
work in a town school. He surely could have qualified by writing an
examination if it hadn't been a state policy at the time to make it difficult
for a teacher who'd had no recent refresher courses in Education to pass.

Ila Ritchie taught the primary grades again in the fall of 1917 and Grace
Duggan another good looking girl came to be the intermediate teacher. We older
pupils had Virgil Carmichael from Indiana. He had a bald spot, although not
old, he stammered some, and a wart grew on the top of his head. If we high
school students felt let down by the country boy teacher, it surely must have
been discouraging for his attractive co-workers.

However, Virgil's teaching proved satisfactory, and his Indiana speech
only as amusing to us as our western talk may have been to him. We weren't
used to hearing about "a pretty day" with the adjective given its short "e"
sound. Our "pretty" had a short "i" sound.

When Carmichael didn't receive a suitable certificate toward the end of
the term the school board sympathized and told him they'd like him to continue
teaching. To their generous offer he replied,

"Y-y-you are l-looking after y-your interests and I'm l-looking after mine and
I p-p-plan to go back to P-Purdue to school." This he did and it must have
been disconcerting considering the types of substitutes Wetonka had found in
previous years. The problem, no doubt, arose because State Certificates
required for high school teaching were not plentiful and the salary not
attractive.

Since the show must go on, Miss Duggan substituted in our room and a
local woman filled in for her. Then Miss Ethel Gipe came to be our regular
teacher. She had been attending the Normal in Aberdeen where she lived.

What a faculty we had! Three beautiful young women. I'm sure we were
inspired to emulate them and be more careful of our grooming. They all seemed
interested in us and tried to advise us about future schooling. I recall no
difficulties at school. There were few boys in the upper room and none seemed
adult. When you are in the tenth grade and that is the extent of teaching in
the school, there are no "big boys" left. You are big girls but the boys are
just classmates you've been tolerating during the school years.

It was a year of change and finality: The war in Europe seemed ever
closer. Our Camp Fire organization had become a Red Cross knitting group, the
townsmen drilled with mock guns of wood in the hall, and men took turns, in
pairs, guarding the town at night to be ready for invading Germans! There were
few dances. Some young married couples attended these with us high school
students and the music might be that of the Edison cabinet machine the Yeoman
Lodge had bought. A really special time would be a holiday dance when young
men already trained as soldiers were home on leave. We thought them
magnificent in their trim uniforms. Ila Ritchie and Bill Ferguson were
engaged. He thought he must enlist when the United States entered the war. Our
brother quarreled with our Dad and he enlisted.

So soon young men from the town and farms, that we had known well, were
at the front in France. We wrote to them and sent some packages of treats.

It rained on the last day of school so we had the "picnic" in the old
Wetonka Mercantile below the hall. The store stood empty because even the
Yeagers, a Jewish family, had not been able to make it pay, and it had not yet
become "The Hardware".

It was a last day of school but I had two pleasant years of school later
in the building. After I had attended "Northern Normal and Industrial School"
in Aberdeen two years and taught a year at the Bielke School east of town, I
intended to return to the normal but an unexpected vacancy in the Wetonka
Intermediate room in the fall of 1921 seemed a good place to fill. That year I
taught with Mayme Moyle and Ila Ritchie Ferguson and inherited the 1910-1911
babies Ila had started as first graders.

When I had been back to "Northern" again and acquired a "State
Certificate" I spent my most pleasant teaching year as principal and teacher
of the high school students. It was the first year Wetonka had accredited high
school work and the first year four teachers were needed. Although there were
nearly one hundred in school only nine high school students were enrolled:
Ellsworth Chittick, Douglas Chittick, Bernice Hamilton, Grace Hamilton, Walter
Heil, Ivan Kindred, Thelma Krueger, Barbara Schlenker, and Leonard Von Eschen.
I had known all of them except Barbara most of their lives. Douglas and
Leonard were not first year high school boys but had returned to school where
they could take two courses with the ninth grade and study. Douglas took two
subjects "by extension" from "the Normal" which he had attended before working
at the First State Bank. They were sincere and superior students that any
teacher would treasure.

There were a number of terms of school in the town after that before it
became more practical to pay the tuition for high school students who attended
four year high schools of their choice. Later as the town population declined,
fewer teachers were required until at last a local girl, Alice Pendry, taught
alone and the town used one room in the sturdy 1911 building as a classroom.
Did I say "at last"? At last there is no school--a bus from Leola picks up the
children of the area.

                        RELIGION BY RAIL

During six years of my life I went to no church so I don't think I
qualified as an orthodox Christian. Kneeling by the bed at night I said, "Now
I lay me down to sleep...", as I'd been taught but depended upon the family
members to rescue me from disaster. I surely didn't qualify as a born again
Christian. Then in the new town of Wetonka in 1907 things began to change in
the name of the Lord. Our dad had as definite ideas about religion and human
behavior as he had about politics and history. On an overnight trip to
Aberdeen he shared a room at The Askew House, a small hotel popular with rural
people. His roommate, a Callaghan boy from Westport, had been one of his first
students. Dad was impressed by the number of special prayers the young man
knew. One evening after that he said, "These children should be learning more
prayers," and at bedtime he taught us the Lord's Prayer which we added,
thereafter, to our nightly chores.

In the summer of 1908 the train brought a special car, a chapel car,
which remained on the "side track" for several days before being moved on to
Leola. It must have been an especially arranged Pullman with roomettes,
kitchen and dining facilities for the two priests and Negro cook; and the
passenger area altered to serve as a chapel. The mission services and may have
made enough Catholics lived in the area establishment of a local church.
renewal for country people long without a church and a time to baptize babies.

Dad told Mother she should be baptized and although I heard no argument
over this, Mother must have felt it unnecessary. She had been thoroughly
done--dipped in the Elm River by the Methodists--pretty dress and all. In
Westport she had attended the Methodist Church near our home and played the
organ.

'It's a good time to be baptized along with Sylvia and the baby," Dad said.
That special baby, Ambrose! He had already been on a train trip and stayed
overnight at The Askew House when Askew's had their silver anniversary.

Somewhat reluctantly Mother agreed and they prepared for the big day. Dad
asked a good German couple to be sponsors--the J. Hobart Dolls. They'd had a
family of sixteen children and our parents knew some of the older ones from
Westport when two of the daughters clerked in our store.

At the appointed time we went to the interesting chapel car and the
priest, Father J. J. "0'Neill, gave the sacrament to Mother, the little
brother and me. I didn't understand the words, but I remember the water and
salt. I felt very important to be included in this special service with Mother
and our baby. Now I was baptised as the older kids had been when priests came
to Westport before I was born. The Certificate of Baptism gives the date as
June 15, 1908 and names the place, St. Anthony's Chapel Car.

The priests gave mother a beautiful leather prayer book with padded
covers. We still have it. Later Mother baked a special angel food cake with
strawberry candies on top, for the fathers. At the Presbyterian Sunday School
held in our upstairs which served as Wetonka's first hall, I used to get a
pretty card with the lesson printed on it, but these priests knew I couldn't
read a prayer book.

Home again from the chapel and in play clothes I joined my little friend,
Jimmy Von Eschen, at the old yard swing. I had become a Christian and Jimmie
may have been a pagan, technically, but neither of us knew it.

Mother felt better about her renewed baptismal vows after she met the
priests and other interested Catholics. There had been no church to attend
and, as she said, she knew little about Catholicism. How tearfully glad she
must have been to receive an understanding letter from her intelligent,
Methodist father commending her step and assuring her he thought it best that
there be a single church affiliation in a family. Her grandchildren really
converted her. In later years, attending St. Joseph's Church in Spearfish and
observing Father Sobolewski's dedication and influence, she said, "There has
to be something to a faith which does so much for young people."

                  HARDWARE AND GENTLE DEALERS

With all the lumber, lime, cement, and plaster the Hawkeye and Floete
companies provided for the Wetonka community's growth there had to be
hardware. The first store for such goods was built by James Calder. He and his
brother, Adam, from Aberdeen shire, Scotland, had been working on farms south
of the new town. James' wife, Kate Hills, of a pioneer family had been a rural
teacher.

At the time of James and Kate's store venture they had two small boys,
Lyman and Charles. Three more boys, Ronald, John, and Gordon, were born later
at the farm they operated northwest of town.

The first Christmas Program of the new village was held in Calder's
building and it may not have been stocked as a store at the time. I was not
old enough for school but I recall being taught a poem by my mother. It was to
be recited at an entertainment and I must speak loud! At the gathering I sat
close to Mamma and when my name was called she sent me up front with the
words, "Be sure to speak loudly." For a bashful five-year-old just going
forward represented achievement. I'm certain I whispered the words and ran
back to safety by Mother. I had noticed no change in our mother but to others
her condition would be obvious. Two days after Christmas our second brother,
Mother's fifth child, arrived. She was not quite thirty.

Mrs. Calder finished Wetonka's first term of school for Mrs. Will Leighty
who asked to be relieved of the teaching assignment to join her husband at
their farm. I was allowed to attend these last weeks of school since older
boys had dropped out to work on farms and the attendance was down.

I have been told that Mr. Calder had had experience in store work and ran
a good business but they did not keep the store long. A night fire, in the
building, brought the townsmen out in force and they successfully fought the
flames. When the fire flared again the sleeping volunteers were unconscious of
the disaster until the flames were too advanced for a water pail crew.

This was the town's first destructive fire. One got sympathy if no
insurance eased the pain but fell under suspicion when a pay off occurred.
However, no matter what gossip might suggest, no official statement was ever
made against a neighbor. Our parents "lucked out" in their 1910 fire. Our dad
had just answered a letter about renewal by saying, "I do not want the policy
at the higher rate you have quoted." What a time to have a fire! No insurance.
but no suggestion of arson either.

Calder's gave up as merchants after their loss. They built up a
substantial farm three quarters of a mile northwest of town and this remained
their home until their move to Twin Falls, Idaho, in the 1930's.

Mr. and Mrs. Calder were both prominent in community affairs. Jim served
as a member of the school board and often took part in debates at the
"Literary Society" which seemed to get organized each fall with the beginning
of school. It was the P.T.A. of the era and the forerunner of the Community
Club. Mrs. Calder made worthwhile contributions to the society's programs and
actively took part in Presbyterian Church affairs--it's holiday entertainments
and work at the Ladies Aide. Their farm had a border of Russian Olive trees
when they were rare in Dakota and the yard was bright with flowers. Sometimes
the only flowers available for a funeral were sweet peas and petunias from Kate
Calder's garden.

The next hardware store built at the same location had a longer life. The
owner, John B. Hamilton, and his wife Gertrude Gauss Hamilton, had lived
previously in Iowa. John B. had been a high school teacher in Belle Fourche,
South Dakota, for a time. An artistic penman he often left a chalk drawing of
a bird on the blackboard of a classroom when there had been an adult meeting
there. These were done in the style of sketches found in old "copy books" for
practice with shading pens.

Hamilton's had one boy, Gary, about four years of age--a sharp little boy
who early showed a talent for drawing. A daughter, Bernice, born in 1910,
seemed to be gifted in music. Irwin, the 1912 baby, became one of my good
little fourth graders in 1921. Gary, when a student at the "Normal" in
Aberdeen in the twenties sometimes entertained as a cartoonist at student
functions. He also played trombone in the college band. As a resident of
Chicago later he played with the Chicago Symphony.

When the First National Bank of Aberdeen built a branch bank in Wetonka,
Mr. Hamilton became its cashier. The family continued to live upstairs over
the store until they built a home north of the school. At this time a young
farmer from Iowa, Irving Beer, operated the store. He owned it later if not at
the beginning. An agreeable person, although gruff sounding, his long red neck
and prominent Adam's apple soon earned him the popular name of Turk. Bachelor
Turk married Mabel Fessenden and marriage took them back to Iowa where Turk
again farmed.

Another bachelor Frank Miller, was long a part of the hardware scene but
he fixed shoes in the rear of the store. His work fascinated me and I, with
more enthusiasm than skill, attempted half-soles myself. The leather soles and
tacks were for sale and we had a butcher knife, a file and a shoe stand at
home. My edges weren't as smooth as Frank's but the thick soles made my skates
stay on better. I don't know how pleased my chum's parents were when I fixed
her shoes, but her mother trusted us and gave us the money for the leather.

Frank was also an amateur photographer. Once my sister Zola and I
surprised Frank at his home and asked that he photograph our baby. Mother
seemed only half pleased when we brought a print home after Frank developed
it. She thought she and the baby should have been consulted and the baby
dressed better for a portrait. It turned out to be the only picture we had of
Marshall as an infant and I'm glad for our impulsive action.

The hardware establishment never seemed to prosper. It could be that the
larger general store built by Heil's offered stiff competition, and it could
be the town didn't need two such stores after the initial rush of building
subsided. Also rural mail delivery, parcel post, and the growth of mail order
firms hurt local business.

There were pretty dishes and some gift items in both stores. Our
"rainbow" dishes or "carnival glass" came from Turk's, also the mirror, comb
and brush set in its satin lined box which Zola received as a prize. Miss
Belseth gave it to her at the end of school and I was completely envious. I
withdrew my $1.82 bank account which Mr. Hamilton's bank had been keeping for
me, and bought the remaining set. The next year Miss Belseth gave me a ring
with three sets from Lindley's Store. It had been marked $3.50! If I'd only
known my turn would come I might not have felt the prize thing unjust and
could have continued as a capitalist.

An attractive young boy from Ipswich, Lynn Jones, took care of the
hardware store one summer, and Mae Jones, a younger sister, was Zola's good
friend. If their father, Ed Jones, an auctioneer, owned the store he disposed
of it in a short time. After that summer Mr. and Mrs. Will Crain had the
business and lived in the apartment above it. They later had a store in Timber
Lake.

Mr. and Mrs. Harvey Selser were running Wetonka's hardware when Dad
bought our first power washer--hand and foot power. It had a wood tub and
washing dolly. It trapped unwilling children for fifteen minute intervals and
might call for repeats depending on how many machine loads there were that
day. No matter its drawbacks it saved Mother a lot of hand rubbing. She had
never found us too reliable at the washboard.

Rei Hills, Mrs. Calder's single brother, owned the store when he married
Greta Woodward, a granddaughter of J. G. Wetrick--the diminutive Swiss farmer
from north of town who raised chickens and made regular deliveries to
restaurants and markets in Aberdeen. He always turned with several steps "in
place" to see in a different direction because he had a stiff neck from an
accident caused by runaway horses. He didn't waste words. A pithy remark in
his flat, nasal voice, that I recall, pointed out a Christian inconsistency,
"People are always talking about heaven, but I don't see any very anxious to
go there." Greta and Rei had a farm home afterwards at Hecla but joined other
Wetonka people at Twin Falls, Idaho, during the depression.

The old building really bloomed in the 1920's as a store worthy of a
bigger town when it became something else. As "Lohner's" with groceries, fresh
vegetables and fruit, refrigerated meat, dry goods, and gifts it attracted
customers who might have driven to larger markets. Tom and Olive Lohner and
Tom's father were excellent sales people and the store continued until 1931
when fire on a windy and cold spring night destroyed the old building, the
butcher shop Ed Mass had built, and the pool hall just west of it.

During these years Heil's old "Merchantile" had become the hardware store
with James McIntyre and later Fred Kruse as storekeepers. Mr. McIntyre and Lou
Geisler had come to Wetonka to operate the old Hawkeye Elevator long owned and
run by Joe Lorber and Joe Lynch.

As the town failed with business passing to larger markets and the
depression forcing people to seek jobs elsewhere, the hardware survived
precariously. Finally owned by Art Quick he kept it open as a sort of
pastime--a place to be and visit with cronies on Main Street. Art had farmed
in the community before the birth of the town and managed to remain solvent
through dry years, wet years, booms and bank failures. Among other properties
his store outlived him. Heil's "Wetonka Merchantile and Opera House" now known
as "the old hardware", boarded up, neglected, Wetonka still has it or did at
the time of the picture in 1971.

All buildings in the area, vacant and unwanted, are under benevolent
threat of the Hutterite Community located on the former Ed Hamilton farm.
These thrifty and conservative people help the town to appear less ghostly by
buying up structures containing good materials. These they move to the
settlement for use or to salvage the lumber. For this reason, even as we mourn
the apparent decay of the old store and dance hall, the silence of the old
orchestras and rhythm of the dancers, we can hope the building may yet become
a fine shop or storage house west of town.

                         CHRISTMAS 1910

We lived in the "old place", the post office, store, and home, built
three years before in the new railroad town. There was a barn for the
livestock our postmaster-grain-buyer-father needed for his extra-curricular
farming, and a machine shed for McCormick implements he sold.

On the hot July afternoon, Mr. Vincent, the farm customer said, "Ed,
there's a fire in town. Looks like your place." Dad regretted the reply he'd
recently sent answering an insurance notice as he hurried his team, "I do not
want this policy at the new rate you have quoted." What a time to have a fire!

What a fire! It had burned through the roof and beyond control.
Two-year-old Ambrose, completely fascinated with matches since the Fourth had
found an ideal situation upstairs--loose matches on the hired man's bedside
table and a straw tick uncovered for airing. Choking he emerged from the
stairway. "All 'moke upstairs," he reported to ten-year-old Zola. The thick
smoke discouraged even Mother and her pail of water.

The town men dragged out the post-office equipment, mail, and our
furniture--little clothing; it was upstairs. Dad and an athletic friend, Ray
Morrison, risked their lives carrying supplies from the basement--the building
fell in behind them as they reached an exit with a last load.

Mother, quite sick, had no time for illness. What moments she could spare
she had spent at the new piano. Zola, two years older than I, evidently
understood why mother wore the pretty Mother Hubbard dresses again. Even at
seven she had said, "You can have the doll with the tin head. I want the dolly
we're to get at Christmas." Ambrose was that doll, but he had been late for
Christmas.

Our parents were so resourceful I didn't realize the trauma of the fire.
The next morning the post office and store were open for business in a small
building beside the hotel. That day Dad nailed together one of his "instant
sheds" where we camped while another building was erected--this time of cement
blocks and on Main Street. They must have had to borrow money but we heard no
grumbling over bad luck.

Mother found time between housework and post office duties to make our
Christmas dresses. Zola's and mine were alike. She sent no order for wool
serge but used dark blue poplin from our store. White silk braid was carefully
sewn on by hand. We were ready for the Christmas program or would be when the
rag curlers were removed from our hair. At fifteen, Inez could do that.

Mother rested after the heavy office work Christmas Eve. I took her the
little china mustard jar I had got her when she said she would not be able to
attend the entertainment.

At the combination school and church program, Zola hesitated over her
lines. She said, "When I saw them come in for Dr. Still I couldn't think
straight. Mrs. Doell left too." Mrs. Doell often went where there was illness,
but Mrs. Carlson had brought our little brother. After the distribution of
gifts we were told to go to Aunt Katie's for overnight. In the morning Osie
came breathlessly to announce, "We have three boys at home now!"

"What! Triplets?" Zola asked, but Osie meant himself, Ambrose, and a new
baby boy born fifteen minutes after midnight!

The boy made everything right--three girls and three boys. After all the
events of the year a Christmas baby!

In the next town, Dad's sister, Aunt Mary, hurried to tell our stylish
Aunt Grace, "Did you hear about Arline's Christmas present, a baby boy?"

"I'll bet Arline didn't think it much of a present," Aunt Grace said.

How wrong you were, Aunt Grace! None of the hardships of pregnancy marred
the enjoyment of the sixth child--a beautiful child with a happy disposition
who later showed talent in music. Mother felt she could have asked for no
greater blessing.

I'm sure the family recalls no finer Christmas.

                           MR. EARLE

Mr. Earle came from the north of Ireland. He did not come directly to
Wetonka and we never learned where he had been in the United States before
coming to our new town in 1908. Dad liked him immediately because he was Irish
and because he knew about poetry and other bookish things. They had long
discussions and shared original verse, but Mr. Earle volunteered little
information about himself. As time passed we often wondered why he came to
America. A graduate of Trinity College in Dublin and no longer a young man he
should have qualified for better work than exterior painting. That was his
first work in Wetonka. He painted the lovely sign on Stone and Lindley's
store--"WETONKA EXCHANGE" in old English lettering. He painted other signs and
buildings. He painted ours and made the brick designed metal sheeting look
really brick-like by carefully striping in white the design part which
represented mortar.

Because Mr. Earle was Irish we thought of him as one of the family. He
paid attention to young children and sought to entertain them by blowing upon
his hunting-case watch to open it. At six I knew about such watches, but a
cute swimming fowl he drew with one line and a dot intrigued me. That little
swan or goose has helped me throughout life -anytime I needed a quick figure
for an illustration or a graph Mr. Earle's bird came to mind. Then there were
the horseback rides on his knees accompanied with a song: For the gentle ride,
"This is the way the ladies go, the ladies go, the ladies go; this is the way
the ladies go, galloping, galloping, galloping." For the faster, rougher ride,
"This is the way the gentlemen go ..." and for the reckless high bounce, "This
is the way the cowboys go!..." This is sure-fire child entertainment and I
have often been sorry when I introduced it to a young friend who wanted it to
go on forever. Mr. Earle must surely have regretted at times that he started
it. The tune was that of "Here we go round the mulberry bush."

William Earle, a friendly, fair skinned, blue eyed, Irish type was not
rugged. One didn't associate him with grueling labor or ingenious solutions to
mechanical problems. Dad called him "Billy" and as Billy Earle he became a
town character. If he'd come to paint he remained to teach--rural schools,
mostly south of town.

During the summer of 1911 a new school was needed to replace the two
rural school buildings which had been serving as the two-room village school.
They had burned during the winter. The building under construction could have
been a proper, even pleasant school for that era, but the school board
economized on the heating plant, and Mr. Earle as supervisor knew no more than
the board members about heating and plumbing. Even in his own field of
painting he had to defend his blue ceilings and green walls with the
explanation, "It's like nature with the blue skies and the green grass." Billy
was well known by this time as a man very fond of drink. Some said, "If he'd
quit mixing the paint with the beer he'd do better." This marked the beginning
and the end of Mr. Earle's career as supervising architect. With the first
cold weather the hot air furnace, in a remote basement room, proved to be
completely inadequate; the plumbing was destroyed as the water froze, and
individual room heaters and outdoor toilet facilities were used ever after.
The brick building itself withstood the ravages of time and continued in use
until it became more economical to bus the school children to Leola.

During the long years Mr. Earle lived upstairs in the "section house" in
rooms once needed by railroad track repair men. Only one downstairs apartment
was required for the track foreman who directed both crews--or probably, more
accurately, one crew was able to do all the necessary work. The Durand family
lived below Billy in the south apartment. When he had imbibed too freely and
become noisy or cried out at imagined terrors, Mrs. Durand sometimes banged
the ceiling and told him off in her mule skinner's vocabulary. I liked her
very much and had learned to expect the colorful talk. Her older girl and I
were friends and I spent lots of time at their home. I do remember being
startled on their first day in town when Mrs. Durand expressed herself
characteristically on being told there was no place to rent except part of the
section house. My faith in this railroad bosses' home was not shaken by Mrs.
Durand's condemnation. I had beautiful memories of two super-grandma types,
Mrs. Frances and Mrs. Walker, who had lived in the north apartment and loved
all children. Mrs. Durand, herself, liked most people and especially those she
thought were having a difficult time in life. I feel sure Mr. Earle benefited
through her generosity in gifts of food and invitations to holiday meals, but
she did like to tell what an untidy place he kept upstairs. I never saw it.

Mr. Earle seemed little interested in women but one older widow who came
as school principal had romantic hopes. The younger man she favored was as
unaware of her designs on him as she was of Billy's attraction to her. One day
as the Durand girl and I played near their home he came downstairs with a note
which he asked us to take to Mrs. Murphy. What a note! Beautifully addressed,
yes, but the fold! We had never seen anything like it. One corner of the linen
paper which also served as an envelope came out in a shape like three squares
of a cross made of five squares. The only reason Mrs. Murphy got that epistle
free of juvenile censorship was that we could figure no way to get it open and
closed again without having evidence of our snoopiness. It might have been
only a neat solution of an algebra problem. Mrs. Murphy excelled in music and
had the respect of her students but she could have asked help on a tricky
problem. Billy was known to sit in a cloud of smoke from his old pipe and
eventually produce a proper step-by-step solution of a difficult math question.

At Mr. Earle's rural school programs he sometimes recited his own poem,
"David and Goliath". The Bible story written with Irish expressions and given
in Billy's brogue might even be said to improve on Bible history. He said he
had written it as a college student. I first remember hearing him recite it at
a program in the Odd Fellows' Hall. I was told it had been included in a book
of materials for use in lodge entertainments and marked anonymous. Mr. Earle
said he had no idea how it had been obtained for publication. Dad thought it
the best thing Billy had written.

In 1913 or 1914 Wetonka received approval for rural mail delivery. Mr.
Earle and two other men wrote the Civil Service examination in Leola in hopes
of qualifying for the carrier position. Billy, with his good education and
careful penmanship, received the highest rating and felt certain of the
appointment. He and his horse, Pearl ("Peril" as he said it), were soon in
service Monday, Wednesday and Friday on the north route; and Tuesday, Thursday
and Saturday on the south. True it had been called a "temporary appointment"
but we thought its a sure thing that the applicant with the highest score got
the job. Then the blow fell--Mr. Earle was not able to furnish citizenship
papers. He said they had been lost in the San Francisco fire in 1906 but this
didn't satisfy the Postal Department. Billy was out, but not Pearl: the new
carrier, Mr. Chittick, bought her and her colt and these two horses pulled the
buggy or sleigh thousands of miles before being replaced most days by
Chitticks' Ford. Actually, Mr. Earle escaped a very hard job he was not
physically capable of doing. Thirty mile trips on unimproved country roads can
be hazardous in stormy winter weather. Mr. Chittick, a rugged and able man,
surely sacrificed years of his life in doing this work. He died before
retirement age of a stroke which followed a bout with his stuck car in heavy
snow.

When old enough to work in "our post office" I asked one day about the
letter for a Mr. William Earle Pye. Mother said, "It's the way Mr. Earle's
letters from Ireland are addressed." He must have had to tell our parents his
full name when he first came to town, and as far as I know they told no one.
For some reason he wished to conceal his identity and this fact might explain
the lack of naturalization papers. If he had wanted citizenship, no doubt, his
true identity would have become known with whatever results he feared.

One summer my father and Mr. Earle had a partnership going. They sold
farm machinery. As "Friel and Earle", I think Dad furnished the credit rating
because he had sold implements before. Billy furnished the good little
blacksmith shop he had been using as a barn for his horse. The shop became the
office and parts department. Binder twine also needed to be stored inside and
I'm sure they made use of the long shed which had been moved from Main Street
and which had been part of the discontinued Floete Lumber Company's
facilities. It was there at the north end of the yard where the machinery was
kept.

The partnership seemed to work out since Billy had time to file invoices
and could be counted upon to write a proper letter if necessary. He did not
attempt high pressure sales pitches because he knew little about farming or
machinery. Farmers would have been scornful of the attempt. They knew what
they needed and came in to see "if you had it".

Dad was never able to give up farming on a small scale no matter what
else he did, but he showed up to assemble machines and check on the sales. In
the fall he had another partnership established with James Calder. As "Friel
and Calder" they were operating a threshing outfit. They had bought an old
steam outfit which could handle a lot of grain but often needed repairs. it
was an advantage to buy the parts needed for these repairs through "Friel and
Earle" at wholesale prices. After several orders including some expensive
equipment and no prompt settlement Billy "smoked" this over carefully and
concluded he'd be the one most likely not to profit from the orders for the
threshing rig. His memorable complaint amused our Dad, "If this keeps on, the
"firum" of "Friel and Calder" will probably bankrupt the "firum" of "Friel and
Earle". As usual they reached an acceptable agreement and remained friends,
but it was not a profitable venture. Bad times had come to the area after World
War I and many promissory notes received for machinery became worthless.

The "library incident" involving Mr. Earle took time to understand. Now I
think it not unlike a practice I have found helpful: If one is sure an
accomplished fact will be beneficial and harm no one, often the expedient way
is to work it out without permission--don't involve someone else -don't bring
it up for debate!

The "library" developed through the efforts of Leslie Lee Saunders, an
able revivalist minister who came to Leola and Wetonka for a series of evening
meetings. He contacted distant city congregations and reported a lack of the
essentials of culture in the two towns. Evidently books and funds were
solicited. Wetonka hated to acknowledge the deficiency but felt the evangelist
sincere in his opinion. We didn't understand the need for his bold signature
in every volume or the reason for heavy underscoring of single, not too
difficult, words throughout the pages. However, enthusiasm had grown with the
arrival of the contributions--two hundred fifty books and some money. Space
over the hardware store was obtained and at least one reading, table, some
chairs and lamps were purchased. The studious C. J. Hill took charge. This was
real progress but only briefly enjoyed. Who would continue to care for a few
books and supervise young people for the small fees and possible fines? The
next time the rooms above the store were needed as living quarters the books
had to go. Dad agreed to house the books in the post office since we were
gradually closing out the store goods and there were empty shelves. At first
we charged the fees and recorded the borrowers' names. We never collected
fines or worried about overdue books. Later we had a truly free library--no
fees, no fines. And the number of books even grew as we added strays and books
of our own after we'd read them. When my husband and I left Deadwood I added a
complete set of the books of Alexandre Dumas, the Elder. I found them in the
attic of the house we'd owned there.

In the early "Twenties" Mr. Earle was an untidy, elderly man. He had lost
some teeth and seemed always to have a loose one in front which he agitated
with his tongue. He had not lost his sense of humor: One day as my young
brother and I played a bunch of popular music we even vocalized the parody of
the time, "Hallelujah, I'm a Bum." Earle's familiar voice called from the post
office room, 'D'ye know that's a pious Methodist hymn y'are desecratin'?" We
knew this to be no rebuke and that he had enjoyed it. He was not concerned
about religion although we assumed he had been Episcopalian as a youth.

With the added years Billy must have found it difficult to find
employment as a teacher. He read quite a number of books and Mother noticed he
did not return all of them. She remembered book size packages he had sent to
Ireland, so she consulted the tedious postal regulations and found it her
right and duty to examine a parcel which aroused her suspicions. When she
opened Mr. Earle's next package, sure enough, it was one of the "Wetonka
Library" books! In private Mother showed Billy the remains of his wrappings
and their contents. He had, of course, no good explanation, so that, as far as
we know, is as far as we got as a traveling library. in Earle's way of
thinking he may have contrived a justification for sending books he had
enjoyed to those he felt would appreciate them, 'After all they are just
gathering dust here where few are reading these old classics."

Mr. Earle had left Wetonka one year when I came home after an absence.
"He's gone back to Ireland," I was told and that seemed good--he felt free to
go back. I hoped he could finish his life in comfort at a family home he had
not visited for years, but at Christmas a card came from him at St. John, New
Brunswick. The lovely poem he had enclosed spelled out my name with the first
letters of each line! In Minneapolis in 1974 my sister, Zola, showed me the
poem Mr. Earle had sent her--probably the same year. There had been no address
unless that of the hotel stationery was enough. I am not sure if I tried to
thank him. There was that question of what name to use. We thought, "Oh, he's
coming back," but he did not arrive. Then we wondered if he ever went to
Ireland.

In 1974 my sister and I had been to Dublin where the Pye name appears on
a number of city signs. The cab driver explained, "Oh, the Pye's are a big
radio and TV company here."

                       THE HILLS OF HOME

C. J. came first--a rotund, quiet, small man who looked over our scorched
little town and bought some land from Mr. Holmes. In August of 1910 I was
staying a few days with Genevieve Holmes because our home and clothing had
burned and Mrs. Holmes had volunteered to make me a dress. Mr. Hill spoke
interestingly at the table when he came to dinner but we children knew nothing
of the land deal and didn't know why he was there. In fact, no one ever knew
why Mr. Hill came.

He built a very small unpretentious house on his land and added some
out-buildings later. When his wife and little daughter came the family was
complete. It seemed they came for no reason except to live in Wetonka. All the
way from New York State to live in Wetonka! And keep some chickens and a cow!

C. J. could have been in his fifties and Mrs. Hill about twenty years
younger--a refined, pretty, neat, and spinsterish woman. The little girl of
four seemed delicate and precocious as one might expect the child of mature
and studious parents to be. As she grew older an untreated tooth problem
spoiled her appearance and she was not attractive. Nervous and excitable she
must have suffered in the rough give-and-take of the village school--so
different from her home life. I'm sure she was teased and laughed at, and I
hope there were enough kindly people about to make things right for her. I
remember that we laughed and told on her when she said she'd like to be a
horse. The pretty golden blond teacher, Olga Belseth, said, "Marcia means
she'd like to be a horse when it's time to play horse," and that we could
understand.

Because Hills were reserved, although friendly, very little was ever
learned about them. Marcia had been the surviving baby of a pair of twins, and
Mrs. Hill a second wife. There was nothing about their former home, work, or
education. They did not try for employment that would have paid salaries, but
were soon recognized as helpful in town activities. Mrs. Hill, talented as a
reader, seemed always able to find a different and interesting selection for
any school or church program. When a dedicated teacher got the older girls
interested in participating in a district speaking contest, off they all went
to get help and training from Mrs. Hill.

The home had always been a delightful place to visit. Although Genevieve
and I were older than Marcia we went to Hills to play and admired the quaint
and different things they had. I had never seen a down-filled satin quilt
before, or had cream puffs to eat, or watched a cupboard lowered by pulleys
into the cellar to keep the food cool.

Sometimes an entire group arrived at Hills to practice because Mrs. Hill
was teaching us a routine for a program at the Presbyterian Church. Sometimes
a class arrived at Hills without warning because a teacher assigned a debate
question. We had found out that C. J. had a fund of information and would
willingly dig out more. He'd give us a preliminary set-up and then invite us
back, one team at a time, for further help. He loved the adult debates at the
school "Literary Society" or community club. Usually a member of a team he was
not an effective speaker--his points were lost in his stuffy, small-voiced
delivery.

As time went on Marcia was growing up, Mrs. Hill was staying the same,
but C. J. was getting himself talked about. I didn't think it sounded so bad.
What if he did go about with a basket and pick up bits of wood and pieces of
coal? I couldn't always resist a piece of wood myself. It seemed wasteful for
an old dry board to lie in the street when those old railroad ties our parents
bought for kindling were almost impossible to break up.

"But that isn't all," one of the neighbor girls said, "Dad caught him in
a coal shed of the Floete Company and locked him in for a couple hours."

Another girl volunteered, "Yes, and he makes believe he puts money on the
plate at church when he really is taking some off."

A loyal friend suggested, "Well, that would be hard to prove, he could
have been making change, and it would be hard to prove he burned the little
house, but they do seem to have all their furnishings left for the new one."
The second residence--a ready-cut home from a mail order company--was much
nicer than the original.

By 1922 Mr. Hill had died and Marcia and her mother had a sale and left
Wetonka. They did not return to New York but went farther west to Colorado and
later to Oregon.

Many of us have wondered if the little sunburned plains town was a great
hardship for Mrs. Hill. It wouldn't have been for Marcia since she knew no
other life, but why did it have to be? Was C. J. a fugitive who purposely
sought out an obscure distant town to live on embezzled funds? Had they
inherited some money and planned to go west and live simply in retirement?
Could Mrs. Hill have been ill and advised to go to a dry climate in order to
survive? Maybe C. J., no longer well, accepted this village life as a possible
way for them to exist and remain independent.

This was a long time ago and none of us will find any answers now, but
for variety, it helped our little town that there were Hills.

                     CLAUDE CHITTICK FAMILY

That best portion of a good man's life--his little nameless, unremembered acts
of kindness.

- W. Wordsworth -

At the time it seemed too bad that someone, some stranger from Leola
eleven miles away, would get the mail carrier job which Mr. Earle had held
temporarily. Just three men wrote the Civil Service examination and Mr. Claude
Chittick had been second highest. Earle with his bookish, formal education and
careful penmanship was understandably a high scorer, but he had not been able
to prove United States citizenship so could not continue in the work. He sold
his equipment; his mare, Pearl; her colt, and his buggy to the new driver. The
Chittick family came from Leola and moved into Kindred's vacant house. It was
1914.

Claude, a Celtic type of Northern Ireland ancestry, had light skin, red
hair and mustache. His own parents had lived in Ontario. His wife, Helen, was
an artistic studious person with Scandinavian parents. She had been a rural
teacher. Later she served Wetonka well as substitute teacher and school board
member. There were three boys; Douglas, Ellsworth (Victor) and the baby,
Malcolm.

I remember the proper, older boys calling on us their first Sunday in
town. They wanted to do everything right so they did not come through the post
office entrance, but went around the building and knocked on the rear door. It
was the beginning of a long friendship that strengthened as the sterling
qualities of the parents became known and the children grew up to resemble
them. They have attained what seems like greater success than their parents
but only commensurate with the training they received and the characteristics
they inherited and developed in this-later period.

Claude's idea of Civil Service was just that--civil service. He had a
true love of people and favors for friends were a matter of course. Probably
few days passed without a patron on the mail route benefiting by his kindness
in some form--a stamp supplied, a package brought to the door, a lift along
the road, or a verbal message delivered between farms. I have not forgotten
the good turns done for us when we as adolescents cooled at the temporary
camps our dad set up where he put in crops. We'd take fresh meat, bread, and
vegetables along with canned goods when we went out from town with our dad on
Monday mornings, but Mother knew we'd need more bread and meat before the end
of the week. She dutifully stamped her food packages since she as postmaster
lived by postal regulations, but the regulations did not interfere with Claude
and his ideas of service.

Without clocks at the camps we sometimes guessed the sun time right and
got to the road to meet the mail. If not, the mail came down the pasture road
to us. "We thought we still had time to get to the box," we'd apologize, but
Claude brushed off the explanation and made light of his extra drive in and
out from the county road, and the gate opening, "I didn't want to leave the
meat at the gate on a hot day like this."

We always enjoyed seeing him but were especially glad one day when he
drove in. My older sister had been "first cook" that week. Already a young
teacher and no camper she had not got along well sleeping on the floor. She
had developed sciatica along with stomach flu and appreciated getting a ride
home "on the mail." Mother could square this with the postal rules because an
"assistant postmaster" could ride and "inspect" the mail route even though
passengers were not allowed. We girls were entered as assistants when Mother
thought we were old enough to write out money orders, but this didn't mean we
got paid!

At this time Mr. Chittick had a Ford touring car for normal driving but
must keep the horses for storm weather and deep snow. He also needed the
horses for substitutes like me. Sometimes I wondered if he laid off because he
had a number of vacation days each year or because he knew how much I liked to
earn money. Anyway I drove a few times, once with the oldest boy, Douglas, to
show me the way, but other times with the map of the route. Once the map and
my poor judgment got me and the mail service into trouble. The map indicated
straight ahead, but the tall sunflowers and water on the grade suggested a
detour. I continued on the grade until it was obvious there had been no
traffic that way. Pearl's grown colt, Billy, objected when I descended from
the buggy to turn him on the narrow road and a wheel scraped the body of the
vehicle. With a bound Billy accomplished the whole maneuver but the buggy lay
on its side in the sunflowers and Billy was out front and free. Immediately he
was sorry so he stood by and waited for me. He and I and the mail walked over
to Averill's farm to borrow a rig, but there were only women at the house and
they had much more important business on hand than helping to get the mail
around the north route. Threshers would be there for dinner and I must stay to
eat. After that the daughter, Helen, would take me into town when she went in
for supplies.

What a dinner! I really enjoyed it but I should have called home. When I
arrived in mid-afternoon my mother had worried since a farmer came in to
inquire what had happened on the mail route, "Chittick's buggy is overturned
on the road." I was well fed and not hurt so Mr. Chittick qualified as the one
person entitled to a grievance but he graciously explained that an attempt had
been made to deliver the mail and therefore he would be paid and I should
accept my substitute's voucher when it came. I wish I had been advised to
return the money because there surely was expense in righting the buggy and
getting it back to town and perhaps some harness repair. This, however, was
typical of Claude--thinking the other person right and being overgenerous.

It didn't follow that he was over-lenient with his own boys. They
received good training and expert counseling and had the ever present examples
of good character in their devoted parents. Before Claude's death all three
sons had finished college and were established in responsible jobs. There were
also some grandchildren.

While still on the job Claude worked too hard one winter day getting his
stalled car out of deep snow. In the evening the stroke came. When I saw him
at Christmas he had not recovered his speech but had let Helen know he wanted
the package sent to our nieces and nephews who had moved to Spearfish. He had
started the annual gift giving when there were only two children and continued
as the family grew to seven children and had moved across the state. He seemed
to be improving and was able to be up in a chair when the second and fatal
stroke ended his life. It was 1942.

This was, of course, a difficult time for Mrs. Chittick but fortunately
(Victor) Ellsworth, the second son, still lived in Wetonka. He had given up
teaching when a second mail carrier job became available. (Wetonka then had
daily mail service on both routes instead of one carrier serving the two
routes on alternate days.) Later when Ellsworth transferred to Aberdeen his
mother found an apartment there which she enjoyed. During her senior years
there was the long winter outing in Arizona where Ellsworth and his family
were living. In the fall of 1967 she had gone with Douglas to Fremont,
Nebraska, to the wedding of Malcolm's daughter, Nola. Douglas's daughters,
Karen and Kay, were on the trip and then again with their grandmother when
they returned to Brookings where Douglas has been on the faculty of the State
University for years. For Mrs. Chittick the plans and talks of advanced
degrees and professional work must have been very pleasing.

Shortly after her return to the Eastern Star Home in Redfield where she
lived she excused herself from an evening meal. She said she felt ill and must
return to her room. It was September of 1967 and she was eighty-two. For her
death came gently and spared her the sorrow of her youngest son's death later
that year.

                       JUMP OUT THE BACK

Our mother, a very quiet person, did not elaborate upon a subject. To
exaggerate was completely out of character for her. With a good sense of humor
if she described a laughable situation or repeated a funny story no revision
was made and she told it well. She did not nag. When we, as young children,
had been corrected and still chose to pursue offensive behavior, Mother could
be counted on to use a strap. There was no favoritism shown and I'm sure we
regarded her as very just.

Not given to careless talk she had no reason to regret flippant remarks
as we young people often did. What good qualities she had for avoiding
trouble! I can guess how her quietness developed: As a sharp little girl with
four older brothers and three older sisters, she had recognized the advantage
of just listening. This, long before the hearing specialists decided we get
eighty percent of our information through the ear. The system has drawbacks,
however, and in Mother's case one needed to be very close to her to know her.
We lucky ones who shared the home were really devoted!

On one occasion it would have been helpful to Mother if she had prepared
herself to talk to horses. The particular horses I have in mind were Puss and
Jack. They were handsome animals and one needed to observe their mischief to
believe it. Imagine my surprise to hear Mother tell a young brother to feed
and water this team because they'd be driving to the farm to take medicine and
groceries Ed needed. "Ed", our dad, had rented the Clark place and camping
there weekdays, he'd been doing spring field work. This farm was between eight
and nine miles south and east of Wetonka where we lived. There were no very
close neighbors but the mail route from Wetonka passed by the house on a
county road.

That Thursday the mailman, Mr. Chittick, had brought a note in from Dad
telling us he had a bad throat and would need the medicines he usually used.
That horrible quinsy! It's a severe form of tonsillitis which almost closes
the throat and threatens death. We had seen our dad battle this menace too
many times to accept the news lightly. And there was no chance to send the
drugs immediately--no neighbors from the area were in town that day and the
mail would not go out again on the south route until Saturday.

Mother was talking again to seven-year-old Ambrose, "I'll want you to go
with me because you have been out there and know the way."

"But You're not going to drive that outlaw team," I said, in shock.

"We'll have to drive what there is," Mother said practically.

True, Puss and Jack were the only horses we had left in town. They had
shown repeatedly they were non-conformists, so naturally they had not been
taken along to the farm with the other horses. Puss, a mature mare, should
have settled down as a productive farm animal but she remained unreliable and
a bad influence on Jack who was big, powerful and young.

My sister, Zola, and I were almost fifteen and almost thirteen, but knew
little of driving. Our experience amounted only to handling the reins when
dependable horses did what they were supposed to do. Mother could not have had
experience with unruly horses either. Although she had grown up on a farm,
there were older brothers to help and she had not done outdoor farm work. Now,
in this emergency, which seemed very great to all of us, she and Ambrose
harnessed the wild ones and as a precaution wired the neck-yoke to the tongue
of the spring wagon. Besides the medicine they loaded fresh and canned food
which would be required if Dad were too ill to return to town.

Mother was postmaster so she cautioned Zola and me, "It's hard to say
when we'll be back tomorrow. Be sure to have the mail sack for Leola ready by
train time. I've closed the books for today and changed the "stampers", so if
anyone comes in for a money order, date it for tomorrow."

Just like that! and Mother and Ambrose were off in the open wagon on the
chilly, rather dark, late afternoon. Home seemed very quiet. No battle between
the two young brothers with one of them absent. No quarrel between Zola and me
when we were on trial as adults. We usually made candy when on our own but
this evening Zola decided to make escalloped potatoes. Mrs. Backous had
brought some to a school picnic and we hadn't made any before. We were better
cooks than firemen and the old oven in the Home Comfort range did not get hot.
Later in the night when we tried some they were not really done.

Mother and Ambrose were back before noon and Mother definitely was travel
worn! At thirty-seven she sometimes had painful sciatica but she hadn't been
lame before she went to the farm! She ate some of our potatoes and made no
comment although they were dry and crispy after their overnight stay in the
oven. She must have been pleased to find something to eat before mail time
besides sugared fudge. Under our barrage of questions she said things had gone
well--briskly--but not disastrously, the first five miles. Puss and Jack were
naturally friskly after their period of no work but the drive proceeded
without incident as far as the old Lorber place where double wood gates could
be swung open and hooked with the opposite pair to close off the road when
moving the Lorber cattle from the pasture to the barnyard. These gates made no
problem since they were in their normal position and the road was clear of
them, but while passing this place a paper momentarily caught at one of the
gates blew into their path. The intrusion of the white, moving object was too
much for the excitable Puss. She leapt forward and Jack must lunge ahead, too.
The sudden, uncoordinated jump and speed broke the doubletree and made the
wired on neck-yoke a dangerous and unsuitable connection between horsepower
and vehicle! Mother had a firm grip on the reins because she had done as Dad
had often suggested -wrapped the lines around her hands to avoid dropping them
in an emergency. Now the taut reins dragged her out of the wagon, over the
right front wheel and along the road. As she fell she called back to our
brother, "Jump out the back, Ambrose." Dad had cautioned us at times, "In case
of a run-away, jump out the back." It was to avoid entanglement with a fast
moving wheel or give one a better chance in an upset.

Mother rid herself of the reins when Ambrose was safely on the ground. He
ran to her to see if she had been hurt. When she assured him she had no
serious injuries he ran to catch the team. They had stopped after a mile and
not really in remorse or to re-enter service. In Oscar Kienast's field a line
had caught, probably in a wheel, and thus checked on one side, circling would
be the only maneuver they could manage. Luckily, the rig had remained upright
on its wheels.

Ambrose had the horses unhooked from the light wagon by the time Mother
joined him. She and Ambrose picked out the most necessary items from the load
and carrying these things they and the horses walked the remaining
mile-and-a-half to the farm.

Our shocked father had mixed emotions: he was delighted to see them, he
felt Mother had taken too big a chance in driving Puss and Jack, and he was
grateful they had survived! He regretted he had alarmed us so by asking for
the medicine. In desperation, with none of his chlorate of potassium tablets on
hand, he had gargled with kerosene! He was much better!

In the morning Dad had been able to take a gentle team over to the
damaged spring wagon where he made a sturdy, rough repair. Later Mother and
Ambrose drove this quiet team to town. Puss and Jack had to stay and work.
They had been known to run away with a hayrack, but running away with a plow
or drag is something else. They may have had a more glorious or glamourous
life later. Sold to the army in World War I they could have been part of heroic
battle field action or part of the menu in a French cafe.

                            SKATING

It's doubtful if anyone considered skating as a possibility or as
important when the location of the town was decided! Cold weather could be
counted upon but ice for skating usually meant a stream or a lake. How did dry
Wetonka with no creek happen to have a place for skaters? That took
carelessness and maybe indifference after the artesian well provided a good
flow of water. Evidently it was thought to be inexhaustible once the layer of
shale deep below the land surface had been drilled through and water, under
pressure below, rushed forcefully from the pipe. How handy to have a stock
tank always full and running over after years of pumping water from surface
wells!

The overflow from the tank behind our barn went down the slope toward the
Hawkeye grain elevator and then northwest along a country road paralleling the
railroad track until a ground elevation turned it east for about a block. That
area between Moulton and Vivian Avenues became a pond. It expanded in wet
years and grew smaller in long, hot summers. It's possible the surface wells
the German people from Minnesota dug at their homes on Moulton Avenue received
their supply of water partly from this pond. The well at our Aunt Katie's in
this location needed to be only ten feet deep, and did seem to vary a lot in
its level of water.

The pond made the walk to our aunt's home a little longer for us but
children don't mind a few extra steps, and barefoot kids enjoy a pond to wade
through. Evidently Mr. Lynch and his brothers-in-law, the Rachels, disliked
the increase in distance between the elevator and their homes. They built a
foot bridge! Dry Wetonka now had a stream, a pond and a bridge! We loved
Lynch's bridge and often used it although going around "our end" of the puddle
was more direct.

A pond doesn't guarantee skating. One needs clear, still weather at the
time of a severe frost. If cold comes first with wind and wet, freezing snow,
there may not be any good ice that year. Nature must have cooperated to form
smooth ice early in 1908 or 1909. The older town people knew just what to do
with the frozen pond.

Our parents had enjoyed skating on the Elm River at Westport as
teen-agers so I can understand Mother ordering sled skates for Zola and me for
Christmas. Inez and Osie already knew about skating and Osie, at least, had
the conventional one-blade type. A catalog recommendation may have convinced
Mother of the value of such skates as learning devices for young children. As
soon as the skates came, Christmas or not, we got our double-edged gear
strapped on at home and walked to the near-by pond. The sled skate runners are
not too sensitive and not hurt by a little use on snow and dirt! I could be
wrong but I don't think they help in learning to skate. Ours weren't tested
much; there weren't many days for skating after we received them and they were
lost when our home burned in 1910.

The pond also failed as a skating area. The well water no longer flowed
freely behind our barn but discharged into a big round stock tank in a corner
location at Von Eschen's place where it furnished water for cattle in their
own feed lot and pasture, and also for any team or cow a neighbor or farmer
had in town. The overflow spread in the quite level pasture and after a season
with abundant rain and ideal conditions at freezing time, we had a much bigger
skating area.

I studied the catalog and ordered what sounded like a superior pair of
skates in my price range, $2.25. They were still "strap-one" but with toe
clamps which tightened with a skate key. On these I learned to skate using
what my pal, Lepha, and I called the "skating chair"--a strong dining room
chair which had lost its front legs. Pushed along in front of us learners, it
seemed to provide the balance and confidence we needed until we could manage
alone. We took turns using it and became fair skaters for our age.

At this time Mother got our young brother sled skates. Ambrose at six
found them completely inadequate in keeping up with ten and twelve year old
boys. Disregarding techniques and safety he ran at top speed on the ice trying
not to be left behind. In a few days he had soured on double blades and traded
his off for an old pair of "regulars" too big for him. He made time on these
but still not in the correct way--he was turning the skates inward and sliding
on the side of the blade and the edge of the skate platform. Would you believe
he became quite a good skater when he finally had traded upward to a proper
fitting pair.

When our group became teen-agers we had another good skating area with no
help but nature and a strong, ever-flowing well at Fyke's farm half-a-mile up
the railroad track toward Leola. There was a long, irregular stretch of rather
poor ice we sometimes explored in daylight, but the main part for skaters
covered about an acre of ground. We often walked out to the ice after school
or after supper on moonlight nights. More boys came at night and made games
and skating in pairs more enjoyable. They raced and showed off some doing
"figure eights" and skating backwards which we girls also tried. None of us
had ever seen good figure skating or ice shows. We had no idea there was a
beautiful world of skaters with unbelievable skills who practiced daily,
winter and summer, in ice arenas. For us, skating was a possible winter
activity. Something we did for fun.

                         CAMP FIRE THEN

The Camp Fire recreational facility for girls was started by Doctor and
Mrs. Luther Gulick of Washington, D. C. in 1912. Who would believe that our
faraway South Dakota town of Wetonka had a live Camp Fire group by 1915? Dora
Belseth, the 4th, 5th and 6th grade teacher, started it. She, no doubt, knew
of the organization through reading, through her recent teacher training, or
because the progressive town of Ipswich had such a girls' program. Ipswich is
the county seat of Edmonds County and a place where the Belseth family would
have had business contacts since their farm just south of McPherson County was
in Edmonds.

We were very enthusiastic Camp Fire Girls without much chance for wood
crafts or actual camping. A small neglected grove of trees outside of town did
serve as an outdoor meeting place, but I remember more distinctly a candle lit
ceremony which found us in a circle on Dora's schoolroom floor. We sang the
songs, repeated the ritual, and demonstrated our crafts. In our Indian
dresses, beaded headbands, and "jewelry" we were forest maidens despite the
schoolroom walls.

The dresses and headbands were our own work and not all of a quality a
real squaw would have approved. The jewelry we earned the hard way, bead by
bead; a homemaking bead for several weeks care of the family lamps--cleaning,
trimming wicks, and filling with kerosene; scrupulous tooth care for a like
period for a health bead; patriotic efforts for an attractive red, white, and
blue enameled one. A glance at our ceremonial gowns showed that we worked.
Beads! We earned them and on meeting nights we wore them!

We paid for our own materials but Dora must have contributed the
literature we had to begin with. Also the loom for making the headbands
belonged to her. We earned a good sum the second year when we produced a play,
"Deacon Dubbs." I was a Swedish hired girl. With just a little revision the
play exactly fitted our band: a part for one young girl had to become a part
for twins, and men and boy parts were taken by girls. Since the play dominated
the school interest at the end of the year the principal, who loved plays,
helped us stage it and let it take the place of a final school entertainment.
It was well attended so after that the Camp Fire Girls had a bank account.

Our big interest during the summer of 1917 was not truly a Camp Fire
activity although food always made the meetings more enjoyable. This one was
strictly social--a lawn party at Alldritt's home. Each member could invite a
boy and it would be no evening for brown denim Indian dresses!

Alldritt's had raised nine children and now Lepha, the youngest, was
fourteen. She and I were good friends and classmates. Her mother said during
seventeen years when the older chiIdren were young she had never spent a night
away from home. With some of the older boys absent from home and "the baby" in
high school she had time for hobbies. There were lovely Sunday dinners and the
girls often brought guests. As Lepha's pal I'm sure I showed up too often as a
Sunday extra. The big yard became one of Mrs. Alldritt's projects. In the
stark little town the well tended grass, the vine covered fence and the
healthy bushes and flowers were delightful. Several of the shrubs and the start
in wild cucumber vines which I contributed to Dad's little "park" on Main
Street were gifts of Mrs. Alldritt. And my training in tatting and crocheting
was also due to the oldest girl's, Ethel's, and the mother's patience.

At last the week of the party arrived. Already some of the Camp Fire
members had been helping to make the yard perfect. The committees had met and
contributions of food had been planned. The talk centered on boys and dress.
But my cousin Adeline and I would be "on location" with Dad, or "Uncle Ed" as
Adeline called him. Being "on location" with Dad meant being ten miles south of
town cooking for him and a helper, Chuck Goodrich.

At this place our combination quarters and kitchen had the minimum--one
window, one door, a table, some stools, a kerosene cook stove, a water can,
shelves for groceries, and a floor bed for us. The men slept in a tent. The
horses were in the bigger part of the building at night and they had no floor
in their area. They were more important to the project than Adeline and I. The
men could have cooked for themselves as Dad had in the spring when we were
still in school and he had put in the crop alone. Now at harvest time, the
horses pulled the header and the header-box, and the men worked long hours.

On the way to the farm camp Monday morning both of us girls thought about
the party. Surely Adeline would hate to miss it. Just my age she had voted boy
friend who would be disappointed. been too bashful to ask anyone, but I wasn't
convinced we'd have to stay away. Friday seemed off then.

One thing we were learning: don't bring up the subject to Dad requiring
permission. He could be moody and, lost in thought on an unknown matter, a
request might bring a fast answer and it would be, "No!". We didn't mention
the party to him and it's possible he didn't know about it. It's to our credit
we didn't pray for rain. A storm would have stopped the grain cutting and let
us all go back to town. We'd have felt guilty and responsible for the crop
loss.

Thursday came and no word that arrangements had been made to get us home
for the party. No mail again until Saturday! Now Friday was slipping away! The
men had gone back to the field for the afternoon cutting. We sat in the hired
man's Model T and studied the driving manual, but even Adeline, who was brave
around horses, didn't have nerve enough to start Chuck's car.

I said, "If we leave now we could walk to town in time for the "doings",
and it will be getting cooler as we go. Even if Dad sees us he wouldn't Stop
the field work to find out what we had in mind."

Adeline remembered we were low on bread and some other items. She thought the
trip would be helpful!

I wrote a note, "We have gone in to the Camp Fire party. We'll bring the
groceries we need and come back on the mail in the morning."

It turned out we did better than we promised. At home everyone seemed
glad we'd made it. We were so thrilled as we dressed for the evening we felt
no fatigue at all. And the party was really worth it! The night increased in
beauty as the sun faded and a glorious full moon took over. When worries might
have crept in about Dad's reaction, Adeline's understanding boyfriend, Billie
Holmes, who knew Dad well, came up with the suggestion that he and Dora's
brother take us back to camp. I didn't expect permission but mother allowed
it. She must have considered the needed groceries, our dad's displeasure at
our walk-out, and the good characters of the boys. So we not only had the
excitement of the party but the pleasant ride back in Belseth's double buggy
behind the lively sorrel team.

We were no noisy revelers as we thanked the boys and quietly took
ourselves and our groceries into the kitchen "apartment". And Dad had no cross
words for us at breakfast. He may have been secretly pleased that we asked no
favors and solved the problem, complete with fringe benefits like bread and
meat.

During the following school year Wetonka's Camp Fire group became a Red
Cross knitting club, The Daughters of Diligence or D. D.'s! My sister, Zola,
should be credited or blamed for the name, but I'm sure we voted on it. Dora
had married Harold AlIdritt and gone to Watauga to live. Etta Ferguson, who
had been my fifth grade teacher, was the club's sponsor and her mother taught
us to knit. Mrs. Lindley, who handled the materials for the Red Cross
organization, may have had to rip out some of our early work, but we were
always encouraged and finally American soldiers in France must surely have
appreciated our "knit two, purl two" products.

                 MY BEST LEFT-HANDED COMPLIMENT

The banker's wife called me one morning when I was seventeen. "I don't
understand it," I told my mother as I began looking over the music. "She says
Mr. Harms from the piano company in Aberdeen is there and she wants me to play
her piano. I'm terrified. He may be a professional musician and I'll be
paralyzed."

Mother would have felt this an exaggeration. She knew I could play
several acceptable solos and she was such a calm person herself she could have
played any composition she had learned even for before the composer.

With hands already trembling I left. No explanation occurred to me. Why
was Mr. Harms at Heil's? They wouldn't be buying a piano. They already had an
impressive instrument with five pedals, one of which caused a mandolin effect.
I had wondered how it sounded. Their piano seemed to be heard only when some
young person, fooling around, played "Peter, Peter. Pumpkin Eater" with two
fingers on the black keys.

Heil's had come to our very young town with money! They could have been
twenty-five and thirty years of age, maybe younger. Their dear baby, Arthur,
died before the age of two. Later they had two other boys. Both of the parents
had German accents which encouraged mimics to repeat their remarks. Mr. Heil,
a nice looking man, was thought to be sharp in business, but he often came up
with a short comment which did not endear him to the ones addressed. When he
found a bunch of us loafing late in the day at the post office his greeting,
"Everybody vurkin," was not a question, but it still seemed to indicate we
could be better employed.

However, Louie Ludwig was articulately harmless compared to Pauline, his
wife. Nicely built and wearing good quality clothes, she could not hide a skin
problem which had given her a strawberry-like nose. If she had been teased
about this feature as a child one can understand her tendency to note
shortcomings in others. Whatever the reason, Pauline often offended
acquaintances with her high-pitched, accented words, intended to be witty, but
yet a personal reminder. We called them "little digs" then.

She loved small dogs and always had one she had trained to do tricks. A
vivid recollection of her remains: Ready to take the train to Aberdeen in her
pretty clothes, she had a little white poodle on a leash of blue ribbon. This
was pretty stylish for Wetonka and we wondered what Aberdeen people would
think.

Heil's had built The Wetonka State Bank, a cement block structure on Main
Street with basement space below for a barber shop and pool hall, a large
general store directly across the street from the one Stone's and Lindley
already had in operation, and a home. The store they named, "The Wetonka
Merchantile Company", and it had style. An elevated, partly enclosed area
housed the bookkeeper-cashier, and change boxes sped on their little tracks up
to her. Two young Irish girls, Katie and Rose Kaney, came from where Heil's
had previously lived to work in the store, but Heil's didn't keep this
business long. It was sold to others who could not make it a profitable
venture either.

The Wetonka Opera House above the store had style too. Although no opera
company ever came to try it out, it provided a good place for the lyceum
course the school once sponsored and any other community affair where a stage
was needed. That stage! with its interior and exterior scenery, two roll-up
background curtains, and a front curtain which advertised all the town
merchants, had brought a good commercial artist, Fred Kindschi, to town for a
time.

Before the brick school was built the school programs were held in the
hall and the first Catholic Mass I remember was said in "the opera house",
too. It became a dining hall with long tables for the big dinners the churches
put on in the fall to raise money for church expense. It could be full of card
tables for community whist parties. As the old dance hall it undoubtedly holds
the most fond memories for the most people. Now a visiting old timer may feel
relief that the entrances are barred. Even in the 1920's the rhythm of the big
crowds had the floor springing noticeably and the thought would occur, "What
if it suddenly gives away?"

The two-story frame home Heil's built had a continuous porch around two
sides which made it look larger and gave it a graceful appearance. They had
fenced the large yard and tried to maintain a lawn. There was also a neat barn
for the horse, and later, the red Rambler, the first automobile in town.

At the door I was greeted warmly, "Silvy, I vent you to play my new piano
so I see how it sounds vile Mr. Harms iss here." The old instrument was
actually gone from the front room and a lovely lower type upright occupied its
place. Certain that Mr. Harms knew about music as well as piano sales, I
picked Grieg's "Erotik" in one flat and hope my nervous fingers would find most
of the right keys. I followed it with Torjusson's "Valse Impromptu" and then
Mr. Harms had to be on his way. Mrs. Heil was rather quiet, so relieved to
have only a "Peter, Peter, Pumpkin Eater" audience, I banged out popular
numbers like "Floreine," "Carbolic Acid Rag," and semi classic, "Robin's
Return." Pauline loved the fast beat, volume, chords, and runs. She was
delighted and disappointed, too, "Vy didn't you play like det for Mr. Harms?
For him you played so slow and sad vit one finger like the symphony. You know,
Louie and I vent to the Minneapolis Symphony in Aberdeen vit Mr. and Mrs.
Vord."

Now I'm really listening! Heil's at the symphony with the Ward's who own
the big hotel in Aberdeen, whose son-in-law became the governor! How did that
come about? Would they have had some business deal brewing? Or might they just
have had adjoining seats?

Mrs. Heil went on, "I stood it for a vile -that sad music, so loud, then
so soft vit one man playing like vit one finger--then I say, 'Let's go, I
can't stand it,' and Mrs. Vord say, "Vy, I don't see vat can be de matter, I
tink it's joost svell!

Then Louie nudged me I should keep still."

Smart Louie Ludwig, that time he knew when to keep still even if he did
irk us with his "Everybody vurkin." I could sympathize with them, too, for I
could get very worn out with a long proper concert.

My young brother rode to ball games and other events many times with
Heil's and their- son who was a chum. On one occasion Pauline said to him,
"den are ve going to ride in your car, Monnie?" Though quite young he said he
realized it to be a
reminder that we had no car.
 
When I rode home from Aberdeen one Christmas vacation with Henry and
Bertha Kruger, Mrs. Heil was also a passenger. I had been back to school for a
better certificate and although teaching again, I was not really solvent.
quite a bit but that was

My winter coat had cost the fourth winter for it. Pauline noticed, "You got no
new coat dis year, Silvy?" Changeless Pauline! I hope St. Peter had a Rambler
and little dog ready for her, and assured her everyone else up there had what
they really wanted.

                      A BOY AND HIS TOWER

"He's the first white child born in Wetonka," our dad said as he passed
out cigars. Our December 1907 brother, the first baby I had ever seen was very
red. Dad evidently mentioned "white" because he felt he must limit his
statement when there could have been an Indian baby born at this same South
Dakota site while a tribe occupied the area.

The boy was named for Ambrose Kimmet who, with a Mr. Chase, drilled the
new town's artesian well. No one dreamed of the child's later fascination with
the well and the high tower added for the water tank in 1918. By then his love
of the tower surpassed his regard for the train!

He became a very direct little guy much given to short cuts like deciding
to skip creeping and walk at nine months. We older children had reason to
regret this ambulatory prowess because a fast hiker less than a year old can
be difficult to watch. A telephone call from Mrs. Stone on the town's new
communication system brought the message, "Your baby is running away," and
while I think this an overstatement, he was actually on his way to the street
with several business places which eventually became Main Street. That little
trip won him the nickname, "Sport", still used by some of his old friends.
There was another indication of character in this--his liking for being where
things were happening.

Our older sister, Inez, brought him down from the landing near the top of
the grain elevator where Dad was the buyer. I can still see the little,
sleeping figure, in the white diaper, close to the edge of the small platform.
The outside stairway, a unique feature of this elevator, had been understood
as no convenience intended for children ! At five I had never attempted the
climb and our seven-year-old sister went up only with our father. It seemed
the baby liked high places, too,

Somehow he survived several near misses with the switching train
including an unscheduled trip to the next town when a neighbor snatched him
from the entrance of a moving coach. There were several close calls with
run-away horses, too, but he lasted and got to be ten years old!

At that Lime the town, enjoying a boom in World War I, invested in new
water mains and the elevated tank with its shiny ball at the top--one hundred
twenty-two feet above the ground. The boy spent time on the job throughout the
construction work whether the contractors knew it or not. There were the
wonderful new deep ditches which the big machine dug, to check, and finally
the steel framework which would support the tank. What if one were threatened
and chased off the area during working hours, there were evenings and Sundays
for kids. Every steel leg and horizontal or diagonal brace offered a challenge
for stunt climbing. Then it was done! Ambrose was the first to the top, and I
don't mean just to the roof; I mean over the overhang and up to the gold ball!
The town was bonded for $40,000 to pay for it, and other kids, including our
younger brother, Marshall, climbed it, but it was Ambrose's tower!

What a rich, big plaything--and outside of Texas, too! But then its
possession began to include responsibilities and make demands. An almost fatal
command performance occurred in 1923 when the stand pipe needed painting. The
invitation came through the town board and provided brush, paint and payment!
With "do-it-yourself" enthusiasm Ambrose got his equipment together--a
hay-stacker rope borrowed from John Kirnan, and a swing seat from Art Quick.
With the rope tied to the tank above, the seat seemed about the right height
for beginning the job.

"I was set," he said, "with the gallon of black paint fastened to the
right side of the swing and the big brush in my right hand, I slid down the
rope to the seat. I felt great, the bo's'n's chair deal really gave me a
lift--I was soon to find out it could also furnish a fancy spin. First I found
out I could not reach the top of the stand pipe sitting down, but I could by
standing. So I'm off to a standing start. Painting with my right hand, mistake
No. I--I braced my free hand against the pipe surface, a simple scaffold
habit. But a swing is not a scaffold! With both hands pushing forward, the
seat moved out. What's happening to my center of gravity! An instinctive big
push, which would have righted me on a rigid platform, served only to put me
in a flying position with nowhere to go but down. A lucky grab with my free
left hand caught the rope at the right side of the seat, and the swing, the
paint, the paint brush, and I did a triple spin over ninety feet in the air. I
was then hanging below my trapeze by the one hand, still carefully holding the
paint brush in my stronger right! It's hard to explain what gets top priority
in a crisis.

"I did have to drop the brush to get back into the seat, but I couldn't
have gone on working immediately anyway. Most of the paint had settled on
Logan Jones's nearby garage. Not a smooth job, but durable--if the otherwise
unpainted garage is still there, the spots are!

"After the poor start, I worked fast and finished the stand pipe before
dark. The pay was twenty-five dollars. I still felt great--twenty five dollars
for a day's work and what a day!

"One hot day when I helped Elmer Hamilton paint the inside of the tank I
didn't feel that good. We thinned the paint with gasoline to speed up the
drying and get the tank back into service promptly. The combination of fast
drying paint on the hot metal made for lots of fumes in the closed up space.
Elmer painted what he could reach standing. I painted the upper part from a
ladder and it could be I was getting more fresh air circulating through the
small opening on the south side. I didn't understand Elmer's conversation as
he left. In that echo filled interior I caught only his last two words, 'going
down'. Suddenly as I painted in a new location I was dizzy, groggy, and the
tank was whirling. With great effort I worked my way down from the ladder and
toward the entrance--I crawled, I hung on. At last big gulps of outside air
were helping. I waited, and then with death grips on each rung of the outside
ladder, I was down."

"Sport, you look ghastly," Elmer said resting place in the shade. "I told
you it getting dangerous up there. You should have when I did."

"I still felt OK and I didn't get what you said. Then when it hit, it
really hit, and I wasn't sure I'd make it down."

We'll have to quit working while it's so hot," Elmer planned, "or 'spell
each other off' painting. It will take longer and there will be complaints,
but I don't think they want us to kill ourselves. Hope no fire breaks out
while that tank is empty."

So much for fumes and heat. An earlier experience had not come on a hot
day. In December of 1922 when Ambrose was nearly fifteen, word came to him at
school: "Can you come to the water pumping station? The tank is frozen over
and Bob Ricker has gone up to see what needs to be done. He needs help."

It was no news that the tank water had frozen. For ten days the
temperature had been thirty degrees below zero, and with the water system out,
everyone had been carrying what water they needed from the well in pails. That
afternoon it was twenty degrees below with a gale blowing up a blizzard. The
wind had made trouble for Bob. He was home temporarily from work as a seaman
on the Great Lakes. He had climbed the tower before, but this time in his
chilled condition he feared the descent. Ambrose found him in the tank on the
ice. "He was scared and shaking--too shaky to roll a cigarette. I thought he
must need that cigarette and I needed to talk him into coming down, so I
rolled the tobacco. It was my first attempt and a rough job, but Bob smoked
and I talked. 'Bob, you'll have to come down with me.' "

"No, I'll just stick it out till the wind lets up."

"Listen, Bob, no one knows about the wind, but you'll freeze up here on
the ice in no time."

"No, no, not with the lantern," he argued and would not make the attempt.

Ambrose went down the steel ladder to the older men waiting below and
among them they decided Bob might feel safe with a rope for security. A
volunteer brought most of a coil of half-inch rope from the nearby hardware
store and the boy carried one end up to the tank.

"Now, Bob, it's all fixed. We'll pass the rope over the top rung of the
ladder and then you tie it around your waist. The men will let out the rope as
you come down and you'll always be safe."

The young man again refused, explaining that because of his larger size
the wind would blow him right off the supporting rungs.

"Try a short distance--I'll go first and I know we'll make it. Those
fellows below are well able to lower you if you miss your footing."

By this time something got to Bob--companionship or the concern the men
below were showing. He tried the ladder and regained his nerve as he descended.

With Bob grounded Ambrose's work really began because no progress had
been made on solving the ice problem. Up he went again taking a better light,
a gasoline lantern. He then drew up an axe and a long crowbar with the rope.
There was ice in the tank to within six feet of the top and be began chopping
in the middle in order to be lined up with the ten-inch stand pipe which

                            FRIENDS

"Well, they're both German," I thought as I wondered at their friendship.
Later I remembered their wives were cousins, but that would not be enough to
bring them together in the dead of a South Dakota winter. I'm sure neither
would have made a five mile trip over snow buried country roads to please his
wife. In fact I doubt if Joe ever took Lucy anywhere. I had never seen her in
town since her marriage. The Schultz's, John and Mary, seemed to have a
division of labor established. One or the other, not both, did the errands in
town. At last I thought of the bond: liquor, in this case moonshine. John made
it, both drank it, but Joe was addicted to it.

He was neither young nor attractive as I first remember him and he didn't
improve with added years. I'd guess his liking for strong drink had begun
early and been hard on him. With an awkward, blocky figure he had a face one
must see to believe. It was deeply wrinkled and it sagged, especially one eye.
The other eye, probably his seeing eye, was quite large. Brawls might have
been common in his youth and the cause of his facial disaster, but he was
beyond his prime when we knew him and not known to be quarrelsome.

John, a younger man and more erect, would, no doubt, have made a neat
appearance if ever dressed in any but well worn farm clothes. His
distinguishing characteristic was a high "squeally" voice. In contrast, Joe's
booming, rumbling thunder with thick German accent may have been a
questionable asset but one not easily ignored or forgotten.

Our dad did not fail to notice special voices and accents. Although he
showed his own Irish ancestry in the pronunciation of some words and in the
use of typical expressions, it did not hinder his imitation of the sounds and
dialects of his friends. I'm using the word "friends" correctly

                         TOM AND OLIVE

I think they came because Tom's uncle, L. B. J. Lohner, owned a Wetonka
farm. Such an investment would be thought prudent during World War I when
grain prices were high and there had been several years with sufficient rain
for good crops. L. B. his nephew, Tom Lohner, and a friend, Leon Porter, came
to Wetonka in 1919. Tom didn't come directly from Exira, Iowa, which was his
uncle's home. He and his parents had spent nine years at a Carthage South
Dakota, farm, and Tom had worked in the Carthage store two winters.

Both Tom and Leon eventually became Wetonka business men. Leon first ran
the pool hall, and then after his marriage to Pearlie Krueger, and more
farming, he and Pearlie made a success of a cafe and recreation spot, Porter's
Place, in the former First State Bank building. Leon also bought grain for the
Farmer's Equity Exchange before moving to Aberdeen to become a motel owner.

Tom's parents moved to Aberdeen first before joining Tom in the store he
purchased from George Zerr--the old hardware store. It had been J. B.
Hamilton's contribution to the new town in 1908 or 1909. Lohner's lived in the
apartment above the store. The younger son, Andy, back from the Army, also
came. This store, well stocked and more attractive than Lindley's was just as
efficiently managed. A lot of the quality of the business originated with the
girl who came to clerk and remained as Tom's wife--Olive Broadbent of Leola,
Mrs. Lindley's niece. She had gained experience clerking in Leola's drug
store, but a witty, friendly personality with fairness and good judgment were
her great assets. Because of Lohner's popular store I have thought Wetonka
survived at least ten years after good transportation could have taken its
customers to larger markets. Our mother's "open day and night post office"
helped, too. Farmers could work until dark in a busy season and still get into
Lohner's or pick up their mail and buy a money order in Wetonka.

Concern for others was a dominant family trait of the Lohner family. They
truly cared about people and this became more apparent with time. One can
understand young people enjoying dances and hall games which were the chief
entertainments of the small plains towns. It's another thing to drive miles to
a neighboring town after a busy day, and to invite Someone else to ride along.
This was good for the town, too. If Tom and Olive were going several other
couples would go and the next local dance would be better attended as friends
from the other towns reciprocated, increasing the ticket sales and the number
of midnight suppers sold.

When the store and two other buildings burned in 1931, it was recognized
as a permanent loss to the town. In such bad times who would consider
investing in a building or stock of goods with only dim prospects of success?
Olive did open a smaller store in the vacant building across the street which
a Mr. Huff had built as a grocery and in which John Swihart had run a store.
It had also been Bill Alexander's last barber shop. Tom and Olive lived in
Mrs. Rachel's house which they had enlarged and modernized. After the fire
Tom's parents shared this home. Tom Sr. was very ill at this time.

Tom's venture into direct candy and tobacco service to stores prospered.
He had started it on a shoestring with an old limousine which had lots of room
and a low price. Business grew to truck size and then to two trucks with Earl
Kruse, future post master, and Lawrence Miller as drivers and more towns and
business places on the routes. Olive's little store, not needed for family
income, was given up. Sometimes she went along with a driver on a truck
because she knew the stops and customers. A new driver could be trained this
way. She served the business in other ways--state tax stamps must be affixed
to individual packages of cigarettes. This burden became so time consuming
several of the town women were employed to help.

During this period when Lohner Candy Service grew along with the number
of friends in the many communities, Tom's father died and his mother lost her
life in a fall on stair-e at their home. Tom and Olive's understanding of
elderly people and their concern for bereaved families developed as they
suffered personal losses themselves. In 1944 when our dad had a stroke and was
gravely ill before his death, Olive brought a complete meal to our home. We
had explained we could not come to their place since the family members not at
the hospital might be called to go there at any time.

Before Tom Senior's death, he was sometimes brought to Porter's Place. On
days when he had less pain he enjoyed a game of whist. One day looking over
his good friends at the card table, and thinking how little time he had left,
he said, "Val, Ai know Ai've got to die." The unpleasant likelihood stunned and
brought silence until a younger player, Pete Geiberger, broke the gloom with
his rough, but situation saving, "Hell, we've all got to die! Come on, play
cards!" Strangely, a heart attack ended Pete's life about the time Mr.
Lohner's terminal illness caused his death.

Tom and Olive, loyal to Wetonka, continued to make it their home long
after Lohner Candy Service became a Eureka, South Dakota, establishment which
ordered and received supplies directly from factories. When they did move to
Aberdeen in 1951 they also moved their house there. This followed the pattern
for the decline of the town--as a family left and a home became vacant it
would be purchased add moved to a farm or to another community. Lohner's kept
their farm land, including the old Brearton place just south of town. They
said it would furnish the excuse to come back often. Actually, they needed no
excuse; their friendly concern always takes them wherever old acquaintances
are. in Aberdeen a number of elderly persons from Wetonka lived in apartments
or nursing homes. These never lacked callers, treats, or news from "home" if
Tom and Olive were in town.

In Spearfish where our mother lived in her later years, Tom's Christmas
letter became an important part of the season. He seemed to type with the
picture of the old town in mind and then record the present situation and
condition of every structure and person connected with it. Since Mother's
death the newsy report comes to one of the members of her family and is sent
around to the others. Tom doesn't make use of duplicates and sending out
originals when their card list is endless, is a tremendous undertaking. Of
course Olive writes messages, too.

The year before our mother's death, Tom and Olive invited us to bring her
to their home for a visit. There were four of us and they had asked all our
old Wetonka friends and especially the group of widows of Mother's age The
evening of the Saturday when we arrived a large group was entertained at "The
Ranch", a popular restaurant operated by a former Wetonkan, Adeline Erdmann.
There were even more at the open house on Sunday. It was a beautiful
experience and one which could not be duplicated since so many of the ones
renewing friendships that day died soon afterwards.

In 1967 when Mother died and Lohner's learned of the burial at Sacred
Heart Cemetery in Aberdeen they notified everyone they could contact on a trip
to Wetonka and others by phone. There were more old friends at the graveyard
than there had been at the funeral in Spearfish. All were invited to Lohner's
for a brunch which turned out to be a complete meal.

When other old friends have been buried at Aberdeen, Tom and Olive have
offered the same comfort and hospitality. They are retired and the dedication
to relatives and friends continues as the house overflows with welcome
visitors when they are at home. They drive miles to greet or visit dear ones
who do not travel as much as they. There have been many trips to Washington,
D. C. to visit Olive's niece, Annie Sherman, who made her home with them after
the death of her own parents; and many Iowa trips to Lohner's old home and to
their daughter's. They are blessed with grandchildren who rate, above all
else, a vacation at Grandpa Lohner's and the trips to the Wetonka farm.

There have been two European trips and Tom's pictures and accounts of the
countries visited indicate the European contacts enjoyed the Lohner's as much
as Tom and Olive enjoyed them. Traveling companions become friends and Norway
cousins join them in Aberdeen and travel with them to discover other long lost
cousins. Maybe the "one world" concept could be accomplished with more such
active friendships.

"Gude Maawning" still rings clearly in my memory. "It's in my mind," I
said aloud, "but I can never tell anyone how it was."

"She must have been very happy herself to put so much joy into a simple

"It's possible Mrs. Lundquist was a little hard on Charlie. He loved to
visit and could be witty, too. He would say of an acquaintance who had gained
weight, 'She look gude, she hav been vel vintered.' He had become somewhat
heavy himself and that probably wasn't good. I wondered if he hoed in the
garden on the hot day because his wife insisted the work needed to be done. I
hoped he had volunteered, because it was there he had the stroke which ended
his life.

"Not too long after Charlie's death most of our family were home for
Christmas. We attended a dance at the old Lundquist farm given by the tenant.
I was pleased to see Mrs. Lundquist there. As we visited she complained about
the many trips she'd had to make to the abstract office at the county seat
trying to settle up the estate after her husband's death. She never called him
"Charlie" nor was she formal enough to say 'Mr. Lundquist'. As she summed up
her troubles and annoyances she concluded, 'Ef Lundquist var only her et be no
treck et all.' That was very true and also humorous. I understood her
exasperation much better when we had to bring an abstract up to date to
complete a sale and the purchaser's lawyer wanted to know if the 1876 owner
had ever occupied the premises with a wife."

                             BERTHA

Bertha Rappe came from Leola to help Mrs. Heil care for her large house
and new baby. She was a stocky German girl from a farm home with a practical,
no-nonsense approach to life and a heavy-voice German American speech. She
might have fallen in love with Mrs. Heil's dapper brother, Jake, who worked in
their bank, but she chose to marry Henry--Henry Kruger whose parents were
early settlers in McPherson County long before Wetonka was thought of. The
only trees near the little new town were known as Kruger's Grove and were on
land formerly owned by them--probably their "tree claim".

Always as Kruger's daughter-in-law she must have done her part and had
her say for Bertha had her own ideas about family life and community affairs.
Surely the elder Kruger's were pleased Henry had made such a suitable
marriage--and there was a capable German wife at the farm.

When Bertha had her say the comment would be strictly her own after a
quick appraisal of the other speaker's intent and salty with dead-pan humor.

Our dad enjoyed Bertha's surprising remarks and type of delivery.
Thinking to tease her when she complained about some short-coming of the
reliable, ever-patient Henry, he said, "Bertha, you're too good a woman to put
up with it, why don't you poison him?" Bertha's short reply, "Vy, vot vould
people zhay?" gave him a good laugh then and many times afterward as he
recalled it.

In the motorized, prosperous twenties, Kruger's like other
well-established farm families, were depending less on the catalog houses and
local stores. They made the twenty-mile trip to Aberdeen and bought in the
larger markets. We were delighted with Bertha's account of looking for a suit
for Henry in a bargain store. She said the determined clerk urged her to go out
of the store so he could show her the superior material in the sunlight.

"Such vonderful clots, I should look tru it. Sotch tin stoff--like a
telescope et vas--I could see Vetonka."

In the drought stricken thirties even the long prosperous farmers were in
distress. Gradually, relief became available, but the proud, independent
pioneers who had found a way through hard times before meant to come through
again on their own. The thin unmarketable cattle were butchered for food and
the only surviving plant, the Russian thistle, was canned for greens. Along
with those who accepted the gifts of food and grants to save their families, a
number of expert chillers misrepresented their needs and received jobs on
P.W.A. and more than their share of commodities being distributed. When these
lived better than they had in normal times the struggling old-time independents
were naturally disgusted. Among those prospering on relief was a young farmer
popularly known as Buster who had managed to buy a new car.

Knowing how the hard working Bertha would feel about commodities and
grasping former ne'er-do-wells of the time, Dad sought her reaction by saying,
"Bertha, have you heard? The government has been testing the food value of
grasshoppers and they mean to can them to distribute with the commodities."

"Ef dey do zat, I'll get von quardt, and zhat Buster vill have his cellar
full."

                            SURVIVOR

Pretty, twenty-one-year-old Jessie had her own way of speaking. It was
recognizable as rural Iowa talk--distinct, happy, practical, always natural.
This was 1906 when even the newcomers from a neighboring state had
distinguishing characteristics. Probably our family speech came through as that
of Illinois Irish.

That spring four young people were on their barren, little, square farms
about a mile apart These farms were some of those bought from, or rented from
pioneer settlers who had got the land from the government and "proved up" on
it. The two young couples hoped to make the farms profitable by industry and
proper farm methods. They were Art and Jessie Quick and Bill and Mae
Alexander. Art and Jessie were our close neighbors the year we lived at Bern
Farm. There was no rural delivery of mail at the time so we learned to know
all those who came to "our" Bern Post Office for their mail.

Neither family had children but the Quick's had lost a first baby. Jessie
loved youngsters and little ones sensed this. At four, my first memory of her
included a place on her lap and lots of attention.

Another memory was that of beautiful butter she brought into Wetonka to
trade at our small store for groceries. The three pound butter jar had to be
replaced with another from our supply but the customer might get her own jar
back on the next week's exchange. The neat circle of very clean white cloth
which covered the otherwise exposed part of the butter had no value but I
treasured the piece of embroidered dimity Jessie had on hers one day. When the
jar's contents had been used or sold I tried to wash out the cloth to use in
doll sewing I made poor progress until Mother explained that butter fat would
not wash out in cold water.

When Wetonka was still a very new town Alexander's gave up farming and
moved into a house just east of Lynch's. Bill became the barber in the shop
below Heil's bank. Quick's continued to farm but at a place a half mile
southeast of Wetonka, and the friendship of the four grew--always Quick's and
Alexander's together, at first in Quick's double buggy and later in their
Model T at all the picnics, celebrations, and ball games. Both women were hard
workers, excellent cooks, and immaculate housekeepers. They had top billing in
the scale of reference for cleanliness. The highest compliment a local
housewife could receive might be, "She keeps house like Mrs. Quick," or "She's
as clean as Mrs. Alexander."

Jessie Quick might have become an invalid or lost her life when still a
young person if she had not had the spirit to live and the intelligence to
follow the advice given for her survival. She had developed tuberculosis. To
outsiders there was no change of pace, no panic, but a white canvas enclosure
appeared in the yard and Jessie took to her tent for rest and sleeping.

Jessie recovered and she and Art adopted a baby girl. They complimented
their good friends, Will and Mae, by naming the infant, "Wilma." A much loved
little person this child delighted acquaintances as she acquired her mother's
characteristics and Iowa speech. Several years later Alexander's adopted girl
received Jessie's name.

Mrs. Quick became a driver when they bought the Model T, but this was no
overnight accomplishment. No one ever drove more slowly than Jessie. She had a
way of honoring Art with an extra pronoun as when she told of the choice of
the baby, "I thought we should ask for a boy, but Art, he, wanted a little
girl." Now I suppose, "Art, he, said to drive carefully." She did and she
practiced. An approach to a closed elevator appealed to her as a challenge.
She drove up the ramp used by the grain wagons, stopped, backed down the
curving approach, and turned in the street to repeat the maneuver. Her
practice made her a good driver who managed a number of big cars through the
years with no mishaps.

The good automobiles and the stylish bungalow in town were a part of the
prosperity Quick's enjoyed as they farmed and managed well.

Art did not sell his land when the new home with its combination barn and
garage was built. Their several farms were occupied by tenants and Art busied
himself as a stock buyer with a feeding lot across town beyond the last houses
on the northwest. Quicks did not lose out financially in the depression as the
town failed.

Art and Jessie served faithfully as mature citizens in civic and social
events. Jessie had a leading part in "Back to the Farm" when the community
club put on this play in Wetonka and in Leola. Among other things Art was a
school board member for several years. The new member of the family at this
time was Spider, a tiny Chihuahua. He became a town character who sometimes
received a holiday gift in the mail in his own name, Spider Quick.

When older and no longer well, Art owned and kept open the old Wetonka
Merchantile--Heil's old store which had become a hardware store. Without doubt
it was not making money, but it gave Art a place to go every day, a place
where friends dropped in to visit.

Without Wilma, a nurse married to a Mitchell farmer and living a hundred
miles away, Jessie's life after Art's death could have been lonely, but she
made it pleasant. A regrettable fact at this time was the loss of closeness in
the long friendship with Mae, also a widow. It could have been due to Mae's
pride. With limited funds she would not want to be the non-contributing guest
on the many trips to other towns and to Wilma's pleasant home for special
days. Another town widow, Virginia Swihart, became Jessie's companion on the
happy outings in the comfortable car.

By 1970 both Mae and Virginia had died and Jessie at eighty-five drove
carefully, as Art said, and the car, a recent one, was dependable. Her
Christmas card that year said, "I'm just fine, still driving my car. Will go
to Wilma's for the holidays. She is the last of the seventeen or more women
who survived their husbands and lived in the town until death or advanced age
made residence near relatives or in apartments or nursing homes in Aberdeen
necessary. Her picture at eighty-seven showed a comely healthy woman--still
pretty.

The 1973 card came from the senior apartments near the Mitchell hospital
where Wilma is employed. Jessie said she had been keeping up her apartment and
doing some baking. By 1975 at ninety-one a broken hip brought her to
Mitchell's Firesteel Heights nursing home. Her plainly written full page note
said she had progressed to the point of walking with the aid of a walker when
another fall resulted in the fracture of the other hip. Jessie's indomitable
spirit had seen her through again. She reported, "I am just fine. I can dress
and undress, and go to the bathroom alone. I often make my bed--they are very
clean here but are quite short of help."

A 1978 card to a friend told about a recent illness in the Mitchell
Methodist Hospital where she had received over seventy cards for her birthday.
"I'm ninety-four now but feel more like forty nine.
