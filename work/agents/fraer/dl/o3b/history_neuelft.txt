NEU ELFT - FERE CHAMPENOISE I

The residents of the second Fere Champenoise colony, of protestant confession,
emigrated, for the most part, in this nineteenth century in the years 1801-4.
As children they came with their parents from different areas in the kingdoms
of Prussia and Wuerttemberg. They emigrated to Poland, where, generously
supported by the royal Prussian Government, they were settled in various
places within the Polish Kingdom. Troubled in Poland by revolutions and
wartime unrests, they welcomed the kind invitation by the high Russian
Government which promised them land and support for their new settlement in
Russia.  In the spring of  1814, under the guidance of a Russian official,
president Mueller,  they left Poland and arrived in Moldava near the city
Sorokoi in August of the same year. There they were given quarters in the
surrounding villages and support from the high Crown until in June 1816 they
were settled in the Kugelnik valley,  80 miles from the provincial capital
Kishinev, 60 miles from the County seat Ackermann and 52 miles from the town
of Ismail. The whole settlement consisted of 126 families. Herdsmen had leased
the steppes only for grazing until this time, therefore no houses existed for
them to occupy. Their own resources, though different, were generally modest,
approximately 20 - 100 silver rubles per family. Each family received some
building material, several house and field implements, two or three cows and
monthly a certain amount of flour and groats until the next harvest. For this
the high Crown charged each family the sum of 953 bank rubles which the
colonists have been repaying periodically since 1833.  At the time of the
settlement the colony was called Nr.11, but a few years later received the
name Fere Champenoise by order of the government, probably commemorating the
victory of the Russians over the French near Champenoise, France, in 1813.

Because of the great distance of the allotted land the colonists were unable
to fully use it. In 1825 they divided it into two equal parts. With
governmental consent they founded a new colony 5 miles south in the Alaga
valley,  the same distance from the provincial city Kishinev and the county
seat Ackermann but 47 miles from the town of Ismail, and named it New or 2nd
Champenoise. It is situated on the border of the German colonies and only 2.7
miles from the Bulgarian colony Dewletahatsch in the same valley. The steppe's
surface is undulating, the soil is mostly black containing alkali-rich humus,
on the hillsides mixed with clay and yellow sand. It is especially suited for
the cultivation of grain and grass,  in some areas also trees, although with
less success. It is least suited for wine-growing because the soil is too poor
and contains too much alkali. Near the colony, on the eastern slope, under a
layer of about 8 feet sand, is a layer of rock, mostly limestone, for the
needs of the settlers.

Already in the first year of the founding all houses were built of stone and
covered with reed,  with the  colonists'  own funds,  using also salvageable
materials from their former torn down homes. The village is almost 1.4 miles
long, separated into two rows of houses with well proportioned stone walls on
both sides of the street. Bordering them are lovely, shady orchards which,
unfortunately, are beginning to die after only 15 - 20 years of growth. In the
middle, on the slope of the eastside, is the chapel, the school and the grain
storehouse. Opposite, on the slope of the westside, is the town hall as well
as two other,  smaller municipal buildings. There is no spring water. Every
homestead has a well, although most of them produce salty and bitter water,
only a few have good drinking water. On the western side of the village the
Alaga Creek cuts through the orchards and vegetable gardens. Swollen with snow
and rain water at times it causes floods and destruction in the gardens. In
the summer, during long periods of drought, it dries up. This is a short
description of the present situation of New Fere Champenoise.

In retrospect of the 32 years the colonists have lived in this land until
1848, i.e. 9 years in Alt- and 23 years here in New Fere Champenoise, it
becomes clear that they had to experience and learn many things they never
anticipated before their immigration. Still they have not reached the goals,
towards which they struggle, which the government promised them. When they
arrived in this country, knowing neither language nor customs of the land,
they often were cheated and robbed. Unaccustomed to the Climate many died of
fever and dysentery within the first years of their immigration. Too poor to
pay their dead the last respect in an honorable manner they often committed
them to the earth wrapped in reeds and rags.

The fields were inadequately cultivated. Three to four farmers had to pool
their animals to harness a plow and could not always bring the seed into the
ground on time. Locusts and poor harvests brought added burdens. Many families
were forced to earn their bread working for Bulgarians or Moldavians.

The separation from Old Fere Champenoise and the establishing of this colony
ushered in a new period. The fields were close by and fully utilized, they
tilled them with new courage and built up the farmsteads. Even then certain
occurrences  endangered  their wellbeing.  In  1829  the plague  raged through
the upper part of the colony for two months, 15 people died of it. All
communication with the other colonies was severed during this time. This was
followed in 1830 by cholera which again took the lives of 15 people. 1835 and
1844-45 claimed half of the livestock through diseases (Hoof and mouth dis.?)
1834 and 1846 had disastrous harvests, 1840-41 a very cold winter and 1846 and
1847 terrible drought. The latter also had locusts that reproduced rapidly
which created a severe lack of feed and forced the farmers to sell their
livestock  at  a  very  low price.  This  reduced  the  present  count  to 
the following: 206 horses, 818 cattle and 1361 sheep. In spite of such
difficult circumstances and so few plentiful  harvests  the colony is 
improving and prospering, thanks, in part, to the diligence of the female
gender. From wool and flax the women make underwear and clothes for their own
needs, which saves a lot of money, and occasionally for sale.

As to their own houses and inventory so also much effort was devoted to the
communal buildings. In 1832 a chapel was built, in 1838 a new school, in 1840
the grain storehouse and in 1844 the old schoolhouse, which was built at the
founding of the colony and also served for the Sunday worship service, was
remodeled to house the mayor's office.

Many orchards, woodlands and vineyards have been planted and produced good
profits, especially last year. This spring the hope for a promising fruit and
grape harvest was suddenly dashed by a frost on April 25 and 26.

Discipline and order in the colony, the condition of the school as well as the
singing in the church have seen a gratifying improvement.  At present the
colony consists of 537 souls of both genders. One cannot speak of actual
wealth, yet,  compared to the past with its painful misfortunes, the present
conditions are favorable, thanks to the grace of God and the excellent
provision of the high colonial offices and also the faithful assistance of the
clerical and secular city council.

May the merciful God continue to bestow His grace and blessing on this colony
and all their leaders and promoters of their wellbeing.

New Fere Champenoise, 28 April 1848
Mayor: Inske
Assistant: Kraft, Sugland
Author: I. Kurz, Church and School Teacher
