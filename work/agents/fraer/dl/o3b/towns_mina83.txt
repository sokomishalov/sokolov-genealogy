the past as well as the present. Set around a gold star for the Centennial are
the Town Hall and Highway 12 Overpass; the Mina School; the Mina Equity
Elevator; Tobiason's Store and Vera Barker's home containing the Post Office;
The Milwaukee Railroad, Mina Church and Lake Parmley. All are ringed by red
bricks from the Mina Brickyard and the dates of the Centennial Celebration.

THE SAGA OF MINA
The saga of Mina, S.D.
Who never amounted to what she could be
She was named for fame, power and wealth
But there were numerous things that affected her health
Drought, grasshoppers, snow and minus zero degrees
And that many times was with a very stiff breeze

She was named after Mina Earling, not?
Who's Dad was a Milwaukee road big shot
She had numerous stores, livery stable, lumber yard,
A brick yard, grain elevator and a livestock yard
She had a bank and though it grew
It went broke in thirty two
Her school started in the fall of eighty four
And continued for eighty six years or more
From thirty six to forty two she graduated twelve year classes
There were ninety lads and lasses
Who got their twelve year degree
And ventured into the world as smart as could be
In nineteen seventy marked the final day
That school kids came to Mina to study and play

In the dirty thirties an idea took form
A spillway would be built and a lake would be born
The name would be easy for goodness sake
She would name it after herself, Mina Lake
She soon filled with water to her fullest capacity
Now perhaps that would be the city
The people came to live on her shore
There are now about three forty four

Of her baseball teams, some were great
They were well known across the state
The early settlers through hard work and toil
As they cleared and broke the soil
Left a legacy to us all to follow, that strife
Hardships and hard times are all part of life
So take the bad with the good and sometimes through tears
As we observe Mina's first one hundred years.

Elroy Eske

DAKOTA TERRITORY

The U.S. Congress created Dakota Territory in 1861 which consisted of North
Dakota, South Dakota and much of Montana and Wyoming.

The Homestead Act of 1862 also brought people to this area. The Act was passed
to encourage the migration of settlers westward and wheat farming on the
prairies. The Act offered free land up to 160 acres to any family head or to
any man 21 or over owning no more than 160 acres in the U.S. The new settler
received a patent for his claim after establishing five years occupancy and
making improvements on the homestead, or after 14 months continuous residence
and a payment of $1.25/acre.

Some of the land was offered through great land lotteries. People registered
for the land and received claims if they were lucky in the lottery-drawing.

Special trains brought people from all parts of the U.S. to take part in the
lotteries. Indian wars prevented rapid settlement of the Territory. The Sioux
Indians would raid troops trying to survey for roads. In the Laramie Treaty of
1868, the land west of the Missouri River was given to the Indians and no roads
were to be made west of the River. However, after gold was discovered in the
Hills, this treaty fell by the roadside.

Edmunds County was created in 1873 and organized in 1883. President Lincoln
appointed Newton Edmunds, a New Yorker, as chief clerk in the surveyor's
office. He served until 1866. Newton Edmunds became the second Governor of
Dakota Territory and Edmunds County is named after him.

In 1883 the Northwestern and the C. M. & St. Paul Railroad were advertising
what great advantages were to be found in and around Aberdeen, S. D. The
advertisements were printed in the Buffalo, N. Y. and Jamestown, Ohio papers to
lead people to believe that Aberdeen was a little Chicago. After a nine or ten
day trip from the east aboard a train, the conductor would yell "A-ber-deen".
The people would look in every direction but could see nothing. When the
passengers asked the conductor, "I thought you said we were in Aberdeen." The
conductor answered, "So we are." The passengers remarked, "But where is the
town?" The conductor said, "Can't you see those sod shanties over there?" The
passengers answered, "Yes, but that isn't Aberdeen." The conductor replied,
"Well, then you know more than I do."

After unloading a few hundred passengers, the train went north to Columbia.
Many people returned immediately to the East after seeing the city.

At the time that Edmunds and Brown Counties were organized  Range 66 was not
part of any county. It had been omitted by the territorial legislature in
defining the boundaries between the two counties. In 1885 the territorial
legislature changed the boundaries of Edmunds Co. by annexing Range 66. Range
66 included Pembrook, Cortlandt, Richland and Clear Lake Townships. Edmunds Co.
is now 48 miles long by 24 miles wide.

Because the people in northern Dakota Territory asked Congress if they could
have a separate government from the southern territory, Congress divided the
area into states. On Nov. 2, 1889 South Dakota became the 40th state.

There was a severe drought in 1889-1897 and many people left the area.

Various Indian scares in the Dakotas kept young men of Edmunds Co. quite army
minded in 1890. In March the Edmunds Co. Commission granted a petition from a
large number of young men from eastern Edmunds Co. to use the court room for
drill purposes. The drill groups were to form a military company. There were
three drilling groups. This made the people feel safe from the depredations by
the Indians. 1890 was the last big fight between Indians and the white man on
the northern plains and this was the Battle of Wounded Knee. Population by 1900
was 350,000; population by 1910 was 584,000.

There used to be many more farms then there are today. Most of the farms were
two to three quarters. During the 30's land was often sold for as low as $5.00
per acre and there were not too many buyers.

HISTORY OF MINA, S.D. 1883-1983

On May 2, 1883, Dr. L. F. Diefendorf, the first physician in Aberdeen, erected
a small store and post office along the Snake Creek which was also on the stage
line from Aberdeen to Freeport. The post office was called Cortlandt; also had
mill and hotel. On July 12, 1890, the name was changed from Cortland to Mina.
Many believe this was a tribute to the daughter of A. J. Earling, the president
of the Milwaukee Railroad. The post office was moved three miles southeast to
the present site because of the railroad being built west of Aberdeen.

On Sept. 28, 1882, W. V. Bold, R. W. Hunter and John Davis built the first
shanties west of Cortlandt which is now Mina. Messrs. Hunter and Davis returned
east and Mr. Bold, along with Ezra Drew and Ira Dibble from Georgetown, were
the sole occupants of this portion of Edmunds County for the winter. In 1883
Governor Ordway appointed the first Board of County Commissioners and the men
were L. F. Diefendorf, Loren A. Barbour and Charles N. Skillman.

In October 1883 the railroad came through the site where Mina now stands. The
first station agent was W. B. Dilly and after him the position went to W. J.
Randall. In 1890 E. H. Prescott came to Mina from Ipswich to be the station
agent. A saloon was the first business establishment. George Wescott operated a
hotel and livery barn. He also had the post office. He owned both until 1895.
He sold both places and each was moved to a different farm. William Hope
started the first general store. He was also postmaster and agent for Cargil
and Bagly Grain Co. In 1893 he closed the store and it remained vacant until
1898 when E. H. Prescott opened a general store which he operated for a few
years. He later sold the store to B. J. Ferrill.

The first blacksmithing in Mina was done by John Nesbitt. He also was a pony
express rider, and ran the post office after Wescott left. Mr. Nesbitt occupied
the same house that is now on the Millard Farm. After John Nesbitt moved to the
farm about 1898, Ole Anderson ran the post office and operated the general
store and feed store for about ten years. This would be the present location of
the Don Wagner home in Mina.

About 1905 stockyards were constructed east of the elevator by the railroad
company. It was large enough to hold approximately 100 head of cattle.

Farmers would drive the cattle by horses to the stockyards. The cattle would
usually be shipped to Sioux City for the farmer received a better price. Often
the farmer would ride with his cattle to the market so he could watch them being
sold. Sometimes a farmer ten miles out of town would start the drive and
farmers would add their cattle as the drive passed their farms. After the
cattle were in the stockyards, they would relax with a pop which cost five
cents. In 1934 the government purchased the cattle from the farmers because
there wasn't any feed for the cattle and the cattle weren't worth anything. The
government paid five or six dollars each. The cattle were shot and buried in a
pit and covered up. The 1940's were the peak years for the stockyards. They
were closed shortly after that time. Shock Caldwell was buyer for the Coop and
Fred Goodspeed was buyer for Sioux City, Iowa.

The Hasvold Brothers also constructed a stone building around this time where
the Mina Elevator garage is today. This building was sold several times and in
1934 was sold to the school for dormitories. It was later sold to the Mina
Elevator.

Grain companies put up flathouses for handling grain until 1902 when Victorian
Elevator Co. erected a modern elevator. In 1909 Hasvold Brothers built an
elevator. At this same time a blacksmith shop was started by Ben Sherewbury. He
left after a few years and Mina was without a welder until Bill Papke
established his shop. N. R. and Ida McClanahan also purchased the general store
from Bennie Ferrill and kept it until 1937.

Thompson Yards from Aberdeen established a lumber company in Mina and was
located across the street from Carol's Store. Lumber yard was managed by Mr.
Link. About 1924 the lumber yard closed and a cream station opened and was run
by Oscar Bronkow.

A bank was chartered in 1909 by Charles Barkel and later sold to C. C. Conklin.
At that time banks could be declared insolvent and closed whenever the owner
wanted it. This happened in Mina in 1925.

Mina also had a telephone and telegraph office. Mr. Weatherly taught boys the
Morse Code.

International Farm Equipment was located at the present site of the elevator as
was an artesian well dug by Frank Millard. The people would water their horses
when they came to town.

In the fall of 1884 the first Sunday School was started by Bob Finch as
superintendent. When a school house was built, it also served as a church. The
First Presbyterian Church was organized in 1887 but because of hard times it
ceased to exist after a few years. A country minister would have two or three
churches to serve so the churches would take turns having services. Sometimes
it might be a Methodist from Ipswich and next time a Congregational from
Aberdeen. The First Presbyterian Church was reorganized in 1907 under the
leadership of Rev. C. C. Todd with a charter of 18 members. After 62 years of
operation, the church held its last service on June 1, 1969 with remaining
members going to church in Aberdeen, S. D.

Mina also had a brick factory which was established in the spring of 1910 by D.
D. Jones. It was called the Aberdeen Brick Co. and employed 15 to 18 men. This
plant was capable of producing 18 to 20 thousand bricks a day with a million
and half for a session. It discontinued operation in 1922 and was moved to
Scranton, N. D. in 1928. some buildings built with Mina brick are Mina School,
Ipswich Public School, Holy Cross Parochial School, Jones Building, Parmely
Home all of which are located in Ipswich. There are also some buildings in
Aberdeen built with these bricks. Some of the homes in the older section of
Aberdeen (south of Sacred Heart Church) are made with these brick.

In 1915 Hasvold Brothers sold their elevator to the Farmers and two years later
the Farmers also purchased the Victorian Elevator. The present Mina Elevator
was built in 1930 and was proclaimed as one of the largest in the state.

Mina Hall was built in 1921 by the Mina Lodge Association. People bought stock
to make it possible. It was also used as a community hall such as meetings, and
dances. In 1934 it was turned into dormitories for the school. Mina school was
consolidated on 1962 and closed in the 1969-70 school year.

1937 the general store again changed owners to Melvin and Sybil Henerson. In
the 30's a man could work part time unloading box cars of coal at the Mina
Elevator. He would get 100 per ton for unloading. However, if he unloaded a
30-ton carload he would only receive $1.50. Most of the farmers worked on the
township roads hauling gravel. They could make $15 per week which was quite a
lot of money in those days and enabled people to survive the Dust Storm Days.

At one time there was also a hotel and a mill.

U.S. Highway 12 was relocated in 1969 and made wider. The new highway followed
the railroad tracks from Aberdeen to Mina. An overpass was built at Mina for
the train. It would have been too expensive to widen the original highway at
Mina Lake because of the two bridges. This phase of construction went from
Aberdeen to Ipswich.

The town of Mina has 30 full time residents and the town consists of Carol's
Grocery Store and Post Office, Mina Equity Exchange Grain Elevator and refined
fuels.

MINA

Shirley Lewis gives us the story of Mina as he lived it in the "Early Days".

We came to Edmunds County in 1883. Two of my brothers had come in the fall of
1882 and filed southwest of Mina. Our former home was in Wisconsin. In the
spring they came out with emigrant cars with lumber for a house and a
shed-barn, some horses, five cows, some household goods, chickens, machinery
and wagons.

We had to unload it at Aberdeen as that was the end of the road at that time.
The road was the stage line from Aberdeen to Freeport. Sometimes there were
fifty teams on the road at once.

A carpenter in Wisconsin had cut the lumber so it did not take so long to
build, although it seemed quite a while, yet after all, it seemed like a sort
of picnic as there was so much excitement, something new happening every day.
We were always watching for someone who was going to bring the mail for the
neighborhood, Aberdeen being the nearest post office until along towards fall
when Dr. Diefendorf, the first physician in Aberdeen, erected a small building
on a quarter situated on the north band of Snake Creek along the stage line from
Aberdeen to Freeport, and started a small store and post office. His wife and
daughter, Bertha, (now the wife of Dr. H. J. Rock) lived out there to hold the
claim. The post office was called the Cortlandt and when the railroad was built
west from Aberdeen the building and the post office were moved to Mina about
three miles southeast, but the office was known as Cortlandt for several years.

During the summer of '83 the C. M. & St. Paul RR sent its crew thru,
constructing the line west from Aberdeen and one of the things which I remember
about this is that Mr. Herrick, father of the Ipswich general merchant, had
charge of the commissionary department and my mother did baking for him, which
he used to come for every day before dinner driving a single horse hitched to a
democrat wagon.

When Mr. E. L. Cochran asked me to write some reminiscences he said either
pathetic or amusing and as a saloon was the first thing started in Mina, we had
some experiences which while they were pathetic in a way seem quite amusing as
we look back upon them now.

The first postmaster in Mina, while a pretty good fellow, was strongly addicted
to the 'glass that cheers', and whenever any registered mail or anything which
he thought especially important came to the off ice, he just put it in his coat
pocket and carried it where he went because he said he was not going to take
any chances on having it lost or being left in the office. One time when father
went to get the mail he insisted that father take a lot belonging to other
parties saying "You may just as well I have it Dave, I know they are busy today
and won't have time to read anyhow."

Next after the saloon J. A. Westgate, who died in Penn., a few years ago built
a hotel, the W. J. Hope, who has also passed away, erected a building and
started a general store. J. W. Nesbitt followed up with a blacksmith shop, two
grain companies put up flathouses for handling grain. Wade Dillie started a
lumber and coal business and these comprised the business part of Mina for
several years. Dillie was also the first depot agent, followed by Harvey
Randell and then by Ed Prescott, who has held the job ever since.

Mentioning the lumber yard has brought to my mind a couple of other incidents
that were rather amusing yet pathetic. One night after the boys had been having
a good time, an old bachelor, Ed Cody, by name, started for home lost his way
and wandered around until his feet got cold. It was a bright moonlight night
and happened along by the lumber yard, just then he rolled over on a pile of
boards and holding his feet up towards the moon said "There, if you can't get
warm by that big fire you can freeze." One of the others came along at that
time, heard him, got him up and took him home.

Another time when several of the bunch had gotten to feeling quite happy they
decided to butcher a hog that one of them had been fattening and having heard
that is easier to singe the hair off than scald it, they thought they would try
it, so went to the lumberyard got some sticks and built a frame, laid the hog
on it, put some straw underneath and set a fire. All went well until one of the
braces burned off and let the hog fall into the fire. The boys not being in a
steady condition, the result was the hog was well cooked on one side before
they could get it out of the fire.

But Mina was not given to wild performances, in the fall of '84, the first
Sunday School was started with Bob Finch as Superintendent, and when the school
house was built church services were held there part of the time by a minister
who preached in two or three country places, at other times ministers would
come from Aberdeen or Ipswich in the afternoon. Very often Mr. Levill of the
Methodist Church in Ipswich and Jas. H Kyle afterwards our U. S. Senator then
preaching in the Congregational Church would drive to Mina after holding
services in their own church.

Writing of this calls to my memory something which happened a little later on
when a friend E. L. Cochran was living in Mina and acting in the capacity of
Sunday School Superintendent, some of the boys thought they would have some fun
with him and dubbed him the "parson". One morning when he went to the post
office to get his mail there was quite a bunch standing around the stove
smoking and one of them knowing the smoke was distasteful to Ed and thinking
that a superintendent of Sunday Schools would be rather harmless stepped up
beside him and blew smoke from a strong pipe into his face. Ed moved away but
the man followed and did the same thing again. Ed remonstrated with him and
asked him not to do it again. For reply he got a full puff into the face. This
was a little too much and knocking the pipe out of his hand he grabbed him by
the collar with his left hand and proceeded to give him a good beating over the
head with his right.

We experienced the same difficulty around Mina in procuring something for fuel
that I have read about in some of the other letters already published, and all
sorts of contrivances were brought around to make use of the straw and hay. One
fellow was selling an affair shaped like a wash boiler with a draft in one end,
there were two in the set. One was to be filled with straw and placed over the
front part of the stove with the griddle off to burn while the other was being
packed to take its place which was just about enough to keep one person busy.
Another had a little machine like a miniature hay baler which pressed the hay or
straw into cubes about the size of two bricks that could be tied with strings
and stacked up to burn in the stove.

One of the years when F. K. Wing, Daniel Redman and Mr. Sims were on the Board
of Commissioners, they sent a carload of coal to Mina and appointed my father to
distribute it out in half ton lots to the most needy and the following spring
they shipped in a load of ear corn also to be given out in 18 bushels lots for
feed. In the winter of '88 I think it was, J. M. Merrill, one of the first
settlers southwest of Mina wrote friends in Ills. that some people were badly
in need of clothing and they sent her a barrel of old clothing and judging by
the contents they surely thought South Dakota was in a bad way and could use
most anything. She brought it over to our place and she and my mother spent a
day or two sorting things out, seeing what could be used around different
places. That same winter was the time of the awful January 12th blizzard which
was about the worst single experience the people ever had. We were fortunate and
all were at home with fuel enough to keep us warm and the stock all in the
barn, but some did not fare so well.

I remember well the first year I went to school in the first school house built
in this part of Edmunds County and that building is still in use in the south
west corner of Cleveland township. One day at noon we were all outside when
someone noticed a small man walking through the grass toward school. As he got
nearer we seen that he was Joe Parmley, the Supt. of Edmunds County. We all ran
for the school house and took our seats, thoroughly frightened, an looking back
now I cannot see any reason for us being scared. He had started south from
Ipswich, then east and back by way of Mina making the trip on foot.

Of the original settlers in the immediate vicinity of Mina who stayed long
enough to keep their claims and make homes were Ed Thomas, Chas. Cook, Geo.
Meyers, Jacob Brown, J. M. Merrill, John Healy, Mrs. Griffin, E. Goodspeed,
Gottlieb Tabisky, Bob Finch, Mr. Cole, Frank Downley, Fred Blood, Mrs. J. W.
Nesbitt and the Lewis'.

August 29, 1923 - Ipswich Tribune

MINA

The first town in Edmunds Co. coming from the east is Mina, located about 13
miles from Aberdeen and 13 miles east of Ipswich, the County Seat. This is the
trade center of a large territory, north and south and has several wide awake
business establishments to supply the needs of the community. There are two
well stocked general stores, the Mina Cash store and the N. R. McClanahan and
Co. store; the 2 large elevators, owned and operated by the Mina Equity
Exchange, a farmers cooperative organization, managed by Mr. E.G. Wheelock coal
being handled in connection where with; a well stocked lumber yard owned by
Thompson Yards, Inc. the Farmers State Bank, a well managed and strong
Financial institution and the Mfg. plant of Aberdeen Pressed Brick Co. whose
output is in excess of 100 cars annually. This company manufactures a high
grade pressed brick, and has been established and operated for the past 10
years. As monuments to the quality of its splendid product many of the best
buildings in Northern South Dakota are pointed to, The Mina Public Schools,
Ipswich Public School, Holy Cross Parochial School, Jones and Deschner
buildings and the Parmley residence at Ipswich, the Perry Garage, the Boyd-
Romans garage, the West High School, the Schaffer Hotel and many others in
Aberdeen are among the attractive and substantial structures built of Mina
Brick.

The factory normally employs 12 men and the product is sold several months in
advance. The supply of raw material is practically inexhaustible, and while the
plant is not yet of large size, its owners have faith in its future and are
increasing it facilities along with the increasing demand for their product It
is confidently believed that a great factory to manufacture large line of clay
products will eventually be developed here.

Mina has a 2 room brick school building. A Presbyterian church and one of the
best community halls in this part of the state. The business men are a unit for
any movement or enterprise that makes a better Mina and a better community.

MINA EQUITY EXCHANGE

The Mina Equity Exchange was chartered in June of 1915. On August 17, 1915 they
purchased an elevator for $4800, paying $1900, leaving the balance on the
elevator unpaid secured by mortgage drawing 6 percent interest. This left
nothing in the way of capital to do business so a loan was taken from the
Farmers State Bank of Mina for $500. There were 67 stockholders, the most of
them beginning with only one share of $25.

FREIGHT CAR BURNS MINA NEWS - OCT. 14, 1938

Freight car number 91006 was burned in Mina Thursday afternoon at 3:15 p.m. The
origin of the fire was unknown but it was suggested by Conductor 0. E. Bolan
that it may have been started by transients, who were seen on the train, by
sparks from "right-of-way" burning, or other reasons.

The train was sidetracking for a flyer when the fire was noticed, and the train
crew lost no time in uncoupling the burning car.

Due to the lack of water in Mina, there was no way of fighting it except to
blow steam on it from the engine. This was done, but the pipe where the steam
comes from was stationary and it could not reach the top; so they had to let it
burn.

The loss was estimated at about $900.00.

Engineer Mattice has been on the railroad a long time but this was the first
car he lost during his service.

CAR STOLEN FROM FRANK SCHWAB IS FOUND: TAKEN TO HITCHCOCK

A car belonging to Frank Schwab of Mina, which was stolen from its parking
space on the street in Aberdeen Saturday, was located in Hitchcock yesterday by
Sheriff A. W. Schumacher. As soon as it was known that the car was missing,
Schumacher was notified. He immediately got in communication with officials
along roads leading from Aberdeen, asking that they be on the lookout for the
car. The thief succeeded in getting as far as Hitchcock before he was
apprehended.

The car was uninsured and should a successful get-away have been made Mr.
Schwab would have been a heavy loser. Our Sheriff's prompt action in recovering
the car is to be commended. Mr. Schumacher left today for Hitchcock to bring
the car back to its owner.

MINA FARMERS STATE BANK

In 1909 a bank was chartered by Charles Barkel and named MINA FARMERS STATE
BANK. It was later sold to C. C. Conklin.

On August 31, 1922 at about 1:30 a.m. the telegraph wires were cut. Shortly
thereafter two men entered the bank and used six charges to blow open the
vault. After each charge was placed two men came outside the bank until after
the explosion. The noise awoke several people. Milton Hanson and Paul Tuft were
returning from a dance and saw the car used by the cracksmen standing on the
south side of the lumber company. They examined the car and found it contained
a rifle and a flashlight. They took, the number of the car but seeing no one
around they went home. It was nearly five o'clock before the robbers left town
going south with $1,500 in actual cash, $250 worth of bonds and a number of
notes, aggregating about $60,000. The notes were of no value to anyone but the
bank.

No notice was given the sheriff until the train arrived in the morning.

Rewards totaling $550 had been offered for the arrest and conviction of the
bandits.

State and county officials were called to Randolph where brown paper was found
and thought to be part of the robbery loot. George Hawson was working near
Randolph and received some merchandise from his wire wrapped in brown paper. He
was staying with the Frank Dean family. Mrs. Dean had prepared a lunch for the
threshers who were leaving for North Dakota in this brown paper.

The men happened to eat their lunch near the strawstack and left the paper on
the ground where the officers found it.

On Sept. 7, 1922 John Logan, an Aberdeen man, confessed his involvement with
robbing banks and stores in this area of the state. Also arrested were Charles
Valin and Walter Baker as they got off the train in Minneapolis. On Oct. 26,
1922 Walter Baker pleaded guilty to bank robbery and was sentenced to twenty
years in the state pen. Charles Valin pleaded innocent and stood trial. The
outcome is unknown.

This same building was also used as a post office during the time that Mrs.
Caldwell and Veva Barker were Postmasters.

The bank building is now the home of Mr. & Mrs. Owen Brick.

STONE BUILDING

The Hasvold Brothers built a stone building where the present garage for the
Mina Elevator is setting. This was used for various things throughout its
history such as a one-lane bowling alley and a barber shop upstairs with Will
Insley as barber. It was used as a general store. It was sold to "Mens" Moulton.

There was a card room in the basement owned by J. H. Shaner. Mr. Quashnick then
became the owner and he sold out to "Doc" Molaby. Lizzie Behselich, who is
Lawrence Kaven's mother, worked in the store. There was an apartment upstairs
along with a dance hall. There were times when there were two dances held a
week with a good turnout and a good time. He sold the building to Roy and Alice
Goodspeed. They had a grocery store in the front of the building and a garage
in the back. In 1923 the Goodspeeds had a grocery store in the north side of
Ole Anderson's house and had the supplies in a railroad box car. In those days
the Gypsies used to stop in and take over the store so Alice kept an unloaded
revolver and when they stopped she would take the revolver and chase them out
of the store. They sold the store to Julia, Mae and Paul Tuft.

In 1934 they sold it to the school district. It was then used for a dormitory
upstairs and a dining room downstairs. Later it was sold to the elevator and
they constructed the present garage on the land.

MINA HALL

A Lodge Hall was promoted and constructed by The Royal Neighbors and Modern
Woodman. Some of the citizens that promotedthis building were Conklin,
McClanahan, Prescott and Craven. They were also assisted by their wives.  The
Lodge Hall was second to none in the country and was the leader in Edmunds
County.  It had a full basement set off into a dining room, kitchen, gymnasium,
coat room, etc.  The Lodge rooms occupied the main floor, which was reached by
a flight of five or six easy steps.  The building was  preeminently a lodge
hall but was used by the community for dancing, meetings and other social
functions.  Sometimes it was called a pleasure center.

In 1934 it was donated to the Mina School District and used for classrooms,
gymnasium, dormitories, principal's office and living quarters until 1942.  It
was then rented out for special occasions until 1969 when Clarence Papke
purchased it and remodeled it for the post office and tavern. The tavern was
closed in 1979. About a year later the Papke's retired from the post office and
that was moved to the Mina Store. The Papke's still live in the bottom level.

MINA GROCERY STORE

William Hope started a general store in the late 1800's. He also had the post
office in the same store. A few years later he sold the store to Elsie and Ben
Ferrill who had it for several years. They sold it to N. R. McClanahan and his
wife Ida. In 1904 it burned down and they rebuilt the present store. They had
the store and post office for a number of years. Eventually Mr. McClanahan took
the mail route and his brother-in-law, Harry Lewis, took over the post office.
The McClanahans had the store until 1936, then sold it to Mr. & Mrs. M. J.
Henderson. They had the store until 1944 when they sold it to Marjorie and Art
Tobiason. Marjorie ran the store and Art worked at the Mina Elevator part-time
and farmed with his father-in-law Fred Goodspeed. Art passed away in 1978 and
Marjorie ran the store until July 1981 when she sold it to Carol and Kenny
Pulling. Carol also has the Community Post Office in the store. Kenny works for
Safety Service in Aberdeen, S. D.

MINA LAKE

Mina Dam impounds one of the first man-made lakes in northern South Dakota. It
may be said to have served as a pattern in the construction of other large
artificial lakes and as an example in initiating a comprehensive water
conservation program in this section of the State.

Mina Lake came into being as an outgrowth of a project to divert the waters of
Snake Creek into Scatterwood Lake, 28 miles southwest of Aberdeen, which at the
time (1930-31) was nearly dry. Scatterwood Lake had been a beautiful body of
water providing excellent recreational facilities and had also been well
adapted as a breeding ground for waterfowl.

The plan was to construct a dam at the fork of its two branches in Cortlandt
Township and by means of a spillway and a two-mile diversion ditch lead the
waters into Grass Lake and thence into Scatterwood by means of the so-called
"Pickus" ditch constructed some years before.

J. W. Parmley of Ipswich, former member of the State Highway Commission and
identified with the public seal of the State, was a leader in the movement. At
the outset he retained A. S. Mannes, Aberdeen civil engineer, to run levels
from Scatterwood Lake to Snake Creek three miles each and to other points north
upstream in order to determine the feasibility of diversion.

It was found that the Scatterwood Lake bed was 14 feet higher than the bed of
Snake Creek east three miles.

More detailed surveys, carried out by the State Engineer and an engineer
attached to the Game and Fish Dept., revealed that a 35 foot dam constructed at
the forks of the Creek would create a lake from 20 to 30 feet in depth for a
distance of about two miles from the dam. These surveys also indicated that
water from 8 to 16 feet in depth would be impounded for some distance in the
upper arms of both branches of the creek, creating a lake with a shore line of
approximately 15 miles.

When it was discovered that the plan to divert the flow of water from the dam
under the Milwaukee railroad tracks to Scatterwood was not feasible by reason
of the expense, the plan to create a lake near the village of Mina as an
independent body of water was born.

On June 29, 1932, a meeting was called on the site of the proposed new lake. A
joint committee of Brown and Edmunds Counties and a delegation representing the
Game and Fish Dept., together with a tremendous crowd of interested people,
were in attendance. It is estimated that 1000 automobiles were on the grounds
on the occasion.

Several addresses were made outlining the possibilities of the project. Chet
Snyder, Chairman of the Game and Fish Commission, offered little encouragement,
stating that funds at the disposal of the commission were inadequate for so
large a project. The principal boosters of the plan were not discouraged. A
short time later another meeting was called at Huron. This was attended by a
large delegation of Mina Lake promoters. The Mina Lake boosters now had
sufficient data to impress the group that the project was not only feasible but
a likely success.

By the time work was finally started on the dam, September 5, 1933, the
committee had met nearly every Monday night including Ka Squire, Theodore
Wosnuk, W. J. Tiffany, J. W. Parmley, Elmer Lass, H. J. & M. B. Bebermeyer,
Thomas Hanson, Frank L. Sieh, N. R. McClanahan, Frank Schwab, Alfred Lindboe,
Leon Wells, N. C. Haley, F. H. McGinnis, A. Mannes, Clifford Lee, Hugh Agor, H.
C. Behrens, A. E. Herrboldt, Charles A. Russell, and James A. Matthews.

Labor and obtaining easements were two big problems. Unemployment at this time
was very huge. With the Emergency Relief Administration of the Federal
Government ready to supply the labor, this problem had been solved.

856 acres of land would eventually be flooded by the impounded waters. The land
was privately owned and easements had to be secured to protect the Game and Fish
Commission from damage claims. There were no funds to secure the easements. It
was not long until the Real Estate Committee had raised $4,500 to obtain and
pay for the necessary easements. Land owners were reluctant to grant permission
to flood any of their holdings. Mr. Behrens went in person to Chicago to secure
permission from the Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific Railroad. He did
this at his own expense. The Railroad was more than eager to help and provided
materials and labor to install a railroad bridge over Snake Creek.

December 15, 1933 was declared Mina Dam and Lake Day. An entertainment benefit
was held in Ipswich, Mina and Aberdeen with proceeds going to the Mina Dam
project. There were also dances called "Dam Dances" held at Mina to raise money
for the project.

Highway Departments of Brown and Edmunds Counties loaned their road equipment
and supplied some of the operating funds. Labor was drawn from the Federal
Emergency Relief Administration rolls.

100 teams of horses were used to fill the backside of the dam and every seven
days 200 new workers would start work through the WPA Program.

Just below the structure, a natural amphitheater with unlimited possibilities
was also planned. It was to be a place for staging big sports events, rodeo,
etc. Sportsmen said it was one of the best locations for a gigantic bowl of the
State. It was estimated by terracing the hill just below the dam on the south
side and leveling off the land at the location, a bowl with a seating capacity
of 25,000 can easily be made.

On February 4, 1934 Engineers pronounced the dam complete. It had required
three years of promotion effort but less than six months of actual labor to
build. The dam was 900 feet long and 44 feet high. The spillway rose 34 ft.
from the bed of the creek, thus providing more than 30 feet of water in the
lake at the Dam. It is also estimated that the lake holds 3,175,000,000 gallons
of water at capacity. The Lake covers 850 acres. Water went over the spillway
for the first time in the spring of 1943.

Millions of fish fry were planted. Sportsmen from Aberdeen and Ipswich watched
operations which were under the direction of Mr. Van Luer of the Game and Fish
Dept. Men from the Pickerel Lake hatchery and several game wardens helped.
About eighteen large cans of walleyed pike fry were planted, estimated to be
about half a million fish. The fry are about a quarter to a half inch in
length. The temperature of the water must be equalized gradually so as not to
endanger the lives of the small fish. This was done by setting the cans filled
with fry in the lake near the Snyder Farm where the temperature became the same
as that of the lake water.

The next operation was for two men with a rowboat to take several cans out into
the deep part of the lake and there gradually release the can full into the
lake water. This is done so that the small fish can go to the bottom of the
deep water and there find vegetation which will protect them from other larger
fish and also furnish food for growth.

U.S. 12, The Yellowstone Trail, crossed both arms of the lake on grades almost
equal in dimensions to the dam. Both sides of the grades and upper side of the
dam are riprapped with hand-laid granite boulders thus preventing wave-wash.
These crossings give transcontinental travelers splendid views of one of the
largest lakes in the central northwest.

The Committee named the lake Shake Maza, a Sioux Indian term for "shaped like a
horseshoe." The name never caught on and the lake was officially renamed Lake
Parmely. The lake is commonly called Mina.

The land between the two arms of the lake was intended to be a public park. The
Mina Dam Committee raised the necessary funds ($3,100) and purchased the land
in 1938. Edmunds County accepted donation of the land and managed the park
until 1950. Since 1969, the Game, Fish and Parks has managed the area. In 1974
an additional 204 acres was purchased to add to the original 90-acre park.

Many trees were planted as a WPA project. Many trees were also donated by the
Game and Fish Commission and by the U.S. Biological Survey.

To insure sufficient moisture for the plants during dry periods, an irrigation
system had been installed. A condemned railroad water tank was purchased and
donated to the Park by the Aberdeen chapter of the Izaak Walton League. More
than 6,000 linear feet of terracing was done on the shore line slopes during
1938-39 and a shore line drive was graded and an entrance road improved from US
12 by WPA.

Today the park was a spacious 36-unit campground complete with comfort station.
A boat dock near the campground and several beaching areas. There is also a
swimming area with a floating dock, water slide and a spacious sand beach.
There is a picnic area with a play fort, swing sets and other play equipment. A
2-lane boat-launching ramp and, of course, fishing. Crappie, Walleye, Northern
Pike, Perch, Bass and Bullhead are stocked in the Lake. There is also a ramp
for the physically handicapped.

Adam Oberlander had the first claim at Mina Lake. He won a lot with Charles
Doolittle and Howard Loss. They won the lot at a dance at Mina by drawing their
names from a hat.

Mina Lake was built to be a 50-year lake but because of conservation practices,
there is less than two feet of silt at the present time.

A Sanitary Water District was formed in 1976 and through these efforts a
central water system was built and put into service in Sept., 1979. There is a
proposed sewage disposal system being studied and the bid letting was March,
1983. Completion of the project is set for the fall of 1983. There are
approximately 200 full-time residents living at the lake. There are also
various businesses operating along the lake.

HISTORY OF THE MINA POST OFFICE

A post office was established in Edmunds County, Dakota Territory on May 2,
1883 as Cortlandt. Cortlandt, Sec. 23-123-66, was located one and a half miles
northwest from the present location. The postmasters were: L. F. Diefendorf (He
is the man that started Cortlandt.), William J. Hope, John W. Nesbitt, Judson
A. Westgate, Ola Anderson and Nelson R. McClanahan.

A post office was also established at Albion, Sec. 22-122-66, in 1890 at the
John Cunningham home. Frank A. Finch was appointed postmaster. Other
postmasters were: Henry S. Hitcheofck, Mary J. Cunningham, Harry C. Cunningham.
A mail route was started in 1908 and was 33 miles long. Alfred Poole was the
first carrier.

July 12, 1909 Cortlandt and Albion were changed to Mina. Postmasters were:
Nelson R. McClanahan, Esther S. Gearey, James J. Lewis, Winnie Lewis, Mertie E.
Caldwell, Veva M. Barker, Clarence W. Papke, Ellen M. Papke and Carol Pulling
is the current postmaster.

Mr. Nelson R. McClanahan also started the north mail route form Mina in the
late twenties. He carried mail with a Model-T Ford coupe. When winters were
severe with a lot of snow he carried the mail with a team of horses and sled
with a little cab on the sled with a kerosene heater on it for warmth. In order
to make his route in one day he had farmers on horseback help him get the mail
delivered. You would always see "Mac", as he was known to everyone, even those
stormy days.

Patrons on "Mac's" mail route starting from Mina were: Herb Shaner, William
Lewis, O. J. Snyder, Frank Millard, Ed Nesbitt, Gerhard Artz, Pete Kessler,
Harry Wendelin, John Hoffman, Frank Schwab, Charlie Boulais, Cy Udell, John
Brooks, Wendelin Usselman, Anton Malsom, Mike Dutenhoffer, Mike Kessler, John
Usselman, Adam Usselman, Louis Usselman, Joe Lacher, Andrew Braun, Kesslers,
Wendelin Dosch, Kramers, Grover Winter, Paul Heinz, Case Bros., Lonnie
Crawford, A. P. Hasvold, VanOrman, Pete Walberg, Shock Caldwell and Fred
Goodspeed.

Before the north route was consolidated with the south route, Paul Case was
carrier until his death in 1943. Bill Case then became carrier until the
change-over with Harrison Gearey. Art Tobiason was assistant.

In 1921 Harrison G. Gearey started carrying the mail on Tr. #1. He also used a
Model-T Ford and a team of horses for his travels on 33 miles of dirt road.
Later on a Rt. #2 was added and his delivery career ended with 67 miles of
graded roads. He served under six postmasters. At times snow was so deep he
would have to change horses half-way and would reach home around 11 o'clock at
night. Sometimes he would stay overnight at a patron's home and return to Mina
the next day.

If a farmer needed anything from the store at Mina, like a pound of coffee, a
note was left in the mailbox and the next day Harrison would bring it. He never
charged postage for the items.

One year he could only travel on horseback but most of the time he walked to
keep from freezing and save the strength of his horse. The mail bag was as much
weight as the animal could carry.

Weather conditions never stopped Harrison - he only failed to deliver mail 12
days in the 42 years that he delivered mail.

He used 18 cars for delivering mail. He retired in 1963.

Ed Lacher took Harrison's position and at present is still a mail carrier for
Mina. When he started hauling mail in 1963 his route was 66 miles and 89 boxes.
Today his route is 135 miles and 225 boxes. Atlas Wendt is assistant mail
carrier.

The post office changed to a Community Post Office in 1981 because of the
federal government trying to cut expenses and improve service. The present post

MAN SERIOUSLY HURT BY RUNAWAY HORSE (same time as bank robbery)

W. L. Mitchell, farmer of an Edmunds County farm, living just north of Mina, is
in a very serious condition today as the result of an accident last evening Mr.
Mitchell, a man of about 75 years of age, was leading a horse with a halter.
The horse became frightened and in running away dragged the man for some
distance before he became untangled from the halter rope.

Dr. B. C. Murdy of Aberdeen was called to attend the man's injuries and found
him to be suffering from a broken arm and leg. Shortly after the doctor arrived
the man lost consciousness and the physician now believes that he is suffering
from a fractured skull.

INJURED MAN DIES HERE LAST EVENING

W. L. Mitchell, well known farmer four miles north of Mina in Courtlandt
Township, died here yesterday evening at a local hospital as a result of an
accident which occurred Friday afternoon. Mr. Mitchell was leading a horse by
the halter when the animal bolted dragging him for some distance and seriously
hurting him. Mr. Mitchell was found to be suffering from a broken arm and a
broken leg and it is believed his skull was fractured. He was removed to an
Aberdeen hospital where the end came at 6:50 last evening. Due to his advanced
age, 75 years, his recovery seemed doubtful from the first.

William Lyle Mitchell was born January 25, 1847 in Ohio and was at the time of
his death 75 years, 4 months, and 17 days old. He was a well known farmer
residing about four miles north of Mina, and had lived there for the past six
years, coming to S. D. from Sibley, IA. Mr. Mitchell is survived by his wife
and son, Perry C. Mitchell of Fort Dodge, Ind. lnterment will be at Riverside.

CATHOLIC CHURCH

In the early pioneer days, the Catholic Community of Highland Township attended
Mass in Aberdeen. But on rare occasions Holy Mass would be held at the Carl
Schwab home whenever a Catholic priest from Aberdeen would travel to the
farming community. A hand-carved altar made by Carl held a special space in the
farm home and here neighbors and relatives would gather for Mass and baptisms.
On one occasion all of the John Grote children and his wife were baptized. In
1914 Sophia (Schwab) Herman died and in her will it was stated to take the
money she had left and build a Catholic Church.

Three acres of land was donated by Carl to build the church. Carl along with
his sons Carl, Joe and Henry soon had the church constructed. Even though this
new beautiful church stood in a convenient place where all could travel within
a short time, Holy Mass was still only held about once a year. In later years
the Feast of Corpus Christi was celebrated. Father Joseph Schell, pastor of St.
Mary's Parish, Aberdeen, would bring a group of young people including a choir
and perhaps a few nuns. After concluding the church celebration, the day
usually ended with a fabulous meal at one of the farm homes.

In 1938 a tornado destroyed the Catholic Church in Andover, S. D., so this
church was sold to Andover on Nov. 1 1938. The steeple was removed and on a
giant wooden dolly pulled by a caterpillar. The pews and altar were taken to a
church in Seneca, S. D.

The graves in the church cemetery were moved to St. Mary's Cemetery in
Aberdeen, S. D.

RICHLAND CEMETERY

Harry Lester and Bert Kushman dug many graves in the Richland Cemetery. They
put in the concrete corner markers to identify the different lots. They also
put up the iron entrance and sign. When they were marking off the lot edges for
the driveway there was a grave in the drive. They dug it up and buried it in
the proper lot space. There are three unmarked graves at the cemetery.

There was a steam engine explosion and three men were killed. They were never
identified because they were transient workers.

Carl Kumpf, John Kaven and Harry Lester were digging a grave and had to work at
it until close to midnight because the funeral was the next day. They used a
lantern for light. When they would see a car come down the road, they would
reach up out of the grave and wave the lantern. Not a single car stopped to see
what was going on. The grave they were digging was Harry Cunningham's dad.

The cemetery was the property of Richland Township and is now taken care of by
a Perpetual Care Association.

RICHLAND CHURCH HISTORY

The Richland Wesleyan Church was organized in December 1909 in what was then
known as the "Insley" schoolhouse in Richland Township. It began with the
nucleus of the Richland Class consisting of 31 members and they worshiped in
the schoolhouse until the summer of 1910 when the first Richland Church was
built. The first parsonage was built in 1916 and the first pastor was Rev. J.
C. McGovern.

A basement was put under the church and other improvements were made at the
cost of $725 in 1942.

Electricity was made available to the church and parsonage in May 1949 under
the REA program. Because of crowded conditions and inadequate facilities, the
church made plans for the construction of a new church building and work was
started in the fall of 1958. By the cooperative labors of members and friends,
under the direction of Carpenter Stotz, the building was completed and
dedicated on Easter Sunday, March 29, 1959.

The Fellowship Hall was completed in the spring of 1965. This building provides
a place for all church-related activities where Christian fellowship is enjoyed
by everyone.

A new parsonage was built and dedicated in 1968 with the oversight of carpenter
Allan Hinds.

Within ten years and at an approximate cost of $49,000 and many hours of
donated labor, a church building, Fellowship Hall, and parsonage were erected
to the glory of God.

The first electric organ was purchased in December, 1953. Auxiliaries of the
Church included an active Women's Missionary Society and Young Missionary
Workers Band. Faith Promise Partners was organized in 1974 and has given
partial support to many missionaries. The Wesleyan Men contribute to pioneer
churches. They host an annual pancake supper. The Richland Wesleyan Youth have
a Bible Bowl Team and a Puppet Team called "Rainbow Express". The Bible Bowl
team placed second at the International Wesleyan Youth Convention in Dec.,
1982. The Puppet Team also went to the International Convention. CYC ministers
to about forty children each week.

An extensive expansion and remodeling project was begun in July, 1982. It will
provide adequate Sunday School room, church office, library, and other needed
facilities. It is scheduled for completion and dedication in May of 1983. This
will triple the floor space needed for the growing church.

THE FIRST PRESBYTERIAN CHURCH

The First Presbyterian Church of Mina, S. D. was organized in the year 1887,
but due to extremely hard times and the fact that many families had left the
community, it ceased to exist after a few years.

The church was reorganized in 1907 under the leadership of the Rev. Mr. C. C.
Todd of Aberdeen with a charter membership of 18 persons. The first service was
held by the group in the Mina school house on September 1, 1907. This first
service was held following the signing of a petition by 48 people that a church
be established in Mina again.

The Charter members were: P. H. Barker, Mrs. Carrie M. Barker, Lena B. Barker
(Goodspeed), Roy Barker, Veva Barker, Mrs. Jennie M. Hansen, Henry Krusinger,
Mae H. Poole (Lewis), Alfred Poole, Mrs. Hannah J. Nesbitt, Helen L. Lewis
(Barker), Winnie M. Lewis (Sinclair), Alice Downey (Voelz), Lucinda Nesbitt
(Millard), Bessie Nesbitt (Wimpsett), Rollo I. Lewis, Harrison Gearey.

During the fall of 1910 on November 20 the First Presbyterian Church of Mina
was built and paid for on dedication day. In order to accomplish this, Rev.
Fisher collected $300.00 by riding horse for several weeks getting $5.00 or
$10.00 where ever he could. On dedication day he was so tired, the members
asked him to go home and rest for a while. He was ordained and installed on
this same day.

On April 15, 1918, the Trustees and Elders decided to build a one story house
for the minister.

A Ladies Aid was organized in 1916 which helped to support the church and keep
it alive during the many rough years.

In 1949, under the leadership of Hugh Alexander, a new basement was put under
the church building. At the same time the whole church was repaired. A kitchen,
dining room and meeting room were made possible by this addition.

In 1963 the church became a part of a Larger Parish organization. In this
parish were Wetonka, Mina, Mansfield, Pierpont and Langford. The minister in
this large parish was Rev. Robert Coles. From this work, Rev. Coles in 1965
wrote a dream paper which eventually led to the development of the Aberdeen
Area Ministry. Both of these developments were milestones in attempting new
forms of ministry by the Mina Presbyterian Church.

In 1967 the Mina Church had been served by the Aberdeen Area Ministry with
their moderator being Rev. Donald L. Stoebner of Ipswich.

Last service held June 1, 1969 due to people moving to different locations.

MINA GO-GETTERS 4-H Club
The Mina Go-Getters 4-H Club was organized in 1957 with 20 members and leaders
Bennie Doane and Mrs. George (Alvina) Pettigrew. The meetings were held in the
Mina School. Bennie Doane moved away in 1959 and Frank J. Braun took over as
boys' leader. The club was very active and well represented at county
Achievement Days and South Dakota State Fair. The club members carried many
projects with livestock being one of the main projects exhibited at the county
and state. When the enrollment declined in later years the meetings were held
in the homes. The club continued until 1975 with Alvina Pettigrew, Frank J.
Braun and Verla Wendt as leaders.

RED CROSS AT MINA

Friday was a great day in Mina. The weather was a little cloudy at first but
cleared off to be a nice warm day and ended up in a good soaking rain and
hailstorm, which took the crowd home early in the evening.

The program started at ten thirty and Messrs. Jewett, Parmley and Rev. Bates,
and the Liberty Loan singers from Ipswich furnished entertainment until noon.

A rack on which were mounted four wooden kaisers attracted the crowd and
everybody was willing to take out their spite on the Kaiser for three shots for
a dime. This rack netted the Red Cross $53.08.

A chicken pie dinner was served at one o'clock and was continued until the
crowd was fed. Supper was served to those who stayed. The supper and dinner
netted the Red Cross $213.68.

The sale started off at 3:00 with auctioneers Baird and Moulton.

Old Glory was the first thing sold and it brought $793.00 or more.

Baird did very well but was forced to leave in the middle of the sale in order
to get to Watertown to cry a sale there.

Col. M. J. Moulton finished the sale and did creditably good work. Total amount
of the sale was $2100.00.

After the sale supper was served and the Kangaroo court got busy and fined the
crowd $70.09 for being too patriotic.

The rain kept the crowd from coming to the dance at the brick yards in the
evening but never the less the Red Cross made $10.80.

The confectionery tent was kept busy all day and took in $178.29. Besides this
donations amounted to $37.00. All together it was a big day and Mina put itself
on the map.

The day was held in honor of seven boys who left for Camp Lewis the following
day. Those who left were: Frank, Russel and Rollo Lewis, Herbert Gearey, Einar
Jorgenson, Delbert Webb, and Martin Swanson.

Besides this the people remembered those who went not long ago.

MINA SCHOOL

Mina grade school started in 1904 with 19 students. In 1924 the ninth grade was
added. In 1931 it became a two-year high school. In 1932-33-34 it was a
three-year high school. 1934 dormitory deans were started in the former Hasvold
Building which was across the street from the elevator. Mr. Henderson was the
first dean of the school. In 1935 an informal dedication was held for the newly
improved school auditorium and community center. The program honored former
stockholders of the Mina Lodge Association who deeded their hall over to the
Mina school board. The citizens were proud of the advancement made by their
school system in the past and happy over the acquisition of the building which
gave them gymnasium and auditorium facilities as well as home economics and
manual training laboratories. J. W. Parmley of Ipswich and Joe Foran of
Aberdeen, district WPA director, assisted in arranging labor for improving the
building. The hall was considered a remarkable structure for a town the size of
Mina. It was built in 1920 and used extensively as a community center and was
available for public gatherings.

1935-36 the school became a four-year high school. L. A. Thesenga was the
superintendent that worked very hard to get an accredited high school.

The first graduating class was May, 1936. The following is a news item from the
Ipswich paper: 1936 MINA HIGH SCHOOL COMMENCEMENT TO BE OPENED SUNDAY Mina -
Mina high school's commencement week will open Sunday with a baccalaureate
service in the high school auditorium with Rev. George Clark as the speaker.
Supt. C. J. Dalthorp of Aberdeen will deliver the commencement address at
exercises Thursday. A picnic Friday will conclude the school year with the Mina
baseball team meeting Aberdeen in a principal attraction. Members of the
graduating class are Howard Schwab, Alvina Schwab, Allen Crady, Minnie Jenson,
Pat McCormick, Jack Kushman, Evelyn Caldwell, Vernon Angerhofer, Robert Price,
Irene Pettigrew and Bertha Pettigrew.

Mina Grade School was used as a demonstration school by Northern State Teachers.

In Feb. 15, 1939 Mina School had a weather service which predicted the weather
for the coming year.

The high school was closed in 1942. Many of the students were tuition students,
and went to the various surrounding towns.

Cortlandt Twp. Grade Schools were consolidated in 1962. 1969-70 was the last
year the grade school was open in Mina. The brick school house built in 1912 is
now a private home.

RURAL SCHOOLS

Those were the days when teachers taught eight grades in a one-room rural
school. For heat, there was a soft coal heater. Coal was stored in a bin
adjoining the school room. The furnishings were just essentials such as a desk
for each pupil, teacher's desk, and chair, set of maps, a globe, bookcase with
a minimum number of library books, required text books, water fountain, a flag,
set of encyclopedias. The teacher did the janitor work, including having the
school room warm when the students arrived at school.

There were no hot lunch programs. The youngsters carried their lunch in a lunch
pail, which many times was a syrup bucket. Sometimes during the coldest winter
the students had baked potatoes, potato soup or some other hot food such as
cocoa. This was prepared at recess and ready at noon by setting this food on
top of the stove.

Students would carry the drinking water from a farm which would be a short
distance from the school.

Money was scarce and hard to procure. Many times the school board was unable to
pay the teacher's salary in cash or by check so the school board gave them
registered warrants which the banks would take at a 10 or 12 percent discount.

NORTHWEST SCHOOL - CLEAR LAKE TWP.

The Northwest School in Clear Lake Township, Edmunds Go., and three other
schools in the township were built in the year of 1885 by Mr. A. E. Brandes.
Families from the Thomas and John Hansen and their sister Margaret
Hansen-Schwab attended this school. A new school was built in 1954 which
replaced the four schools.

school house he attended. It was being used as a farm building. He has restored

18'x 21' and the entry is 4 1/2 x 18'. He also moved it to his home on Brown
Co. 12 West. Many articles have been donated or loaned to furnish the school.

HIGH SCHOOL IN THE ELFMAN HOME

In the spring of 1924 there were about eleven eighth grade graduates in
Richland Township. Most of the students had aspirations of going to high
school. Times were very difficult which made it a financial sacrifice to send a
young person away to high school. The parents could also use their help on the
farm. Someone thought of the idea that the tuition cost of students going away
to school to the district would be equal to the salary of a qualified teacher.
The idea was presented to Superintendent P. D. Selby of Ipswich. Mr. Selby said
full cooperation would be extended and if the same textbooks were used, the
students could take their final exams in Ipswich.

Mr. Emil Elfman had two students who wanted to go to high school so he
furnished two large rooms of his home as school rooms. These really met the
qualifications of a regular school. The home was located one and a half miles
north of Richland Cemetery.

A few of the students went on to Ipswich High School.

TWO YEAR SEARCH FOR BELL ENDS AT MINA

About two years ago, Mr. and Mrs. Fred Bain of Webster, had the opportunity to
visit Sr. Mary Braun at her mission in El Estor, Izabol, Guatemala. Fred noted
the need for a bell to call the natives to worship and the search ended at the
Elroy Eske farm two miles north of Mina,. and about 10 miles from Sr. Mary's
original home.

The bell is the same bell which for generations summoned the Mina children to
classes. The bell was acquired by the Eske family when the school property was
sold several years ago and had been on display in the Eske yard. Realizing the
great need for the bell, the Eskes relinquished the bell to the Guetemala
mission.

The bell, which weighs about 450 pounds, was crated and shipped. A tower was
constructed to house the bell. Once assembled it was heard for many miles
around. Watches and clocks are a luxury to the poor Indians, so the bell
informed the natives as to time of services. Sr. Mary Braun has served the
mission for four years.

Sr. Miriam Simieon of Hoven, Fr. Tom Moran of Chicago, another priest and a
nurse are also stationed at the mission. The missionaries work with Indians
from about 35 villages. They are involved in primary, secondary and adult
education and maintain a clinic. It is some times necessary to cross a 22 mile
lake to reach some of the villages. Donkeys are also used at times to reach
some of the more remote areas.

MINA BASEBALL TEAMS

As far back as we can; remember Mina had a team in the early 1900s. Some of the
men who played on this team are Harrison Geary, William Craven, Lewis Brothers,
Leon Prescott, Bill Brown and Russell Craven bat boy.

In the late twenties a team was formed under the leadership of Russel Craven.
This team played in the Edmunds County league. Some of the teams that played in
this league were Loyalton, Hosmer, Roscoe, Ipswich, Mina "Stoney Run" and Mina.
We don't know much about the records of this team. Some members of the team are

manager started a young team who played in the Northern League on the high
school baseball diamond across the road from high school. Teams in this league

HEALTH REMEDIES AND BEAUTY HELPS

A remedy for a cold is to melt a little lard, preferable goose grease, in a
tablespoon over the lamp. Add a little turpentine. Rub on neck and chest. Tie
or pin a clean wool sock around neck.

To relieve a cold in the head, nothing is better than powdered borax snuffed up
the nose.

To treat sunstroke, apply cold wet cloths over head, bind a wet, salty cloth to
the back of the neck, and apply salt behind the ears. Put mustard plasters on
leg calves and on the soles of the feet.

It is not healthful to sleep with the face toward a near wall.

Feather beds are not only unwholesome and uncomfortable. they also harbor and
transmit diseases.

For perspiring feet, sprinkle the inside of your shoes with powdered alum.

For chilblains, boil potatoes with the skins on and drain. Soak your feet in
the potato water, a shot as you can stand it. You can save the water and use it
again for the same purpose. You must do this nightly for several weeks.

Albion, Edmunds County, S. D. Jan; 8, 1901
I came to Dakota in 1882, being then just old enough to take 160 acres of
government land, but without money or experience. When I arrived in Dakota
Territory every face I met was strange to me, and I did not have a friend or
relative in this new country. I was compelled to purchase horses and machinery
at an exorbitant price and to pay a high rate of interest. In a few months I
had contracted an enormous debt. I did all the breaking I could, bought more
land, and raised good crops, always plowing deep and trying to keep my land in
such condition that I could reasonably expect fair returns. At last, seeing
that exclusive wheat raising was very expensive, taking into consideration the
high priced labor I was compelled to employ, I concluded to change my mode of
farming to diversify, and farm more as they do in the east.

I sowed a less acreage of wheat, and more to oats, barley, rye, millet, and
speltz, always planting a good batch of potatoes and a large piece of corn.
Commenced to raise hogs and cattle and for five winters I have fed and finished
cattle so that I have topped the eastern market raising both feed and cattle on
my farm in Edmunds County, S. Dak.

Last year I raised about 6,000 bushels of corn, and expect to plant 160 acres
to corn this coming season. I find in feeding cattle in the fall and winter
that they fatten and do better here than in Iowa or Illinois. And as our
weather is generally less changeable, and not damp or wet, our yards being dry
and clean. I have 18 hd. of horses and 95 head of cattle. Several head being
registered shorthorns and 25 hd. of hogs, including several brood sows. I have
1280 acres of land, of which 550 acres are under cultivation, and the balance
is in hay and pasture land. I do not care to state what I consider the property
worth that I have accumulated since coming to this country, but I would not
care to take $6000 for the 160 acres on which buildings stand. While I have
never had a failure in crops since coming here I have had some light crops, but
on the average I have had good crops as compared with other countries.

On a trip east last summer through the states of Indiana, Michigan and Ohio, I
saw wheat that would not go over 2 bu. to the acre, but the farmers seemed
satisfied and thought they were reasonably prosperous, and would commence to
sympathize with me and call my attention to some article, stating that we would
not have any crops in the west; but when I arrived home I cut wheat that would
go 25 bu. per acre and in fact all my crops were a good average for any
country.

I am writing this on the 20th day of Jan. and it is 72' above zero. My cattle
are running on the prairie and in fact have been all winter, picking their own
living and are in good condition.

I did not try to raise tame hay the first years I was here, but I now have a
meadow of tame hay and it is a success. I have a great many neighbors who came
here very poor, but are now out of debt, and who have nice bunches of cattle in
addition to the large amount of land they farm. They are satisfied and
contented and instead of wanting to sell, they are purchasing land and
enlarging their farms.

Now, last but not least, I was married in 1885 and have 6 boys, strong and
hearty, which speaks well for the prolific nature of this country.

In conclusion, I will say to all who may chance to come this way, my latch
string is always out to all strangers as well as to friends.

Very truly yours,
E. W. Powers

CURLING IRONS

Today we have electric curling irons with thermostatic controls which are used
by all ages; however, high school girls are probably the biggest users of them.
Young girls 50 years ago also had curling irons; however they weren't electric.
The heat for the iron came from a kerosene lamp that was used to light a room.
The curling iron was placed upside down in a lamp chimney so that the metal
prongs were suspended above the flaming wick and left there until the prongs
were hot. Periodically the iron was returned to the lamp to re-heat the prongs.
If the iron was too hot, bald patches resulted instead of curls. You had to be
an expert to judge the iron when it was at the right temperature for safe
curling. Testing the iron was done by listening to the sizzle produced when a
moistened finger was touched to the heated metal.

The results were more "frizzy" then curls or swirls.

Curling irons were only used for church on Sunday or dances. Their hair would
never have stood the punishment of using the iron each day as we do today.

There was also a marcel iron which pleated the hair. This was done by pressing
the hair into channels in a hot metal plate that produced distinctive ridges.
These were also heated in a lamp chimney.

A few years later Sears Roebuck catalog offered water wave combs. These
wavy-toothed celluloid combs varied in lengths were curved slightly to hug the
head. To make a wave, two combs were inserted with the teeth toward each other,
one above the other. In the wet hair, one comb was then pushed slightly forward
and one back. They were then pressed together until the teeth locked, holding a
ridge of hair between them. After the head was covered with sets of combs, a
scarf was tied about it until hair was dry.

Finger waves were the next development. It was easier to do someone else's hair
than your own. The hair was saturated with a sticky waving solution, then
pulled and pushed to make narrow waves all around the head.

Curls once more succeeded the smooth waves when pin curies became the way to do
hair. A person would pick up a small lock of hair, wind it around the index
finger into a tight coil and pin it flat to the head, being careful to tuck the
ends under so there wouldn't be any frizz.

Later were metal curlers, plastic curlers, sponge rollers, brush rollers, and
other gadgets.

When the first permanent wave was introduced, the cost was $2.50 if done at a
beauty parlor. These were starting to show up in the little towns.

The hair was wound on curlers, then a machine from which dangled dozens of
metal clamps was wheeled beside the woman's chair and a clamp was attached to
each curler. The machine was then plugged into an electric outlet to heat the
curlers.

Now in 1983 we have made a full circle back to the curling iron.

THE CASE FAMILY

One of the early settlers of Cortlandt Township was William Case. He proved up
on his homestead claim in 1883 on the NW1/4 of Section five.

He was lucky as he could get all kinds of water by digging a shallow well on
that land. Homesteaders who acquired land later hauled water from his well to
their places with barrels on wagons and stoneboats with barrels on them.

William helped the surveyors lay out the section and quarter lines in the
township. He also was a member of the first school board with two other members
for Mina. William Case and his wife Martha raised five children: Paul, Jesse,
Lena, William & Helen. The all went to school located one quarter mile south of
the present George Pettigrew farm. They drove a horse and buggy back and forth
to school.

are living on the farm SW 1/4 123-66 Section four. They are the remaining
family of William Case.

Margaret and Eugene on the farm SE #5-123-66.

A TRIBUTE TO VEVA BARKER

I'd like to share some of the things that made Veva Barker a very special
person in the Mina commonly where she lived most of her ninety years until she
passed away the third week in January 1982.

Veva began teaching when she was very young. I'm sure it would have been
difficult for her to name or count all of the children who were students of
hers. We were fortunate to have her for our first four grades in school. She
was a teacher. School was fun as well as a learning experience. This meant
everything from the wonderful world of reading to her hand guiding ours as we
did our push pulls and ovals in penmanship or making May baskets and gathering
May flowers to put in them. We couldn't wait for the noon hour to be over so we
could go in and listen to the next chapter of some good book she had picked to
read to us. Our Christmas programs were productions. We were all stars. The
costumes were beautiful. The angel wings she made were the prettiest in Edmunds
County.

Discipline wasn't neglected, either. Many of us made the long trip to the
basement to pick out a shingle to be used for a spanking when the rules had
been broken too often.

Sundays found her in church with a group of children around her teaching them
about a God who was so important to her all her life.

Veva also spent many years operating the Mina post office out of her home. It
wasn't unusual to stop by to pick up your mail and be invited in for fresh
cookies and coffee.

Finally, there came a time when she retired from teaching and the post office,
but she never retired from community affairs or doing for others.

Her mind was a treasure. She could recall names and dates from the past that
seemed like history to us. Unfortunately, she couldn't speak the last two years
of her life but from the spark in her eyes it was very evident she knew what
was going on around her. As the old saying goes, "she left her mark on this
community." She was loved and she loved her friends and family. She will be
missed.
Submitted by:
Carolyn Goodspeed Salisbury
213 Glen Oak
Bakersfield, Calif. 93309

PEMBROOK TOWNSHIP HISTORY

Township 124 Range 66
Some of the earliest family names of settlers in Pembrook Township still
remain. Many are long gone, but each contributed a share in making this little
spot in South Dakota a fine place to live and raise families.

"It was a hundred years ago this summer (1883) that Charles Gustave Lundquist
dug a shelter for his cow and horse in the creek bank, built himself a sod hut
nearby and spent the long winter alone. That is where Norman and Diane Rauscher
and their children Steve and Mark now live (Section 17). Can you imagine all
winter by yourself? Makes you wonder how they managed," commented Lewis
Krueger, Aberdeen, in relating this story of his grandfather. Story has it that
Lundquist was the first white man in this area to have smoke out of his
chimney. Mrs. Charles G. Lundquist was a Blomster girl. Children of the
original Lundquist family were Annie (Krueger), Selma (Anderson) and Ellen
(Anderson) and two boys, John and Dr. Charles Gilbert, who had a medical
practice out of the Leola area.

Dr. Lundquist married Idlette Rueb who had been a World War I nurse. They
adopted one boy and one girl. These children did not reside in the Pembrook
area. Selma (Lundquist) and Simon Anderson, with their children, continued to
live on the Lundquist place for some time. Both girls were school teachers and
eventually these families moved to Pennsylvania. John continued his education
and became a lawyer, not staying in the Pembrook area. Annie Lundquist married
William G. Krueger. They lived on Section 11. Here they raised the family of
Selma, Lillian, Ila, Beverly and Lewis.

Lewis and wife Evelyn (Boyle) and children Eileen and Gerald stayed on the home
place and continued farming. At the present time, Lewis resides in Aberdeen and
Gerald, with his wife Mary Ann (Nelson) and children Cheryl, Merilee, and
Gerald Lee manage the family place. Eileen is now Mrs. James Zimmerman and
resides with her husband on a farm near Ellendale, ND. They have two sons,
Derek and Dean.

Soon there was a settler on every quarter. Names remembered are: Jim Nicol,
Fannie Burch, Salines, Gus Bray, Jim Ladd, John Nicol, Nelson, John Belseth,
Steve Ellis, Hills, Starks, Wm. Palmquist, Johnson. The first one picked land
near a creek, if possible, because water was needed for stock, etc. Many of
those who homesteaded gave up and moved away, but the early Swedes and others
who came a little later were a determined people.

In 1897 Noah Blomster with his wife, Matilda (Pierson), moved with their
children James, Emery and George (Arthur, Walter, Alice and Earl were born in
South Dakota) from Blomster Hollow, Pennsylvania, to a homestead on Section 28.
That land then passed to James and his wife, Judith (Johnson), and their
children John W., Joan, Janice and Joy. Now the third generation, John W. and
wife, Sally (Boulais), and children Bridgette, John Erik and Jay farm this
homestead with Judith Blomster still in residence on the farm. Earl continued
to live with the James Blomster family until his death.

Other children of Noah Blomster settled on nearby land. Arthur Blomster and
wife, Pearl (Boulais), and children Walter, Betty and Stanley lived on Section
27 for years, then moved to Section 22. Stanley continued on this land till his
death in 1982.

Walter and his wife, Ellen (Boulais), and children, Wesley and Dwayn,
eventually settled on Section 27. At the present time son, Dwayn, and wife,
Sheila (Wagner) and children Rena, Jessica and Sarah are on this same land.

Daughter, Alice Blomster, married "the boy next door," so to speak, Arthur
Palmquist. Arthur was the son of another homesteader, Charles Palmquist, whose
brother, William, settled on NE 1/4-32. The children of Alice and Art Palmquist
were Gladys and Evelyn. Mrs. Alice Palmquist now lives with daughter, Evelyn,
in Minneapolis, MN. Gladys is in California. The Art Palmquist farm in Section
21 is now owned by Douglas and Mary (Gorman) Pettigrew and children, Erin and
Brett. Douglas is the grandson of another early pioneer family, Frank Schwab,
from the neighboring Cortlandt Township.

Another early settler, John P. Brog (Brooks), settled on SW 1/4-32. This land
passed on to son Andrew L. Brooks and wife, Marian (Gustafson) and children
Helen, Cheri, Harley Dale, Donna, Linda, and Jerald. In time the Dale Brooks
family with daughter, Brandi, and the Jerald Brooks family with son, Mark, took
over the home place to farm, with Marian and Andy building a new residence to
live in on land close by. Awhile later Jerald Brooks moved on to his
grandfather Carl Gustafson's farm on Section 10.

Settling on Section 4 was the Herman F. Erdmann family. This land was passed on
to son, Arnold, and wife, Dorothy (Crisp) and family. Now Arnold's youngest
son, Paul, and his wife Phyliss (Schaffer) and their children, James, Michael,
Jason, Kevin and Sarah are farming it.

Herman Angerhofer and family lived on Section 17 for awhile. One of his
daughters, Letha, married Harris Kruse. In time they bought the Helmer Braunson
farm on NW 1/4-14. Their family consisted of daughter Darlene and son Leland.
Leland and wife Beth (Gerlach) and daughter, Traci, now live on the farm along
with Letha and Harris.

In 1919 Charles O. D. Boulais and wife Cecelia (Schwab) and son Charles J. C.
settled on Section 35. This land passed on to Charles J. C. and wife, Fern
(Erdmann), and Children Cecelia, Charles O., Sally and Nancy. In time this land
continued on to Charles O. D. and wife, Gloria (Zirbel), and children Kellee
and Jordan with Fern Boulais still in residence on the farm.

At one time brothers of C. O. D. Boulais lived on Section 26. They were George
Boulais and Edward (Herb) Boulais. Edward was the father of Pearl and Ellen

Other early settlers were Wm. G. and son Oscar C. Kienast (Section 24) whose
daughter, Mrs. Ray (Srelda) Vining, Aberdeen, can recall the discovery of a
giant Mastodon (prehistoric elephant) skull on their farm on September 2, 1949;

miles and left a trail of carcasses almost to Aberdeen; or the high pressure
artesian well (1528 ft. deep) that flooded their farm in 1947. (Section lines
were plowed by a Mr. Larson who got the job because he has a pair of oxen that
walked such a straight line.) After the death of Oscar Kienast, Srelda and her
husband, Ray Vining, and their children, Gary Linda, continued farming the land
for many years before moving to Aberdeen.

Pioneer became the name of the post office, because it was located in the
dining room of the Lundquist house, the original pioneer family. After Mina
became a town, the mail arrived there and someone would go after the mail at
Mina and deliver it to Pioneer post office where the settlers could pick up
their mail. A couple of men who usually carried the mail from Mina twice a week
to the Pioneer post office where James Callum and John Taylor. Around 1914 the
mail route came from Wetonka with mail delivery Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday.
More recently came daily delivery and as of July 1, 1982, the route comes from
Westport six days a week.

The cemetery was established on Section 33 by a Mr. Nelson who lost his wife
and buried her there. Many trees were planted around the cemetery but prairie
fires, pests and neglect destroyed most of them. In 1981, the Pembrook Township
Cemetery Association was organized by John Blomster to restore and maintain the
cemetery. Tombstone names read Braunson, Carlson, Rennie, Palmquist, Blomster,
Holm, Calkins, Krueger and Johnson.

Water always seemed to be a problem for the area. Surface water was extremely
salty, but artesian wells solved that problem. In digging the deeper wells,
several farms, Vining, Brooks and two Blomster farms hit extremely high
pressure wells. A couple were eventually plugged as unmanageable, but the
present well being used by John Blomster still flows at 100 gallons a minute.
Coming from a depth of 1533 feet, the water temperature is a constant 75
degrees and has been harnessed as a major heat source for several buildings.
Recently newer wells have been dug for the Boulais, Brooks, Braun, Wendt and
Krueger farms at comparable depth, gallons and pressure rates and some are
being used as a heat source for energy savings. Another energy saving effort
was allowing the Northern Border Pipeline to cross Sections 7, 17, 20, 21,
28, 27, 26, 35, and 36 of Pembrook Township to carry natural gas on its way from
Canada to Illinois. In 1981 the crews buried the 42-inch diameter pipe three
feet underground and established a compressor station on Section 28. Aberdeen
draws some natural gas from this pipeline as it passes on its southern edge.

Schools follow settlement and always seem to be the center of the community.
Early Pembrook Township students went to one of three small schools (East,
South or North), whichever was closest to their homes. These small schools were
often pulled on skids, across sections, near families with very young children
so that their walk was not so long. Being up on skids gave a open invitation to
wandering varmints; and more than once students had to endure very aromatic
classrooms. In 1948 a Central School was moved in from Adrian Township to
SE 1/4-16 and continued as an eight grade school until May 1965, when enrollment
dropped to two students. Pembrook Township was consolidated into the Ipswich
School District on July 1, 1968. The last teacher was Mrs. (C. J. C.) Fern
Boulais who had also taught at East School in 1935-36. Members of the last
Pembrook School Board were A .L. Brooks, Mrs. James (Judith) Blomster, and Mrs.
Robert (Beverly) Wendt. The East School was moved to NE 1/4-18 and remodeled by
Albert Willis. This house is presently the home of the Robert Wendt family.
Central School grounds are now the site of the Larry Erdmann farm (Section 16)
and the Central School building was moved to the Long Lake Hutterite Colony,
where it continues to be used as a school building.

Gone are the days of the eight-grade, one-room school houses, but the memories
remain. The vision of benches and desks, maps and books, shuffling feet on a
dusty wooden floor and giggles and laughter in the cloakroom warmly touch the
hearts of many rural residents. Wood-coal stoves, stone crock water coolers,
kids catching gophers during noon hour, and the usual games of pump pump
pull-away and winter fox and goose. Dinner pails were lined up by the coat
racks. sometimes a potato baked by the heating stove made a hot lunch possible.

Families participated in community affairs held in the school house. Besides
regular school programs, Community Club met once a month and offered
entertainment, refreshments and fellowship. At the close of each school year, a
community picnic was held at one of the local parks. Following their marriage,
John and Sally Blomster restarted the community picnic tradition in 1971 and,
except for the very first year, it has been held at their home ever since.

Named for the local post office, Pioneer Extension Club was organized in May
1934 by Mrs. Carl (Wavie) Krueger as an educational/fellowship group. Charter
members, from Belle and Pembrook Townships, were: Mrs. L. G. Calkins, Mrs.
Casper Belseth, Mrs. J. Biegler, Mrs. Will Mastel, Mrs. Pete McGinn, Mrs. Wavie
Krueger, Mrs. W. Dutenhoeffer, Mrs. Mark Blunt, Mrs. H. F. Erdmann, Mrs. W.
Fessenden, Mrs. James Blunt, Mrs. Will Krueger, Mrs. H. Braunson, Mrs. Ora
Erdmann, Mrs. Arnold Erdmann, Mrs. Alice Palmquist, Mrs. Pearl Blomster, Mrs.
Alma Angerhofer, Mrs. James Blomster, Mrs. Marian Brooks, and Mrs. Roxie
Estergaard. Through the years approximately 65 women have belonged to this
club. Present members are: Mrs. Randy (Sheryl) Bauer, Mrs. James (Judity)
Blomster, Mrs. John (Sally) Blomster, Mrs. Dwayn (Sheila) Blomster, Mrs.
Charles (Gloria) Boulais, Mrs. A. L. (Marian) Brooks, Mrs. Arnold (Dorothy)
Erdmann, Mrs. Paul (Phyliss) Erdmann, Mrs. Carl (Wavie) Krueger, Mrs. Peter
(Roxie) Estergaard, Mrs. Leland (Beth) Kruse, Mrs. George (Alvina) Pettigrew
and Mrs. Douglas (Mary) Pettigrew. Now in its 49th club year, Pioneer Extension
Club still has five charter members, however, Wavle Krueger is the only member
who has had continuous membership during the entire 49 years.

For the younger residents of Pembrook Township, the Jolly Juniors 4-H club was
started by Mrs. Art (Alice) Palmquist. As the size of the club increased Mrs.
C. J. C. (Fern) Boulais became an assistant leader in 1950. Setting up projects
for the boys led C. J. C. Boulais to his role as leader in 1951. As happened
with the school, the club ran out of members and folded. Now, in 1983,
community youth are involved in the Mina Good News Bears 4-H club under the
leadership of Mrs. Don (Dot) Meyer.

Changes in the community came with the mechanization of agriculture. Horses
disappeared as tractors steamed over the fields. Many remember the 1927
cross-country airplane race. Alice Palmquist recalls, "There were 127 planes
and they flew so low that they had to raise to fly over our pasture fence. I
didn't have time to do much work that day. It was so wonderful to see those
planes." Lewis Krueger remembers, "They scared the heck out of our cattle, but
it was really something."

Perhaps the greatest changes in the community have been the installation of
telephones and electricity. Up until 1954, the phone line ended at the C. J. C.
Boulais farm. Messages were often taken and delivered by Mr. Boulais to other
community families without phones. Working in crews with the phone company,
community men dug in and set poles, strung line and hauled cable so that in
June 1954 the jangle of the telephone could be heard in all the homes of
Pembrook Township. Similarly, the surge of power from F.E.M. Electric of Ipswich
was connected in October 1950. Community effort made it possible to switch to
the brighter 110-volt system. Change was well on its way!

Surprisingly, things cycle back to their origins and despite advanced schooling
and more attractive employment opportunities, some of the youth of Pembrook
Township have returned to their roots to take up the guardianship of the
homestead and attempt to offer to their children the advantages of a rural
community. Many present farms have been passed down through several generations
although they were not all original homesteaders.

If events or names have been omitted that someone feels should have been
included it was not intentional. Rather, it is because so little is known by
our young people of the rich past of this area and there are such few sources
of information left. Great thanks to all who volunteered information and may we
urge each family to gather their own precious memories in such a form so as to
be able to pass them on to the coming generations.

BOULAIS FAMILY FARM SW 1/4-35-124-66

Charles Oliver (C. O. D.) Boulais was born April 16, 1877 at Dorchester,
Wisconsin. His family came to South Dakota with a load of lumber, which they
sold at Rudolph, SD where the homesteaded. C. O. D. moved to Mercier Township,
Brown County; and, eventually he and his wife, Ceclia Schwab Boulais and their
eight-year old son, Charles J. C., settled in Pembrook Township in the fall of
1919 on the present Boulais farm. They bought the farm from the Lentz family
who had built the farmhouse; the Boulais' built the remaining farm buildings
throughout later years. C. O. D. was active in the Live-Stock Improvement
Association, the Angus Association, as a Town Board member, and in the Mina
Equity Elevator.

Cecelia died in 1929, and the men "batched" for ten years until C. J. C married
Fern Erdmann on June 25, 1939. Fern Erdmann had been a school teacher in the
Pembrook Schools. With the arrival of Cecelia in 1943, three generations of
Boulais' lived together on the family farm. Two more children, Charles O.
(Chip) in 1946 and Sally in 1947, were born before Grandpa C. O. D. died in
1949; and the fourth child, Nancy, was born in 1953.

Over the years many crops were raised on the Boulais farm--Galloway cattle,
Black Angus cattle, hogs, sheep, goats, chickens and turkeys. They also had
horses and raised a large garden. During the depression years money was scarce
but neighbors still got together as families and enjoyed many forms of
recreation--barn dances, square dances, house parties and baseball games. The
Stony Run and Mina teams played other teams in the area; C. J. C. was a pitcher
for the Stony Run. When pheasants were plentiful and limits were much higher,
many military people visited here, particularly when they needed rest and
recreation from the rigors of war. Mrs. C. J. C. Boulais remembers many
interesting people, usually from the East, who traveled to South Dakota by
railroad for the hunting. Another vivid memory of life in the 1940'S was that of
the threshing crews. The men worked long, hard days in the hot sun in the
fields; and the women worked long, hard days in the hot kitchen preparing large
meals for 12 to 16 men. That was a really busy time!

The entire Boulais family was active in 4-H--all four children were members of
the Jolly Juniors 4-H club and both parents served as leaders for several
years. C. J. C. was instrumental in getting the land for the present 4-H
building in Ipswich and also in construction of the building. He served as
Secretary-Treasurer on the Board of Directors at the Mina Equity Exchange for
the years 1949-1960 and was also a member of the Pembrook School Board. Chip is
a current member of the Pembrook Township Board.

After C. J. C.'s death in 1961, Mrs. Boulais returned to teaching. She taught
in the rural schools until Trail School (in Mercier Township) closed in 1969.
Today she is an elementary teacher in the Aberdeen Public School System.

Cecelia Boulais is a computer specialist and currently lives in Seattle,
Washington; Chip and his family (he married Gloria Zirbel on June 22, 1970)
live on the farm at Mina. Sally (Mrs. John) Blomster and her husband also live
in Pembrook Township with their three children, Bridgette age 9, John Erik 6
and Jay 2. Nancy Boulais Mitchell lives at rural Aberdeen, SD. She continues to
work with horses, a love she shared with her father.

Three generations again live on the Boulais family farm--Mrs. C. J. C. Boulais,
Mr. And Mrs. Charles O. Boulais, and their two children, Kellee age 3 and
Jordan 1.

BROOKS FAMILY HISTORY S 1/2-32-124-66

John Brooks got his start in Pembrook Township about the turn of the century.
His father, Andrew Brooks or Borg, from near Mansfield pastured his cattle in
Pembrook Township during the summer. His sons, John, Tom, Chester, Bill and Ray
used to herd them a distance of about 30 miles. About 1900-1901 John bought the
quarter that the Brooks farm was built on. He obtained the land by preemptions
from the man who had homesteaded it. It was a desirable quarter because the
creek ran through it and it also had a good surface well. He bought the SE
1/4-32, and the SE 1/4-32, and the NE 1/4-5 in Cortlandt Township about the
same time. There was a cattle shelter close to the well too.

John married Myrtle Moody in Dec. 1903 at Bisbee, ND where she was teaching
school. John was farming in Pembrook Township at that time and Myrtle came here
after her school year ended in the spring of 1904. They first lived in a
granary The first son Winifred was born in 1905 but died 3 days later. Andy was
born in Dec. 1906 in Bisbee, ND. His mother went there because her aunt who
lived there was a mid-wife. She brought Andy to the farm in Feb. They came by
train to Mina. The Prescotts lived in Mina - Mr. Prescott was depot agent. They
were very good to Myrtle and the baby and gave them a warm place to stay until
John could get to Mina to get them. It was a very severe winter and they were
afraid the baby would freeze to death in that granary they had made into a
home. A year or so later a new house was built and in 1911 a large barn was
built. In 1911 a grove of trees was planted on the north and west sides of the
farm yard. The large modern house was built in 1916. Andy's mother had
homesteaded on a quarter of land near Stanley, ND. She sold that land for $20
per acre and bought a quarter in Belle Township for $20 per acre.

Andy can remember there were no fences from the farm until close to Leola -
just wide open spaces. He used to ride to Aberdeen in the buggy with his mother
when she took eggs and butter to McDirmids Slaters store and got groceries.
They took cans of cream to Mina and shipped it to Bristol.

John bought his first car in Oct. 1914 from Spauldings in Aberdeen. He drove it
home and put it in the shed. Next morning everyone wanted a ride but they
couldn't get it started until they pulled it out of the shed with a team of
horses and let it stand in the sun and warm up.

John plowed part of his land with a walking plow but Andy remembers him using
four and five horses on a gang plow. It didn't have a seat so he had to walk
behind the plow all day. His first tractor was a Bull tractor. It had three
wheels - one in the front that ran in the furrow and the drive wheel also ran
in the furrow. It had an adjustment or leveling device that kept it running
level. It was used mainly for threshing - it was very hard to start and they
didn't get much use out of it. They junked it and bought a Grey tractor that
they used for the years. Their next tractor was a Canadian Grey but it too was
a lemon. John had lots of good Percheron horses and they did most of the
farming with horses until the forties. One time Andy and the hired man were
mowing hay when a bad hail storm struck. The hired man's team ran up into the
corner of the fence and Andy's team ran up over the sickle of that mower. One
horse was so badly cut it had to be shot.

One fall John threshed a large stack of straw in his pasture -intending to have
the cattle in it for the winter. A fire John had set to burn some weeds got
away from him and burned up the straw stack.

Andy's mother taught school for four months one winter when the school house
was across the road from where the tower now stands. Andy was a small child and
was home with his father. She said most of the pupils were larger than she was.
When Andy was old enough to attend school the building was moved southwest of
the Noah Blomster farm so it was more centrally located. Ellen Lundquist was
his first teacher. That school is now a tool shed on the Brook's farm.

Andy's folks would take a shotgun along when they drove to town in a buggy or
wagon and on the way home would shoot a grouse of prairie chicken or two and
dress them out. When they got home they would have fresh meat for supper. Much
of their fresh meat was grouse, prairie chicken, ducks or geese which were so
plentiful in the early days.

KIENAST FAMILY HISTORY N 1/2-24-124-66

Oscar C. & Mame Kienast, came to Pembrook Township about 1910. Oscar's parents,
Wm. G and Mary Ann Kienast came at the same time. They had purchased land in
eastern Edmunds County in what is now Pembrook Township. The land was bordered
on the east by Brown County. The Kienasts came from Rock County, Minnesota by
emigrant car. The car was unloaded at Aberdeen and they then went by team and
wagon to the site that was to be their new home which was about twenty miles
northwest of Aberdeen.

There were very few roads. The site they had picked to build on had native
grasses growing as high as two or more feet. There were no lines or roads to
help locate where you were. Lumber was hauled by team and wagon from Aberdeen
and Wetonka to build the first farm buildings. The first building was a granary
with lean-tos on both sides. One side was for cattle and the other side for
horses. They lived in the granary the first winter while the house was being
built. As there were no roads or lines, Wm. Kienast figured where the building
would be placed. However, he read the compass wrong and the building was not
exactly straight north and south. Twin girls, Srelda and Elda, were born to the
Oscar Kienast's in that house the next winter.

There was a drought and in the fall of 1913 they moved to northern Minnesota,
but still kept the farm in Pembrook. They came back to the farm in the spring
of 1915 and the crops were put right in the stubble fields. 1915 was a good
year with plenty of moisture and good growing conditions and they harvested a
big crop.

The Kienast twins went with their grandfather in a buggy when he took a
petition around to be signed that was to organize the township. The Oscar
Kienasts lived on that farm until about 1945 when they bought a home in
Aberdeen. Oscar communed back and forth to the farm every day and was actively
involved until his death in 1960.

The Kienast children attended school through the eighth grade in the south
school of the township. Since then this school has been sold and another built
that was more centrally located. The Vining children later attended this school
through the eight grades. The first school was closed for a time during the flu
epidemic of World War I. Several of the teachers boarded at the Kienast home.
At one time there were only 3 children attending the school. After graduating
from the eighth grade, Srelda and Elda attended high school and college in
Aberdeen.

After several years of working in Aberdeen, Srelda Kienast Vining and her
husband Ray Vining returned to the farm in 1942. They lived on the farm and
operated it for Mrs. Oscar Kienast until her death in 1966. In 1967 the Vining
family moved to Aberdeen where they still reside. The Vining's two children are
Gary and Linda. Gary lives on Richmond Lake and Linda in Aberdeen. They have 4
grandsons.

MAMMUTHUS IMPERATOR

The Imperatial Mammoth ranged the U.S. during the Pleistocene Period more than
10,000 years ago. It was a close relative to the woolly mammoth and other great
elephants of the ice age. In life this elephant stood about 13 feet high at the
shoulders. Its tusks were 12 feet 6 inches long and 7 inches in diameter at the
base.

The skull has a rather large depression on the right side of the forehead. This
is evidently the result of an injury acquired when the animal was young. It may
well represent a wound received in a fight with another elephant.

This specimen was collected from the Pleistocene Drift on the O. C. Kienast
farm six miles north of Mina, S. D. in 1939.

THE BARTELS HISTORY S 1/2-13-122-66

In 1905, Charles Bartels a young man of twenty-four years old, from Alta Vista,
Iowa came to South Dakota, to farm a half section of land his father had bought
unseen, southwest of Aberdeen, SD at a town call Mina.

A small granary was built and he lived in it for three years, boarded at the
folks of John and Gust Rodhr's. He broke the sod with a walking plow. Then a
small house was built, consisting of a kitchen, front room and two bedrooms.
Charles went back to Iowa to get Jenesena Jacobson, who lived at West Union,
and had been teaching school while waiting for him. They were married June
23rd, 1909, and came back to the farm. It must of been hard for Jenesena who
had been raised where there were lots of trees and orchards and to come to the
wide open prairies, no trees in sight, and horse and buggy transportation.

In 1910 a fourteen stubble bottom plow was bought by Charles and it was pulled
by a under mounted Avery steam engine owned by John and Paul Kushman. A lot of
sod busting was done then, several hundred acres of flax was put in, plus their
other crops. Header barges were used, later on double binders were fixed behind
a tractor, which meant lots of shocking, Paul Kushman bought a threshing
machine, this was threshing crews to cook for, Jenesena was busy cooking on a
cook stove, carrying water and washing by hand on a scrub board for the
threshers and her children. In the rainy season the threshing crew would go
hunting ducks.

At the same time the house was built, a large barn was built, in 1911 this barn
burned, it was replaced by a two lean barn, which stood by the windmill. On
July 2, 1922 a tornado destroyed it, then the large hog house was built and is
still standing. In 1916 a round barn was built, which is still here and is a
conversational piece, for anyone who has never seen it.

In 1916 the Bartels moved to Mina, so Ernest could take his first year of
school, the country school was miles away. Then they moved back to the farm and
all attended the Dewey school in Richland township.

In 1918 an addition was added to the house already here, making it a big two
story house, which was the trend at that time, of building spacious, two story
houses. Heating was not the best in those days, water would be froze by morning
and the fire out in the stove.

Then came the dust storms in the 1930's, there were no crops and farmers had to
take Government loans to keep their farms, many farms were lost during this
terrible time. Kerosene and gas lamps or lanterns had to be burned during the
day so they could see in the houses. It was hard to get from the house to the
barn and back again. Then in the middle of the 1930's there was a bout with the
grasshoppers, which ate everything that grew. There was no feed for the cattle
or horses, this was another terrible time for the farmers. To keep the farm,
Ernest and his brother Ellsworth went shearing sheep, to buy gas and fix up the
machinery, some times they made their own machinery. Ellsworth also went
picking corn with Albert Shaner every fall at Minnesota and Iowa. They picked
corn for the same people and made some lasting friends.

Ernest remembers having seven feet of snow in the yard, had to scoop pathways
to get to the barns and around the yard. Horses were used to get groceries and
the mail from Mina. Families were blocked in for a month at a time, walking or
horses were the only means of transportation. The snow would be so deep, Ernest
used a horse to make a pathway out to the hay stack, for the cows every morning.

The Bartels family attended the Presbyterian church in Mina, and later joined
the St. John Lutheran church in Highland township, where the four children were
confirmed. As time went on Charles, son Ernest and grandson Colin transferred
to the Zion Lutheran church in Aberdeen, where Ernest and Colin are still
members.

There were four children born to this union: Ernest, Ellsworth, Lillian and
Alice. Lillian went to California in 1942, and worked for Douglas Aircraft. She
got married in 1944 to Martin Tunnicliff and had three sons, Charles and Jan
who live in California and Jay in Germany, making a career out of the army.
Lillian has two granddaughters, two grandsons and one step granddaughter. Her
husband died in 1974.

In October of 1939, Ellsworth died as a result of a tractor-plow accident.

In 1943, Alice went back to California with Lillian. Alice worked for
California Shipyards and Proctor-Gambles. Alice married Roy Ballou, they have
four daughters Piper, Midge and Cherie who are married, and Carolyn who lives
at Page, Arizona. Alice also had a son from a previous marriage, Colin who has
made his home with his Uncle Ernest. Alice had nine grandchildren.

Lillian, Alice and Roy left California's smog and crowded population and are
now living at Kingman, Arizona.

Ernest married Alvina Knoll Brick on July 7, 1967, he has two Stepchildren Dana
and Alina, Dana has one son Deland, and Alina two children Justin and Jenesena,
they all reside in Aberdeen. Ernest and Alvina still are living on the home
place, where Ernest has resided all his life.

The Bartels, will always be remembered for their community work, Charles for
his teaching and playing music, Jenesena the leader of the girls 4-H, for her
years of service, and both for their activities in the church, school and
community club service. They gave their time and talents to make Mina a better
place to live. Charles passed away in 1962 and Jenesena in 1957.

LABISKY FAMILY HISTORY NW 1/4-27-122-66

In the year 1881, Gottlieb Labisky, a Polish immigrant, and his wife Mary (nee
Cramer) and their infant daughter, Rose journeyed from southwestern Minnesota
to establish a homestead in Richland Township, Edmunds County, Dakota
Territory. The land on which they settled was located about 1 1/2 miles west
and three miles south of what later became the town of Mina. The building site
was located on the west side of Stony Run Creek, and during the first year or
two their dwelling was a sod shanty. Two sons were born to Gottlieb and Mary
during the time they lived on this homestead; first, Edward, and then Frank
(1885).

After spending a number of years on the original homestead, the Labisky family
relocated to a quarter section of land two miles to the south, adjacent to the
Richland Cemetery. Gottlieb purchased this land from his brother, August, who
had homesteaded there but had grown weary of the trying times and had returned
to Minnesota.

Rose Labisky taught school for a brief period of time in Richland Township. She
later married Fred Tolson, and for several years they lived on the family farm.
They had two sons and one daughter --Earl, George and Frances.

Shortly after World War One, Edward Labisky was killed when a private plane in
which he was a passenger crashed in Richland Township.

In 1920, Frank Labisky married Alma Troge, who was born and raised in the
Warner area, the daughter of early-day homesteaders. They lived in Aberdeen for
a number of years, where Frank was associated with McCarthy Brothers, a
Minneapolis-based grain firm. In 1930, Frank and Alma moved to the family farm
in Richland Township where they resided until 1947, when Frank retired from
farming. the farm, which had grown considerably in size, was sold at that time.
It is currently owned by Lyle Palmer. Frank died in 1966, and Alma in 1979.

Frank and Alma Labisky had two sons -- Wallace and Ronald. Wallace currently
lives in Aberdeen, where he is self-employed as a writer, photographer and
consultant in the firearms field. He also holds editorial-staff positions on
two national magazines. Ronald, who has a doctorate in professional wildlife
management, is currently teaching at the graduate level at the University of
Florida (Gainesville), as well as supervising all research projects in the
School of Forest Resources and Conservation.

ELTON PALMER FARM

History of the Edwin W. Powers' place now owned by Elton and Ellen Palmer. The
farm is located on the SW'/4 of section 22, 122, 66, six miles south of highway
12 on Edmunds Co. Road 15 and 1/2 mile east. It consists of 1280 acres. Edwin
W. Powers homesteaded here in 1883. We understand that the part of our house
that is now the kitchen with a room above was built by Mr. Powers and that the
family lived in it while the square part of the house was built. The thick
basement walls are made of rock from the surrounding fields.
Mr. Powers raised a lot of cattle and while the C. M. & St. Paul Railroad was
being built west from Aberdeen, he slaughtered and sold meat to the camps and
settlers along the right of way.

Mr. Powers was county sheriff and U.S. marshall at different times.

Mr. Powers built a very large barn on this place in 1898. The barn was 120 ft.
long and 75 ft. wide. It held 400 tons of loose prairie hay and housed 200
purebred shorthorn cattle. Along the south side were 16 box stalls for calving.
There was running water throughout the Barn from the artesian well. The
artesian well that is pictured in old SD history and geography books was on
this place.

Mr. Powers sold the farm and moved to Oregon in 1906. The big barn was torn
down sometime in the 1940's. The lumber was used for the granary and barn that
are still here. In 1890 the Duluth, Pierre and Black Hills Railroad was graded
from Aberdeen to Pierre. No rails were ever put on the grade. This grade
crosses the southeast part of our farm.

There have been many different families that have lived here since the Powers
left. Elton, Paul and Dale Palmer bought the farm in 1951. Elton bought from
the others in 1963. Elton Palmer and Ellen Steward were married in June, 1953.
We have lived here since. We have five daughters: Beverly (Mrs. David French in
Savage, Montana; Barbara (Mrs. Dave Stratton, Aberdeen, SD); Beth, Becky and
Charlene Palmer at home.

We raise beef cattle and grain and have a feedlot where we fatten our own
cattle. We have always belonged to the Richland Wesleyan Church and held
various offices.

The girls all started grade school at the Richland School which was just across
the field from our place. They belonged to 4-H clubs and all graduated from
Ipswich High School.

THUR. FEB. 5, 1885 - Mina Murmurs

While Mina cannot be said to be booming, it certainly is as prosperous as could
be expected. The trade of the town is increasing rapidly, and promises to be
greater during the coming summer. Large quantities of coal have been sold and
the amount of wheat marketed at this point is as large as at other towns of the
size of Mina, and greater than some. Altogether, Mina is one of the best little
towns in this section, and now that she is within the confines of Edmunds
County her prospects are brighter than ever before.

Several incidents connected with the dance have furnished considerable
amusement to the people of Mina. Ernest Goodspeed decided it would be a gallant
act to take a lady and went southeast of town to secure a partner. Arriving at
the house, he was met at the door by the young lady's mother, who refused him
admission and threatened him with a broomstick. Taking log bail he stopped at
the barn to reconnoiter, where he was met by the father with a pitchfork in
hand. Goodspeed skipped for Mina, and came over the hill and down Main Street
at a speed that astonished the natives. He failed to explain his haste, and
nothing was thought of the matter at the time.

A few hours after, Will Healey visited the same household for the purpose of
securing the young lad's company for the party, and met with a warmer reception
than Goodspeed did. The mother met him at the door, and when he stated his
mission she grabbed a kettle of boiling water and made a rush for him. He
escaped unhurt, and reached town in perfect health, but out of breath and minus
a hat. The whereabouts he has failed to satisfactorily explain.

W. J. Hope is despondent and sad, and his friends are greatly troubled over his
condition. A few mornings ago he was discovered butting his head against a
telegraph pole, but he was rescued before damaging his cranium.

JOHN GROTE FAMILY

John Grote was born April 9, 1849 in Germany. He came to the United States when
he was 9 years old on a sail ship. He came along with a tag around his neck
stating where he was going. His destination was St. Louis, MO where he had an
older brother living. When still a young man he moved to Mason City, Iowa.

Alice Jensen was born Nov. 22, 1864 in Norway and came to America with her
parents, grand parents, and 2 other children (leaving an aunt and 2 uncles)
April 11, 1864 Alice (real name Else changed it in America) was 5 months old.
During the trip on a sail boat they were at one time surrounded by icebergs,
there was no passageway and they waited and hoped the iceberg wouldn't crush
them. One morning an iceberg had moved and they were able to get by them.
Another time the wind was in the wrong direction so they had to throw out an
anchor and wait for a favorable wind. After 13 weeks they landed in Quebeck,
Canada. The civil war was on so the eastern America ports were closed. Then
they started out for St. Ansgar, Iowa where an uncle lived (Ole Iverson) on a
farm. Sometimes they rode on cattle and freight cars they seldom got to ride a
passenger train. They walked and walked for weeks across the prairie. When they
got to McGregor, Iowa there were no trains going to St. Ansgar, luckily there
was a man going that way with a wagon pulled by oxen. He hauled the trunks and
chests but they had to walk. They slept under the wagons on the ground, when it
rained. It was terribly hot and the mosquitoes almost ate them up. Water was
scarce and they suffered much from thirst. They ran out of food once and were
happy when they came to a farm where they bought a loaf of bread. They finally
arrived at St. Ansgar, Iowa where an uncle lived. They all lived in a one room
log cabin and they had 3 children when they arrived it made 16 in all until
some left to go to work.

SOLLIE FAMILY HISTORY

Peter D. Sollie, a pioneer, and his family lived eight miles SE of Mina. He and
his wife Matilda raised eight children. These were: Lewis Sollie. Lewis met
Etta Cunningham at the Albion post office in the John Cunningham home where he
went after the mail. Then he and Etta were married in 1907. They had two
children Glenn and Wayne. Lewis took a homestead claim in Perkins County and
farmed there for seven years. He spent the rest of his life farming near Mina.

Arthur Sollie married Amelia Anderson in 1915. They farmed in the Mina
vicinity. They had two children Alma and Ellis.

Seward Sollie never married. He took a homestead in Perkins County in 1907. He
farmed there seven years. he spent the rest of his years on the family farm
farming.

Ernest Sollie married Lizzie Erickson from Dawson in 1911. They had two
children. Erna Mae and Dorothy (deceased). Ernest was active in community
affairs being leader of the Hi Hope Community Club for years. Ernest, Henry
Schwab and Bertha Hansen formed a dance band and played at Mina dances for
several years. Sometimes Delbert Brick and others would play in this band too.
Lizzie passed away in 1934. He married Ethel Newman in 1941 and they farmed
south of Mina for many years.

He loved to relate this little story. One day when he was a young boy, he was
sitting on a rock in the pasture herding cattle and sheep. He was busy chewing
gum. He had this gum for about three weeks (gum was a luxury in those days)
when into his mouth flew a big fat grasshopper and ruined Ernest's gum and the
rest of his day.

Elmer Sollie married Mae Elliot on June 15, 1918. They had three children.
Virgil, Leota and Fern. Elmer lived and farmed on the Sollie homestead all of
his life. He was very active in community affairs. He served on school boards
and township boards all of his adult life.

Mabel Sollie taught Sunday School, gave music lessons and was active in Mina &
community affairs - singing for weddings, funerals, etc. She married Dan
Mecklenburg in 1916. They had three children Aurel (deceased), Lois and Frances.
They lived in Minnesota a few years but they lived in Billings, Mont., most of
their lives.

Clifford Sollie was the youngest of the family. He grew to manhood on the
Sollie farm. He never married.

Alma Sollie taught school in the Mina community for 12 years. She gave music
lessons, riding on a bicycle as far as Scatterwood Lake to give piano lessons.
She married George Mecklenburg in 1909. He became a minister. They lectured and
traveled all over the world.

Peter Sollie and his wife Matilda were born in Norway. Peter came to Wisconsin
then to Iowa. Then he and his wife took up a homestead claim near the permanent
Sollie home then moving to the Sollie homestead in 1897.

Peter Sollie was the man who laid out the Mina mail route. He took a surveyer
and they set out in a horse and buggy and planned the mail route. The Mina
route was previously called the Cortlandt route, later changed to Mina.

Alfred Pool was the first mail carrier in the Mina route. Harry Lewis also
carried mail for several years. Later Harrison Gearey was the mail man for
several years.

For awhile John Cunningham had a post office in their home and people would go
there to pick up their mail. This post office was called Albion.

Mrs. Mae (Elmer) Sollie related the following incident. She and Mable Sollie
had driven in a horse (named Old Maid) and a buggy to Mina to help children
practice for Children's Day. This was a big event held once a year in the
summer time in the Mina Church. Then Mae and Mabel stayed that night to
practice for the young people program. Mabel was to sing a solo and Mae to
accompany her on the organ. So it was quite late at night when they started for
home. This being the Sollie farm seven miles southeast of Mina.

It was pitch dark and it was difficult to find their way home so a young man
named Rollo Lewis (he worked on the Dewey farm a short distance from the Sollie
farm) led the way with his horse and buggy and the girls followed in their
buggy. But Rollo lost his way. He was suppose to go southeast of Mina, instead
he went straight south across the prairie until they finally reached the road
they should have taken in the first place. This was west of John Kushman's
place. Then Rollo left the girls and went to his home. So Mae and Mabel still
had about three miles east to go. It was very dark so they decided to give Old
Maid the rein and let her take them home. Anyway Old Maid stumbled, broke her
bridle, so the girls jumped down from the buggy, took off their stockings and
working in the dark patched up the bridle the best they could. This was very
late at night and coyotes were howling and to make matters worse, they had to
cross the Snake Creek Bridge and as they looked south, they could see lights
flickering along the creek. They were really frightened, so Mabel drove. She
drove Old Maid slowly and noiselessly across the bridge, then she took the
buggy whip and Old Maid, Mabel and Mae quickly arrived home. They put Old Maid
in the barn, lit the lantern and took the stockings out of the bridle. They
didn't want the Sollie men to know about this incident. They sneaked in the
house and went to bed. By this time it was 2:30 a.m. This is one practice the
girls never forgot.

Mae Sollie used to relate the following story and Mabel filled in the details.
Mabel Sollie had been busy all morning helping prepare dinner for her sister
Alma's wedding that afternoon. She had been baking pie shells and filling them
with lemon and chocolate pudding, topping them with meringues so she was
already tired before the wedding.

Then there was the wedding, a hundred guests were invited and somehow they were
all crowded into the Sollie home. There was the wedding ceremony. Seward Sollie
played the wedding march on his violin accompanied by Dora Bartels playing the
piano. Then after the ceremony all the guests enjoyed a delicious dinner.
Twenty people were served at a time. After this everyone retired to the
upstairs in the barn to enjoy harmonica music and to play games. By this time
Mabel and a friend of hers were very tired but by this time the guests decided
they'd like some lemonade. So Mabel and her friend were elected to make the
lemonade.

Down the steps of the barn they went, walked to the house, squeezed oodles of
lemons, put in the sugar - then for the water the well ran very slowly, so
Mabel and her friend thought instead of waiting for the water palls to be
filled from the slow running faucet, they had a bright idea. They hurried to
the horses watering trough and pulled out pails of water and had the lemonade
made in no time flat, everyone enjoyed this delicious drink and no one was the
wiser.

THE KARL SCHWAB FAMILY HISTORY

Karl Schwab was born to Johann and Theresia (Schertz) Schwab on Dec. 27, 1851
in Austria. On July 25, 1874, Karl married Barbara Portisch, they came to
America July 14, 1878 and settled in Nora Springs, Iowa. They lived here for
five years during which time four of their children were born: Anna on April
21, 1877, Mary (Grote) on Dec. 29, 1878, Carl on Dec. 18, 1880 and John on March
21, 1883. The family moved to Edmunds County on March 29, 1883, here they lived
for a short while in a sod home, moving on soon to Brown County where they
built a sod house, homesteading near Snake Creek until years later a wood frame
house was built. Four more children were added to the Schwab family after
coming to SD: Barbara (Schneider) on March 18, 1885, Joseph on Oct. 18, 1886,
Bertha (Hansen) on May 28, 1890 and Henry on Jan. 17, 1894.

Anna died in 1890 at the age of 13 and John died at the age of 2 in 1885.

The six remaining Schwab children remained within the immediate area of their
parents homestead, each married and built farms of their own. Mary Grote died
July 14, 1940 and Barbara Schneider died Feb. 26, 1941. The rest of the brothers
and sister retired and moved to Aberdeen. Carl died Dec. 2, 1962 and Joseph
died Oct. 13, 1958. Bertha Hansen still lives in an apartment in Aberdeen and
Henry lives in Minneapolis, MN.

Mary married William Grote and they had two children, Anton and Margaret.

Carl married Aurelia Snyder and they had six children, Rozella (Grote) Joseph
Leonard, Leon, Florian, Norman and Darlene (Gefre).

Barbara married John Schneider and they had two daughters, Bertha (Hansen) and
Mary (Kramer).

Joseph married Margaret Hansen.

Bertha married John Hansen, they had four children, Joseph, Alvina (McClain),
Florian, and Arnold.

Henry married Elsie Rydberg and they had four children, Viola, Orlin, Leanna
(Koleski) and Lorraine (Olson).

Karl died Jan. 4, 1920 at the age of 69 and his wife Barbara died April 25,
1935 at the age of 83.

        Pipeline

WORK ON the Mina Lake Water and Sanitary Sewer District this fall has resulted
In the placing of an 1800-foot plastic, pipeline across the lake. George
Gebhart, project director with Scott Engineering of Watertown, said the
butt-fused pipe (no connecting joints) is used in bay areas but this is the
first time it has been used in South Dakota where the pipe was placed under
water. Laying of the pipeline on the bottom of the lake was completed Tuesday
with the aid of a pontoon boat, a pickup truck and other equipment to help,
slide the pipe into the lake. When the project Is completed water will be
pumped from the lake to a nearby treatment plant (yet to be constructed) before
being piped to individual members homes in the water district. The pipe is
located on the north side of the east bridge on old Highway 12. (American News
Photo)

Holds wonderful memories for me,
Her presence returns as a vision,
For it was my Grandma's, you see.

And there her gray head bowed in prayer,
And now when I look in her corner

And the tired old cushion you see,
It represents memories of childhood
And resurrects Grandma for me.
