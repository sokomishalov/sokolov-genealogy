There exist two great reference books that help us sort out and understand
some of the early census records of our Catholic peoples of the Beresan
District.  Those two great works are (1) The Emigration from Germany to Russia
in the years 1763 to 1862 by Karl Stumpp and (2) The German Colonies In South
Russia, 1804 - 1904 by Rev. Conrad Keller.  These indices have been compiled
from these two sources.

If you do not posses or have ready access to these great works, this index will
do you very little good.  If you need to know more about these two books, go
the end of this front matter and check it over.  You need to keep in mind that
if you need to posses your own personal copy of either of these works, you can
purchase copies from the AHSGR (American Historical Society of Germans from
Russia) in Lincoln Nebraska or the GRHS (Germans from Russia Heritage Society)
in Bismarck North Dakota.

The intent of this index is not to replace either of these books but to aid in
their use.  Researchers who attempt to only use the index list will be in a
position to make many mistakes that may not always be obvious.

Let us go through a few drills and show how this work may be used, and give a
couple of examples.

In the Stumpp book, most of the census data regarding the Odessa area was the
1816 census.  Many of the censuses used in the Keller work that will be included
in this index are about 20 years or so later.  Each work will provide a number
that will work as the "census roll" number for the purposes of this index.

We can note that the two works will tend to call the same states in Germany by
different names.

The mother's maiden name is normally provided in the Keller data, while the
maiden name of the wife is seldom discernable in the Stumpp book.  For this
purpose, you will note that we have provided brackets around the Last name of
the wife to reflect her married name when extracted from the Stumpp book.

In both books, it is often (not always) discernable as to the given name of the
father.  But be on the look out for mistakes on the index.

Examples:
   Let us first look at the name of Thomas Martin in Karlsruhe.  We can see in
the earlier Stumpp census in 1816 a Thomas Martin at age 30 - then in Keller
book in 1839/40 census, a Thomas Martin at age 58.  A little basic math will
tell us if he were 30 in 1816 and 23 or 24 years later - he should be 53 or 54.

   We do notice the difference of the names of the places between the
reference, but checking the book  --  we can note that this is the same place.
. . . but why the apparent difference in age?

   Let us take the index data for Thomas to the two books and take a look at
the make up of the families in both books.  In the first census in the Stumpp
book, we can see that Thomas's wife was only 20 years old, and they had one
daughter Barbara at about 6 months of age.  Then when we go to the Keller book,
we find that the wife is now see the wife is 44 years old 23 or 24 years later,
which adds up math wise . . . but where is the daughter Barbara in this later
census in the Keller book?

   All we have to do is go back to the index and we find that in the Keller
book, she is now in another man's house with a husband and now her own son with
her age being 23 - still adds up math wise . . . but why is Thomas's age at
least four years difference?

   As a user of the Stumpp book, if you don't already know it, you will learn
that many ages are two years or so different than what should have been
reflected - some where in the Stumpp book itself, you will find that it warns
that this will happen . . . the worse case could lead you to 6 -7 years
difference - so you must become used to keeping yourself aware that the ages
provided could be in error.

   So - you finally ask, is this the same Thomas Martin - and the answer to
that has to be most probably, but it must be concluded with some reservations -
and should not be attempted without looking from every aspect using these two
books . . .

   Next, let us look at Joseph Braun on the index.  We don't see a father's
name but we see a note in the remarks column that says "son of Barbara Busch". 
What does this mean.  I will leave that one up to you - go to the book and look
it up and try to figure it out for yourself!

Doing a drill with more examples without the use of the books is a drill in
futility.  So we will not attempt any more examples here.

Please use this index as it was intended and remember the rules of using census
data, and that is that it is always suspect information but something a
researcher must always put at the front of their things needing to be done list
. . . after you have gathered what ever census data you can gather, then you
can perhaps be lucky enough to find other support documents that will help
support the conclusions the census data tends to make you want to form up.

Good luck in your research.
