                                Dedication
Seventy-five years have passed since the founding of Ipswich. On June 9 & 10 of
this year, the residents of this community pause and look back on the fruitful
and also the poor years that are now part of history. It would be impossible
within the limits of this book to give in detail all the many aspects of this
history, but as accurately as possible we endeavor to cover the highlights of
the many changes and events that went into the forming of "our town."

We humbly dedicate this book to the pioneers of Ipswich and the surrounding
community through whose faith, courage, and determination the settling of this
virgin country was accomplished.

In The Beginning

A small group of pioneers pressed west from Aberdeen in the fall of 1882,
crossed a dry creek and came to the top of a rise west of the creek and
behold-a vast expanse of rolling prairie unfolded before them. Edmunds county
had its first settlers. But what was this land like before?

Since the beginning, the land underwent many changes. From part of a vast ocean
to a swampy, tropical, steaming swamp land, then completely covered with a
great glacier, to what we know today as Edmunds county. Along with these
geological changes, the vegetable and animal life changed too. The vegetable
life progressed from rushes and ferns through palms and pines to the prairie
grasses and wild flowers of the pioneer days. Animals developed from the
primitive forms to the mammoths of the stone age. In 1949 a partial skeleton
and tusks were unearthed on the Kienest farm north of Mina proof of their
existence in this territory. Then came the "Monarch of the Plains", the
American bison or buffalo, as it was commonly called. Wolves, antelope,
buffalo, and rabbits were frequently seen by the early settlers.

        Teepee ring on banks of Snake Creek

Politically this territory was part of the Province of Louisiana which was
ceded to the United States by France in 1803. The purchase price being fifteen
million dollars. Originally belonging to France, Louisiana was ceded to Spain
by the treaty of Paris in 1763 and retroceded to France for the purpose of
colonization on Oct. 1, 1800. After its purchase and division, this portion of
the Louisiana Purchase was known as Dakota Territory. This territory was
divided into two states, North and South Dakota, which were admitted to the
union in 1889, six years after the organization of Edmunds County.

Before the white man came, Indians inhabited this territory. The Ree, or
Aricara Indians were possibly the first human inhabitants of South Dakota.
Their homes were always near the Missouri River or other large stream, but no
doubt traveled here in search of game. When the white man first had knowledge
of the Dakota country, the Omaha Indians were among its residents. As the
pioneers settled the country to the east, the Kiowas and Sioux were pushed
westward into this territory, but had already moved on by 1882.

The White Man Comes

        Ezra Drew 1912, Age 85 In front of what is now Henry Beck's residence

Edmunds County was created in 1873 and organized in 1883. The County was named
for Newton Edmunds, a New Yorker, who became the second Governor of Dakota
Territory. Born in Niagara County, New York, he came to Dakota Territory in
1861, as chief clerk in the surveyor's office. He was appointed by President
Lincoln in 1863 and served until 1866.

The settlement of Edmunds County dated from October, 1 882. At that time range
66 was not a part of the county, it having been omitted by the territorial
legislature in defining the boundary between Brown and Edmunds counties, so,
that at that time Edmunds County consisted of ranges 67 to 73 in Township 121
to 124 inclusive.

The first actual settlement made in the county was by Ezra Drew and Ira Dibble
and their families on October 16, 1882. These settlers were accompanied by
George Phillips, John and George Jameson and others from Oelwein, Iowa, who
located a townsite on the southwest quarter of section 34, town 123 range 68,
named Georgetown which was platted during January 1883.

During the winter of 1882 and 1883, numerous claims were located in eastern
Edmunds county, some of which were resided upon during part of the winter. This
was especially true of William V. Bold, A. B. Allen and others in the vicinity
of the townsite of Freeport; A. B. Allen erecting the first two story building
in the county, known as the Allen House in February, 1883.

The settlement of the western part of the county was not made until on or about
the first day of April, 1883, when S. B. Basford located a small shanty on the
southwest quarter of section 24, town 123, range 71. Returning to Aberdeen, he
met Charles P. Morgan on his way out with a large tent to start a hotel in the
same locality. J. W. Parmley soon after arrived and settled nearby, and the
settlement was given the name of Roscoe, after Roscoe Conklin. Travel between
Aberdeen and the new town of LeBeau on the Missouri opened at once with daily
stage from Aberdeen to the river 110 miles. During the following six weeks,
nearly all the land as far west as Range 69 was taken by settlers and many went
farther west picking choice locations.

Edmunds County was organized July 26, 1883, by a meeting of the Board of County
Commissioners appointed by Governor Ordway consisting of Louis F. Diefendorf,
Loren A. Barbour and Charles N. Skillman at the Skillman House on the townsite
of Edmunds. The Board after organizing adjourned until the next day when the
county seat was located at Edmunds by a vote of two for Edmunds and one for
Freeport. At this meeting C. L. Pratt was appointed Register of Deeds, and the
Board adjourned to meet August 13th. At this meeting W. R. Ruggles, who had
been appointed Clerk of Courts by the district judge, qualified. The board
appointed John Hughes of Powell City, Sheriff; A. B. Allen of Freeport,
Treasurer; John Sweet of Georgetown, Assessor; J. A. Rice of Roscoe County
Judge; and adjourned to August 27th, when J. W. Parmley was appointed
Superintendent of Schools; and C. L. Seabright, Coroner; also justices and
constables were appointed and the organization was completed.

The Chicago, Milwaukee and St. Paul railway extended its line from Aberdeen
west 26 miles to the center of a triangle formed by the towns of Freeport,
Edmunds and Georgetown. On October 2, 1883, a town was platted on Section 27
and 28 town 123 range 68, and was given the name of Ipswich. Lots were sold at
auction to the amount of over $5,000 the first day and at once the moving of
the three disappointed boom towns to Ipswich was commenced.

Within three weeks a large town had sprung up. On November 1, 1883, the Board
of County Commissioners met in the one remaining building at Edmunds and by
resolution changed the county seat from Edmunds to Ipswich at which it was
later permanently located by a vote of the people of the county.

In 1886, the road was extended from Ipswich west to section 21, town 123, range
73, where the town of Bowdle was established.

The territorial legislature in 1885 changed the boundaries of Edmunds County by
annexing range 66, thus making the county 48 miles long by 24 wide.

The first homestead entry was made October 2, 1882 by Milton Bliss and the
first pre-emption filing by John Ackley, September 20, 1882.

Georgetown - Freeport - Edmunds

After the first settlement was made, Ezra Drew, Ira Dibble, George Phillips,
John and George Jameson and others from Oelwin, Iowa, located a townsite on the
southeast quarter of section 34, town 123, range 68, named it Georgetown, and
platted it during January 1883.

The townsite of Freeport was located on the southeast quarter of section 15,
town 123, range 68. This town was promoted by Joseph Brown, father of Willard
Brown, and J. H. McNammara. These men and some of the other settlers came from
Freeport, Illinois and named their new town after their old home. Freeport was
on a direct line west of Aberdeen and expected to get the railroad when
extended.

On April 23, 1883, George Meadows wrote to his father and mother, "We are just
25 miles from Aberdeen, twenty-four miles due west and one mile south. There is
a town two and one half miles southwest of us, Georgetown, that some
speculators have been booming but now their is talk of scriping a town
(Freeport) two miles northwest of us, and it is hard to tell which may come out
ahead. Excitement runs high, and a squatter joining on the west of me sold out
last week for $150.00 cash and one joining on the north for $180.00. A land
shark told us boys if we wanted $600.00 for our three claims, he would find us
a customer in a week, but we are going to hold until a railroad comes for we
have a choice location."

A month later Mr. Meadows wrote to his brother, Will, "Georgetown is way in the
shade now, Freeport (our town) taking the lead. It has three hotels, three
general stores, blacksmith shop, newspaper, saloon, bakery, stables, and is
four weeks old. This is the country. I can count one hundred shanties from
mine".

The Dakota Tribune was published in Freeport by A. B. Chubbuck and D. B.
Worthington. In its August 16, 1883, edition this advertisement was featured.

"The new town of Freeport, Edmunds County, South Dakota, BOOMS and all eyes are
turned toward the metropolis of the west and the future grand railroad center
of South Dakota. It will be a station on the Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul
Railroad within the next sixty days."

Edmunds was the first county seat although very little business was done there
before its removal to Ipswich. Charles Skillman was the promoter of Edmunds.
The main building of Edmunds was the hotel.

Edmunds was located two miles west and one mile south of the present location
of Ipswich. Georgetown was located one mile south and one mile east of Ipswich.
Freeport was located one mile north and one mile east of Ipswich.

The Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul Railroad extended its line from Aberdeen west
twenty-six miles to the center of the triangle formed by the towns of Freeport,
Edmunds, and Georgetown. On October 2, 1883, a town was platted on sections 27
and 28, town 123, range 68, and given the name of Ipswich. Most of the
buildings were moved in to Ipswich, including the Allen House.

Mr. Chubbuck wrote this editorial in the Dakota Tribune.

"The Edmunds folks have accepted the inevitable and intend to harmonize. We
understand they are all making preparation to move to Ipswich. In that case
where will the commissioners hold their meetings? Under the blue canopy of
heaven?"

"The fame and downfall of such towns as Freeport, Georgetown, and Edmunds can
be recorded as one of the drawbacks of the prosperity and advancement of our
territory and western investments in general. Parties from the east who were
lead by townsite companies to blindly invest, assuring them that their money
would be doubled in a remarkably short time are the ones who cannot be blamed
for carrying away evil reports when the town proves a sham and their money
disappears. The three towns will now merge into one, business will be combined,
and everyone will agree then that the town, being placed where it is by the
railroad company, is the one thing that will bring harmony and strength to this
county rather than turmoil, envy and bitterness. As it now is, the one town of
this section will be a commercial center for this whole valley, will be the
county seat, and with this outlook, it is no wonder that hundreds are pointed
Ipswichward".
        Dorothy Clemen

"HARD, BUT HONEST"
FREEPORT, GEORGETOWN AND EDMUNDS
FREEPORT, GEORGETOWN AND EDMUNDS WILL
JOIN HANDS AND LIGHTLY SKIP TO THE NEW
WHICH WILL BE LOCATED BETWEEN THE THREE,
FULFILLING THE MANY PREDICTIONS OF:

"I TOLD YOU SO"
Careful readers of THE TRIBUNE have probably noticed that for the past six
weeks but very little has been said in these columns regarding railroad
matters, and of Freeport's chances of being a station of the Milwaukee & St.
Paul railroad. Previous to that time nearly everybody in and out of Freeport
surmised that the officials would make Freeport their town, and that it could
be "the" town of this county. On the strength of that, much building has been
done, and today ten business buildings are on Main street. Later developments
showed that her chances were getting remarkably slim, and not wishing to
mislead the uninformed, refrained from making any comments whatever, and even
neglected to mention the location of the preliminary surveys made through this
section, for they both missed Freeport, and to have said anything would have
been a drawback to our prosperous village. The First survey was made about a
month ago, and ran a mile and a half south of town, the second preliminary
running a half mile north. Our townsite representatives a short time since
interviewed Manager Merrill in Chicago and received considerable encouragement,
that gentleman going to much trouble, listening to their plans. Wednesday
evening C. H. Prior, assistant superintendent, and A. D. LaDue, right-of-way
agent, visited Freeport, holding consultation with J. H. McNamara the next
morning. Mr. Prior said 20 acres of land must be given for the railroad town,
whereupon Mr. McNamara informed him that amount would be given on conditions
that the location of Main street would not be changed. Mr. Prior also received
two other offers, one from parties southwest of town a mile and a half, the
other from parties two miles south east. Mr. Prior and Mr. LaDue drove over to
the proffered sites and from what can be learned they decided that Judge Day's
claim, southwest of town, is the best. At all events, Freeport is too far from
the survey, and probably in a few weeks Allen's well will be the sole monument
to Freeport's glorious past as soon as the new town is platted. From the
present outlook it will not be long. No location has yet been fixed upon but it
will soon be determined as C. H. Prior informed us that trains would probably
be running as far west as this in two months. The line has been located
twenty-eight miles west from Aberdeen, and grading has already begun. The force
working between Ashton and Mitchell will complete their work by Sept. 1, and
will then grade on the lines west.
        The Dakota Tribune
        Vol. 1 No. 16
        Aug. 16, 1883

FREEPORT
ITS PAST BRIGHT PROSPECTS, AND FUTURE DILEMMA

Freeport was script about May 1 . It was handled by men of wealth, honor and of
the right stuff to make a prosperous city. Most all of them having some
interests in railroads, it had a live and steady boom, and today, although the
infant child, has more business houses than all the other towns in the county
combined. Lots sold very rapidly, business houses were of the most substantial
character, and when they are moved to the new town will be a live funeral
procession and give it anything but a corpse-like appearance. The railroad was
very favorable to our town up to the time of the different surveys, when it was
found that it would be an impracticable route to come to Freeport. So confident
was Mr. McNamara of the railroad that he erected the largest building in the
county. But it did not reach us, and like the lost sheep that had strayed away
from its master, we will go to the fold. We are all going, and when we get
there will renew our vigor and prosperity, and become the father of this grand
territory. A town placed in the center of this magnificent county, inhabited as
it is by a live intelligent people, will make it one of the most prosperous and
growing cities in the territory. Edmunds county people are made of the "Eli"
material, and when the new town is platted will all get there. Being in the
center of the county, with a railroad to back it, and no other small towns to
catch its trade, it will naturally draw the county seat and be the distributing
city for miles around. Eastern capitalists are awaiting this grand opportunity
for speculation, and our Dakota investors will not be in the rear. With these
natural advantages over us, why should we wonder that Freeport, Georgetown and
Edmunds join hands to make one good town in this large and magnificent county.
        The Dakota Tribune
        Vol. I No. 17
        Aug. 23, 1883

IPSWICH-WARD

About the 25th of April last the town of Freeport sprung into existence and a
few days later, May 3, THE DAKOTA TRIBUNE was first published, Since that time
we have endeavored in all legitimate ways to assist in the up-building of what
we hoped would soon be an important and permanent city, and its growth was so
rapid that it indicated the necessity of such a business center somewhere in
the county. The fates, however, materially assisted by the action of the
railroad company, have decreed that Ipswich, a mile and a half southwest, shall
be the future metropolis, and Freeport, Georgetown and Edmunds will soon go,
and so unanimous are they in their belief that probably not a vestige of the
once prosperous villages will remain. THE DAKOTA TRIBUNE will hereafter be
published at Ipswich. Tis hard to go (for it will require several ox teams) and
the spot has grown dear to all the Freeport business men-their lot cost them
$100 apiece. To the business men of Freeport we shall say au revoir, because we
are quite confident we shall meet again at Ipswich. To the farmers and kind
friends located near what was once Freeport, we say, thanks for all your kind
words, when hauling your wheat to the Ipswich elevator, call on
        Yours Tenderly,
        CHUBBUCK & WORTHINGTON

                Ipswich Born - Railroad Comes

        Ipswich 1883. West side of Main St. North from lst Avenue.

From Chas. G. Brown's letter, printed in the Tribune in 1929. "I came to
Ipswich in the summer of 1883 from Norwich, N. Y. I found a young man in a tar
paper shack located on the N. W. 1/4 of Sect 28, 123-68 who was homesick. I
paid him $200 to move out and let me occupy his shack. I was located. The
quarter was filed on by my wife's mother. A little later a Mr. Prior, the
railway right of way man came through to locate a station on the road. He
wanted 320 acres, half a section. The quarter sections occupied by W. V. Bold,
Chas. Turner, Mr. Morris and Mr. Davis were satisfactory. He wanted half of
each quarter section. They offered him 40 acres making 160 acres. I remember
distinctly Mr. Prior's reply. It was, 'I wouldn't give a damn for 160 acres. I
must have 320 or I will locate the town two miles east of here, they will give
it.'

"The conditions were that the holdings were to be proved and the land deeded to
the railroad after proof. One other condition I recall was that the main street
was to be on the section line.

IPSWICH
IS THE NAME GIVEN TO THE FUTURE METROPOLIS OF EDMUNDS COUNTY
SURVEYORS LAYING OUT A PORTION OF THE SITE INTO TOWN LOTS

Excitement Prevails
Monday morning last, F. L. Austin, C. B. Dare and R. A. Scott, surveyors of the
Milwaukee road, commenced operation in the new town, and on that very day
nearly all the business men of Freeport drove to Aberdeen for the purpose of
securing one or more lots on which to place their buildings. The plat is in the
hands of S. H. Jumper, the agent of the C. M. & St. P. company, and ha reserved
lots. for business men in Edmunds county who have buildings already erected.
Those not reserved will be sold at auction to the highest bidder Thursday next,
September 27, at the First National Bank. No lots will be sold until that day,
and the terms of the reserved lots will depend on the prices of those sold at
auction. Speculators have been waiting for the opening up of the railroad town,
and nearly all the Main street lots will undoubtedly be grabbed, as Ipswich
will surely be a booming town. C. H. Prior, Ass't. Supt., says it will be a
terminus sufficiently long to ensure it a good growth, and as it will be the
only town in the midst of this beautiful valley, it must of necessity spread
out rapidly, Business men of Freeport, Georgetown and Edmunds are preparing to
move, and in less than two weeks quite a transformation will take place at
Ipswich.
        The Dakota Tribune
        Vol. 1 No. 21
        Sept. 20, 1883

THE LORD BE PRAISED
WAS THE SOUL-FELT EJACULATION OF IPSWICH PEOPLE WHEN
THEY SAW THE FIRST TRAIN COME SNORTING INTO THE COUNTY SEAT OF EDMUNDS COUNTY

The railroad has been built into Ipswich. All doubts and speculations have
ceased, and for four days have our citizens daily crowded the foot of Main
street to welcome with cheers the iron horse that brings to our thriving city
the numerous visitors who have heard so much about Ipswich and its remarkable
growth. The novelty will not wear off for some time. Although a train of cars
is a common sight to all citizens, who visit Aberdeen weekly, still the
assurance that the hauling of freight over the long road between this city and
Aberdeen is now unnecessary, is gratifying indeed. Everybody is tickled clear
to the backbone, for few believed the road would be built this fall, and it
amusing for an outsider to hear us all exclaim with a breath of relief: "We've
got a railroad." Already we notice the effects of railroad transportation.
Immense throngs of visitors arrive in town daily and the hotels are enjoying a
rushing business. Supplies of all kinds are obtained cheaper, and as an
illustration coal is now worth $7.50 per ton, and a short time ago our citizens
were paying $18.00 for it. We now possess all the advantages an agricultural
town could wish. We have an enterprising class of citizens, who are doing
business in a county seat and a railroad terminus.
        The Dakota Tribune
        Vol. 1, No. 29
        Nov. 15, 1883

SHE WILL SURELY BOOM
IPSWICH, WITH HER OTHER ADVANTAGES, CAN ALSO BOAST OF BEING THE
COUNTY SEAT OF EDMUNDS COUNTY

Today has been a memorable one for Ipswich. She has been made the county seat
of Edmunds county, and it was done in the following manner: The proprietors of
the Edmunds townsite cancelled the scrip on the site leaving it necessary for
the commissioners to relocate the county seat, and at their meeting yesterday
Ipswich was the town decided on. Teams were hired and before darkness had
fallen, the building used for county purposes at Edmunds had been moved to
court house square, Ipswich.
        The Dakota Tribune
        Vol. 1, No. 27
        Nov. 1, 1883

As in all pioneer communities homes were built hurriedly to satisfy the needs
of the people, later to be replaced with more permanent structures. Such was
the case in Ipswich in 1833. To make business buildings a reality, after the
railroad had come to what is now Ipswich and the residents of the other three
towns decided to move to the new location, it is only natural that many of the
business buildings were moved overland, according to old files of the Dakota
Tribune, many by ox team.

Perhaps the oldest building still in use as living quarters in present day
Ipswich is the home of Percy and Anna Jaquith on Main Street. This house was
first built at Freeport, a mile and a half northeast of Ipswich by Albert B.
Allen as a hotel known as the Allen House. After he brought this two story
structure to Ipswich he used it for a time and later Mr. Allen became county
treasurer.

Several houses can be said to be "old" in point of years since they were built.
One of the oldest is that on north Fourth Street, now occupied by Mr. and Mrs.
Frank Kub. Another is that now occupied by the Donald Engler family and another
is first to the east of the Engler home, both houses in the block immediately
north of the Holy Cross church and parking lot.

One of the first buildings to house Edmunds county officialdom in the early
years before a regular courthouse was built was the frame structure now being
used by the Soil Conservation Service. This is located immediately north of the
Ipswich State Bank. The courthouse was built in 1889 and when completed the
county offices were moved there. This old courthouse was used until it was torn
down in 1934 to make room for the present fireproof building of brick and stone.

The late J. W. Parley, in writing about early day Ipswich fires which accounted
for the loss of many Ipswich buildings wrote:

It is doubtful if any town in the state has suffered from more disastrous fires
than Ipswich. Having been built as a boom town, entirely of lumber and in a
vary reckless manner, when fires were once started it was almost impossible to
check the same. There were numerous fires taking out a building or two at a
time, but the first great fire was on Sept. 19, 1895 when two entire business
blocks and a number of residences were consumed. A little later another fire
took the two remaining business blocks. The business section at one time
extended north a block and a half farther than it now does on Main street. With
a push born of adversity and a spirit that laughs at destruction, the people
went to work and decided to put up only fireproof buildings."

When Ipswich was started by the railroad platting on the present site, the
Dakota Tribune of Sept. 13, 1883 reports:

"The first man in the new town (Ipswich not yet named) is John Kearns, who
moved his building over today with colors flying. Oxen belonging to Messrs.
McCormick, Quinn, Jones and Garrett yanked it over in less than half a day."

Also in September, 1883, a building was moved to Ipswich from Freeport by
Chubbuck & Worthington, publishers of the Dakota Tribune. This building, not
very large was for some years located on the Chubbuck property, now occupied by
the Pat Dosch family on north Main street.

In the Dakota Tribune of Sept. 27 we read: "The surveyors finished platting the
town Wednesday afternoon, Sept. 26, and before the last stake was driven the
town contained half a dozen buildings and three cellars were dug, one new
building raised and a paper printed in the town the next day to tell about it."

Prices paid for lots in the new town of Ipswich were phenomenal, according to
standards of the day. According to the Dakota Tribune of Oct. 18, 1883 inside
lots sold from $255 to $290 while corner lots sold for $380. Two weeks earlier
lots had sold for $200. The paper said that a week later prices had risen to
$325 for inside and $500 for outside lots.

As in all towns that suffer disastrous losses from fires, the big problem was
always "water". It was known at a very early date that Ipswich was in the heart
of the artesian belt and so wells were being driven to find the much needed
liquid. The Dakota Tribune of Dec. 13, 1883 reports: "Water was found at a
depth of 43 feet in Mr. Kelly's well at the Red Star Livery. He intends getting
a plentiful supply and the well is now 60 feet deep.

The Real Estate Journal of April, 1884 reported:
"The artesian well being dug by the city is down 540 feet. The contract is to
dig 1000 feet and the price to be paid is $4,000. This is located within 20
rods of the railroad well which is 960 feet deep and discharges 20,000 barrels
of water daily." This action is the result of a meeting held by Ipswich
business men as reported in the Dakota Tribune of Nov. 29, 1883, which said:

"Our business men, never lacking in enterprise, congregated at Cox's Store
Monday evening to take steps for the digging of a town well. A committee was
appointed consisting of Messrs. Skahen, Kearns, Herrick, Bates and Roach, to
ascertain the cost of a suitable well and a proper location. On Tuesday evening
the meeting was again called to order and the committee appointed reported the
crossing at Main street and First Avenue the most favorable location." On Dec.
6, 1883, a week later the Tribune reported progress as follows:

THE FIRST CORNER STONE

Quite a demonstration took place this morning, which, was as enthusiastic as
anything of the kind that ever occurred. The occasion was the laying of the
first corner stone in Ipswich. John Kearn's building has that glory and the
honor of laying the corner stone and making appropriate remarks was conferred
upon Dr. A. Grant, of Bath, who was in the city at the time. His speech was
very appropriate and elicited great applause, and had we the space would print
his remarks. THE TRIBUNE, the pioneer paper, came in for its share of glory, as
a copy of it was placed under the stone, to remain for time to come.
        D. T., Vol. 1, No. 21, Sept. 27, 1883.

Ipswich is no common town, nor has it a common name. It is heartrending to hear
the many pronunciations given it, such as: Ipswich, Ipswitch, Ipswick, etc.

The Nineties

        East side of Main street about 1898

The courthouse which was finished in 1890 (and of which the town was very
proud) was soon put to use as a stronghold when the Indian Scare came. This was
in 1890. Men went up and down the streets telling everyone to go to the
courthouse, the Indians were coming. I think practically all of the townspeople
took shelter there and many from the country came to town. Every man who owned
a gun brought it with him and guns were brought from the hardware store. The
Indians never came.

Later when we had such destructive fires people felt the courthouse could be a
place of safety if needed. I do not think it was ever needed.

These were the days when water was a luxury as well as a necessity. It was sold
by the barrel or by the pail delivered, where it was wanted, by a water wagon.
There were two very good wells that I remember, one three or four miles
northeast of town and one about four miles west. In 1890 Stacy Cochran and Will
Ruggles edited a newspaper called, "The Ipswich Gazette". This was located on
the east side of main street about in the middle of the block and south of the
present library. The skating rink, which stood where the Standard Oil Station
now stands, was a very important place. Home talent plays were given there and
much enjoyed by everyone. Grace Loomis Shaft sang on all those occasions and
Pearl Loomis Hurlbut accompanied her. The Mitchell boys danced and did black
face comedy acts etc. The Kickapoo Indian Medicine Shows drew a goodly crowd
there also.

Susan B. Anthony visited Ipswich in the early nineties. Where she lectured I do
not remember, but think it was in one of the churches.

The GAR building, which was located where the ASC office is now, was a social
gathering place. As many of the pioneers were Civil War veterans and Winfield
Stott Post was important to them.

In 1892 we had lots of rain. Gardens and crops were bountiful. At that time we
had a county fair. The grain, vegetable and dairy products were  exhibited in
the skating rink mentioned before. The horse racing and sports took place south
of the track and east of the old electric light building, which burned a year
or so ago. As I remember W. E. Herrick, George Spaulding and Dow Lewis had
racing horses.

In 1895 a disastrous fire started between the postoffice building and the one
next to it. This was about where the Tribune building is now. These were frame
buildings with stairways to the second floor going up the outside of the
building. This fire took everything on both sides of main street in that block.

Ipswich was plagued with fires from without as well as within the town. Many
prairie fires came so close to town that most able bodied men went to fight
them. The women gathered a few prized  possessions to take to safety on a
moments notice if need be.

Telephone service came to Ipswich in 1897 and the town was making progress.

        First Kindergarten in Nickle Plate Hotel (St. James).Grace Shaft,
        Teacher. 1898

The winter of 1896 and 97 was the year of the heavy snowfall. The snow was deep
everywhere and it just kept on snowing. Railroad service was badly hampered,
mail did not get through and they had to use huge rotary plows to clear the
tracks and open a way for the trains.

In the fall of 1898 another fire destroyed the south block of main street with
the exception of what is now the Crissman Drug Store, which was called the
Gamble Building at that time. After this fire an ordinance was passed banning
the erection of frame buildings on main street.

There were three hotels at this time. One managed  by Harry Stever stood where
Kusler's pool hall stands and was burned in the fire of 1898. Mr. Stever later
rebuilt where the theater is located.

Another was the American House built in 1891 by Richards and McCrimmon. James
Lawrence bought this in 1892 and it was under his management  until his death
in 1895. Sometime later Mr. Richards, a brother-in-law of Mrs. Lawrence, took
over the management. Levi Thomas was clerk. Mr. Richards death occurred in 1898
and Mrs. Lawrence and Levi Thomas continued to run the American House. Previous
to this Mr. Richards had bought the Central House and started to remodel. After
his death it was sold and completed and is now the St. James Hotel.

Of the ministers at this time I can only recall Rev. Kyle, Rev. Weber and Rev.
Wood of the Congregational Church and Rev. Keene of the Baptist Church.

My teachers were Mrs. Doolittle (Rosie Rogers), Olive Finch, Mamie Cox and
Nettie Lewis. All very good teachers and mostly pleasant memories of them.

There were many fine people in Ipswich in the nineties and I have many happy
memories of those days, it was a good place to live. Many fine people live here
now and it is still a good place to live.
Martha Hoyle

TOTAL LOSS $80,000
IPSWICH LOSES TWO OF HER BEST BLOCKS

As stated in The Democrat last week the fire of Thursday leveled to the ground
two of the best blocks in Ipswich, left two families homeless, destroyed a
large amount of property and crippled some of Ipswich's soundest business men.
True western grit, however, will still prevail. Every business burned out will
be immediately resumed. Most of burned-out merchants were ready for business
Monday morning. In less than twelve hours after the fire, P. E. Cox had a fresh
stock of groceries and was open for business. Other merchants were equally
energetic in getting their affairs into shape. The fire started about half past
four in the afternoon and by five o'clock the whole two blocks were reduced to
ashes, so rapid was the progress of the flames. The water mains were worse than
useless as a good deal of valuable time was wasted in futile attempts to use
the present small flow of the well. Hard and persistent fighting was all that
saved the two blocks south of the present burned district. Hope was given up
again and again of saving the Edmunds County Bank building and with it the
entire block. The fact that the Gamble block was built of brick was all the
world that saved the block on the west side of Main street. It seems almost
impossible that the seething fire in the Cox block across the street would have
burned for nearly two hours and not taken up its neighbor across the street in
flames with it.
        Edmunds County Democrat
        Vol. 6, No. 43
        September 26, 1895

ANOTHER FIRE DESTROYS THE TWO PRINCIPAL BUSINESS BLOCKS
CONSISTING OF NINETEEN BUILDINGS

Almost the entire business part of Ipswich was destroyed by fire Friday morning
between the hours of three o'clock and daylight. The fire originated from some
unknown cause in the rear of Robert's store and before it had burned itself out
nineteen places of business were completely destroyed, including the post
office, which was in the building next to that where the fire originated. When
Mr. Chubbuck reached the postoffice building, it was in a mass of flames and he
was able to save only two sacks of mail, the remainder of the contents  being
completely destroyed, including his newspaper plant. The only buildings left
standing on either side of Main street in the two principal blocks are the
Gamble brick block, containing the hardware and drugstore, the front of which
is badly damaged, the livery stable, blacksmith shop and restaurant.

The fire was fought with all the effort that could be put forth, but could not
be checked until it had burned itself out before reaching the restaurant
building about daylight.

Ipswich has been especially unfortunate in losses from fire. About two years
ago a good part of the business center of the place was burned and the citizens
have been unusually careful since that event.
        South Dakota Tribune

1900-1909

1900 In April a stock company came to town and they heard a new song "For Her
Sake" for the first time. The shows were East Lynn, My Partner, Felicia, and
Mixed Pickles.

On Sept 13, residents went to Aberdeen to hear Theodore Roosevelt, and on Sept.
28th, they went in to hear William Jennings Bryan.

1901 J. W. Parmley assisted in taking pictures for a railroad booklet that was
later used in getting landseekers to come to South Dakota.

The Maccabee Lodge and W. C. T. U. were thriving.

On Sept. 22 a memorial service was held for McKinley in the Opera House and a
large crowd attended.

1902 The first X-ray machine was placed in Dr. Robert Hill's office.

        Ipswich 1901 from old Court House

In November, a petition was circulated for a curfew ordinance.

1903 Parmley's Shetland ponies arrived in April. Today throughout northern
South Dakota, descendants of these ponies will be seen.

In Sept., C. H. Barron bought a Rambler car, the first auto in Ipswich.

1905 J. W. Parmley was elected to the state legislature and re-elected in 1907.
Many homes installed gas plants to be used for lighting and cooking.

1907 Cars could drive to Aberdeen (27 miles) in 1 1/2 hours and to Roscoe (15
miles) in 43 minutes.

In November a big prairie fire threatened the town. Everyone was called out to
help stop the blaze.

1908 In March, an attempt was made to vote the town dry, but "High License"
won. Two big events occurred: A Moving Picture Gallery was opened and the first
graphophone was purchased.

1909 In April, the town was voted dry. The church bells rang every hour for the
victory and many drove up and down the street tooting their horns.

The summer of 1909 was one of the boom periods and landseekers came flocking in
on the trains. J. W. Parmley escorted 34 men with eight teams and several autos
out to see the land which was for sale.

The land boom which started in 1900 and lasted until 1910 was not a rushing
boom, but just a good healthy interest in the sale of land. Many people came
from Iowa in 1902 and the prospects of a very good crop gave settlers a keen
interest in land sale.

Capt. S. V. Arnold sold nineteen quarters of land to a real estate firm in
northeastern Iowa, who in turn, sold to other parties, some of whom are still
living here. In 1902 Father Duren interested many people in the sale of lands
as he did extensive advertising in regard to the quality and price of land in
comparison to what it was in other states. The railroad company offered special
rates for land seekers and local real estate men would have available
transportation to take them out to inspect land that was for sale.

Mr. J. W. Parmley, Mr. Arnold and Mr. M. P. Beebe from the Bank of Ipswich were
instrumental in bringing in many new settlers. Ten dollars per acre was
considered a very good price for the land in the nineteen hundreds.

The Carl Gerhardt family, Chas. Bartells, Theodore and Emil Bartells and
families from Iowa, were among some of the settlers coming during those years
and settled in Union and Richland Township.  In 1904 the Morgan and Coglys came.

In 1909 the Robt. and F. W. Brunkow families, and the Phil Lohnes family came
from Elmwood, Nebraska and settled in Rosette Township.

The land boom quieted down in 1910 and 1911 when the dry years started.
        Elsie Bosanko

1910-1919 World War I

This is the time of changing from the horse and buggy days to the gasoline
days. Some professional men and a few farmers owned automobiles but during this
period many more were in use and many tractors replaced the horse in the
fields. In 1914, 126 families in Edmunds County owned automobiles and the Fords
were the more numerous. The first motor driven gas truck was put to use about
this time.

Many carloads of stock, cattle, hogs and horses were shipped from Ipswich by
railroad as well as many cars of grain by individuals as well as by the
elevators. The sowing of alfalfa was stressed and free seed was given to school
children to be sown on the farms and on the roadside.

Marriages took place, babies came into the World and people departed this life
but space does not permit the recording of each of these. Changes occurred in
the business places of Ipswich and people  moved in and out. Many of the
farmers left during the dry years but others came in later to take their place.

We think of the "good old days", referring to the past, but we find recorded
the expelling of school children for destroying property, of pick- pockets,
attempted murder and suicide much as in any space of time; history repeats
itself.

The Opera House was remodeled, the new school house built, the Deschner
building was completed and now houses the ASC office. The M. P. Beebe residence
was built and is our present Hospital. The Catholic Basement Church was built,
and is the present Holy Cross Hall. Other buildings went up and a few silos
were erected in the rural areas.

National Guards were organized in 1904, to operate with the Herreid Guards of
Eureka, and November 5, 1913 they became a separate unit. In 1917 they were
called to the Mexican Border for service, returned and were again called into
service and camped for a short period on the local school grounds. Upon being
ordered into camp in North Carolina they became Company D of 146th Machine Gun
Battalion. The Guards were commanded by Capt. L. E. Smith.

April 2, 1917, President Wilson, by order of Congress, declared war on Germany.
Many of our Edmunds County boys enlisted and were drafted into service. As
these boys left in groups they were given a good send off and later hailed as
they  returned home. Some made the supreme sacrifice and were honored for their
heroic work.

While the boys were away the folks at home were keeping things going by working
with Victory Loans, Liberty Bonds, War Stamps etc., and each time our quota was
reached and sometimes went over the top. Business men and high school boys
helped the farmers with shocking grain in the  evenings. Many of the women
donned overalls and did a man's work, and the teenage girl should not be
forgotten as she did the chores and farm work. The Red Cross was very active
under the leadership of Mrs. F. J. Tracy, and she was ably assisted by many
others. As auxiliaries were formed much sewing, knitting and surgical dressings
were made ready for the soldiers. Kits were made and presented to each soldier
as he left for service.

The influenza period of 1918 was trying; so many were sick at one time. It was
difficult to care for the sick as most families were afflicted. Dr. Hill, our
pioneer doctor traveled, night and day, getting his rest mostly while traveling
from one patient to the other. All nurses were pressed into work and many
volunteers. An emergency hospital was started in the Rooming House and was ably
managed by Miss Helen Meyers, now Mrs. Hembd and she was assisted by others.

All was not stress and strife however as the social and educational gatherings
were held-chautauquas, home talent plays, prohibition rallies, women suffrage,
skating parties, Welsh Singers, Camp Fire Girls, Boy Scout and many clubs and
religious orders as there are today. It was about this time the confederation
of Women's Clubs took over the beautifying of our town and it became known
through a wide area as the "Zinnia City". Carrie Nation, noted saloon smasher
was here and spoke in the opera house. Ipswich has always been strong for
baseball and this period was no exception; many games were held. The Chinese
pheasant and the ring-neck pheasant were brought here and today the ring-neck
pheasant is our most numerous hunting bird.

Ipswich was put on the map more firmly by being the home of the Yellowstone
Trail as it was started in 1912 with our citizen, J. W. Parmley known as the
"Father 'of the Yellowstone Trail".

Fires were hard to control, due to lots of prairie land and lack of water. The
warehouse and blacksmith shop of A. F. Lawein and office of I. M. Chase were
destroyed by fire. A bucket brigade was formed to save other buildings. Many
tons of twine, several buggies and stored machinery were lost. A prairie fire
at the Blunt farm, just South of Craven, destroyed much hay and some buildings.
It was in this era the Volunteer Fire Department was organized.

As in many parts of the country Ipswich had daylight saving time through part
of this period.

On December 10, 1918 dedication of the Edmunds County Service Flag was held at
the opera house; blue stars were attached for all soldiers and for those who
had made the supreme sacrifice the blue star was replaced by a gold one.
According to the record 370 stars were added.

On June, 1919 a celebration and dinner for returned soldiers was held on the
courthouse lawn. The Arch, now on Federal Highway No. 12, was erected by
citizens of Ipswich and vicinity in honor of living soldiers and in sacred
memory of its departed soldiers, was then located in the street square by the
Ipswich State Bank. The parade started from the arch, led by the band, children
carrying the colors, and a drill by soldiers with Capt. L. E. Smith and Lt. C.
C. Hiigel in command. After the dinner and program a ball game between
civilians and soldiers was held and minor sports enjoyed. Later a book of World
War I 1917-1918-1919 was compiled by C. L. Jackson and dedicated to the
soldiers of the county.

In these days, according to the advertisements, if you had Chamberlains Colic,
Cholera and Diarrhea remedy, you had a cure for everything. Advice given for
warmth on long trips by horse and buggy was to line your coat with common
newspaper which helped to keep out the wind.

1930 In February the Ipswich merchants put a ban on the sale of oleo "to help
bring dairy products back on a solid basis."

Joseph Brown, pioneer of 1883, passed away at his home on March 3rd. The
Ipswich H. S. basketball team won the regional tournament.

In April Lloyd Cronholm was appointed head of the Citizens' Military Training
Camp. The new lights were turned on, on Main Street.

The 1930 census revealed the fact that the County had made a gain of 373 in the
last ten years, the population now totaling 8709.

The Women's Clubs of Ipswich planted the zinnia gardens which have earned for
Ipswich the name "The Zinnia City." The Ipswich Swimming Pool was opened with
Robert Mears in charge.

In July two pioneers passed away, John Smith, and Mrs. Mary Lightner of Roscoe.
The County Commissioners set up a budget of $83,000 for the year 1931. 3 inches
of rainfall was recorded in August.

The Ipswich Library was moved from the building north of the Ipswich Bank to
the front entrance of the Opera House.

Deaths recorded included L. L. Caborn, E. J. Engler, Marvin Cochran and Dean
Hurlbut.

1931 William Morris, pioneer, died in February. In March the International
Peace Garden to be situated  on the border between Canada and North Dakota, was
proposed. J. W. Parmley was chosen as one of the trustees of the project. A
complete history of this was given by Mr. Parmley in the July 23rd issue of the
Tribune.

In July the temperature reached a record of 109 degrees and crops were badly in
need of rain. Grain was cut for hay. In August the first airmail service was
opened in the state.

On August 21st the Marcus P. Beebe Memorial Library was dedicated-this a gift
to the City by the widow, sons and daughters of the late Marcus P. Beebe. In

1932 In February an Unemployment Relief  Committee was set up and Edmunds
County was included in the Federal Feed Loan Area.

In March the Ipswich Tribune began its 49th year of publication. On April I
postage rates were raised to 3c for letters and 2c for postal cards.

The Edmunds County Museum was started, Leota Hall in the Library building to be

In May H. J. and M. B. Bebermeyer became the new owners of the Ipswich Printing
Co., to be known as the Edmunds County Printing Co. On May 18th the Ipswich

Chester Doolittle, cashier, was kidnapped. The bandits were caught and given
thirty years in the penitentiary.

In June a special train took many from Aberdeen, Ipswich, and other towns to
Lemmon for the dedication of the Petrified Wood Park. Also in June 2000
boosters visited the proposed dam site and artificial lake near Mina. Work was

Harvesting yields were fair in the county, and grasshoppers were a real menace.

E. B. TreFethren and Dave Picton died, also M. M. Arnold, a pioneer of this

Clothing, fuel and food were needed in the rural areas in the fall and a Red

1933 Mrs. W. A. Rogers passed away-she came to Ipswich from New York in 1886.

received from Washington that all banking business be suspended for four days.

The American Legion came into possession of the local Opera House - to be known
as Legion Hall.

On April 20th the United States went off the gold standard.

In June wind, hail and rain caused severe damage  in Edmunds County, especially

Myron D. Sheldon passed away in June, and Mrs. C. H. Barron in July. Mrs. H. L.

In September actual construction began on the dam at Mina, and the work was

In October the contract was let for a new Court House in Ipswich. In November a
sixty mile gale caused big damage around Ipswich, and the worst dust storm in

FIRE DESTROYS LEGION HALL

The Ipswich American Legion hall was  completely destroyed by fire on Tuesday
night, April 26, 1949. As a result of the fire, the Taylor building, adjoining
the Legion structure on the south, as also a complete wreck due to collapse of
the roof from a falling brick wall. Both buildings were the oldest two in the
business district of Ipswich.

An explosion, which happened shortly after the firemen had started throwing a
stream of water on the fire, blew out three sides of the brick building and
caused the roof to collapse.

This explosion seemed to be quite a coincidence, as fifty years before, when
the building was new it had suffered an explosion. It was after this first
explosion that the long iron rods were placed through the building and bolts
attached to reinforce the building.

1950-1958

        Main St. looking north. 1958

        Main Street looking south. 1958

In the span of eight years and more that this account covers, Ipswich has
experienced many changes. New people have established homes in the community,
new leaders have succeeded those who moved away or are deceased. New businesses
have come into operation, others have changed hands. About half of the
businesses are still operating under the same proprietors, and a substantial
number of the firms have enlarged or modernized their services to better
accommodate the needs of their patrons.

Changes are evidenced through the building of new homes, the construction and
remodeling of churches, completion of a school addition, and moving of electric
companies into new quarters. Some street improvement has been done and more in
the program for this year. Last year a change in downtown street lighting
brings a night time brightness long anticipated and now realized.

Many Ipswich people have been widely known through the years, and during this
period the abilities of other residents were recognized through appointment to
positions of importance and responsibility.

A number of civic leaders were taken by death. The community felt these losses
but in a measure profited by their good works and example.

Those living here in the past eight years are impressed by what may be a
natural or accidental change in winter weather. In 1950, 1951 and 1952
blizzards caused many hardships and inconveniences through blocked roads,
stranded motorists, losses of livestock, difficulty in getting to feed
supplies, disruption of mail service and postponement of planned events. In the
past three years an almost exact opposite has been experienced. No very severe
winter storms came this way, and there was little snow to hamper travel.

Ample rains have been received in the past two years. Hot June winds materially
cut the small grain yield in 1956, but in 1957 farmers enjoyed some of the most
ideal growing conditions. One of the most bountiful harvests in the records of
the area was very encouraging to those who had fair to poor crops in previous
years. Good subsoil moisture and added spring rains give hopes for another good
year.

One of the first business additions to take place in 1950 was the opening of
the Wall radiator and blacksmith shop, operated by Jim Davis. This later became
the location for the construction of building arches, designed by Carl Wall,
proprietor of the John Deere agency.

In the same year Dick Jones built several units of the Ipswich motel. This has
since been enlarged and further modernized. It is still operating under the
same management. Remodeling of the Red Owl Store and the opening of a milk
pasteurizing plant by Kenneth Likness came in this year.

One of the most significant pieces of construction started in September, 1950,
was the Sewage disposal plant, for which the city had previously voted a
$60,000 bond issue.

Early in 1951 Frank Archer sold his grocery and market to Mr. and Mrs. Peter J.
Geffre. Two months later Mr. Archer died after several months illness.

In the middle of March the FEM Electric Ass'n., Inc. celebrated the opening of
their new headquarters building on U. S. Highway 12. It gave this electric
cooperative a fine new structure from which to serve the three county area of
rural patrons.

Night Softball started on a new lighted field six blocks west of Main street.
Work began in the summer of 1951 on the New Holy Cross Catholic church, with
the cornerstone laying the first Sunday in August. Msgr. P. T. Monaghan of
Aberdeen was here for the occasion.

In autumn of the same year an old landmark was removed from the school
property. A section of the old school building was torn down.

Early in 1952 Pearl Jones opened the Pheasant Center Cafe, Don Williams bought
the Red Owl Store, and Andrew Thares resigned as manager of the Beebe elevator.

Holy Cross Catholic church was dedicated in the latter part of July. Bishop Wm.
O. Brady of the Sioux Falls diocese and many other Catholic clergy were here
for the occasion.

Another church event of note took place in September 14 when the Congregational
church of Ipswich was rededicated, following an extensive remodeling program.
400 people were here for the event.

Among business changes in the early part of 1953 were the sale of a part
interest in the Crissman Drug Store by H. S. Crissman to his son Earle, the
sale of the Gamble Hardware by J. W. Jeffery to R. A. Meyer, and the purchase
of the Engler building  by Roy Jones. This structure was enlarged for use by the
Montana Dakota Utilities Co.

In May, 1953, the Ipswich Lumber Co. held its grand opening. The business was
incorporated by Howard 0. Samp, Henry Beck and John J. Biegler. Later in the
year the Ipswich Rural Telephone Co. bought the Kranzler telephone lines, John
Klein resigned as manager of the Ipswich Cooperative market to accept a
position with FEM Electric, and Bob Herrick sold his interest in the Herrick
and Jackson store.

Yellowstone Trail
America's First Organized Highway Founded in Ipswich in 1912

Under the stimulus of the late J. W. Parmley, pioneer good-roads booster, a
conference of  business men was called in Ipswich in the spring of 1912 at
which the proposal was made that an organized effort be made to promote a good
road connecting towns and cities all the way from Minneapolis to Yellowstone
Park. Following that meeting other gatherings were held in rapid succession in
Aberdeen, Mobridge, Miles City and other towns, culminating in a big meeting at
Lemmon where the final organization was perfected in October 1912. The Lincoln
Highway association was not organized until 1913.

Mr. Parmley was elected president of the Yellowstone Trail association and for
years thereafter he devoted much of his time and effort to the promotion of the
trail. His enthusiasm became contagious and the trail expanded until in 1916 it
adopted a slogan "A Good Road from Plymouth Rock to Puget Sound."

Each state west of Ohio was first thoroughly organized and a "trailman" was
selected in each town to be the representative of the parent organization. Each
year representatives from each town were sent to a state meeting where local
and state problems were discussed and a member selected to represent the state
problems were discussed and a member selected to represent the state on the
executive committee of the association.

        J. W. Parmley

The association at once began the task of marking the trail with the insignia
of a yellow circle with a black arrow pointing toward Yellowstone Park. It was
marked from Syracuse, N. Y. to Seattle, Wash. via Buffalo, Cleveland, Ft.
Wayne, Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul and Minneapolis, Aberdeen, Miles City,
Missoula, and Spokane. The present day U. S. Highways 12 and 10 roughly follow
the original route of the Yellowstone Trail.

During the summer of 1917 the association established three tourist information
bureaus. The number was increased to seven in 1918, and by 1923 there were
thirteen bureaus maintained at the following points: Walla Walla, Spokane,
Missoula, Butte, Billings, Miles City, Aberdeen, Minneapolis, St. Paul,
Milwaukee, Chicago, Fort Wayne, and Cleveland.

The purpose of the bureaus was to help the traveling public with information
and to encourage the American public to travel by automobile. Each bureau was
supplied with maps published by the association in addition to booklets and
folders on local points of interest and summer resorts. Each bureau exchanged
information on the condition of roads in his section. In 1923 more than 250,000
tourists utilized the services of these bureaus. In the first year of operation
they serviced only 800.

In addition the association provided a road condition map service to hotels,
garages, or restaurants who maintained membership in the association. The
service consisted of a large wall map of the entire 3,700 mile trail and a
weekly report on the condition of each section of the trail. The service was
supplied from May to September each year and from 500 to 1,000 business places
availed themselves of the service.

        Third Day after breaking sod on the Yellowstone Trail nine miles west
        of Ipswich. 1912


The result of thus organizing these local groups into a sustained national
program was that in each state more work was done and more money was spent in
building better roads along the route of the trail. Another result, of course,
was an enormous increase in tourist traffic over the route.

Cities and towns along the route began to make accommodations for the motoring
tourist. Free camp grounds were first provided to be followed later with cabin
camps. In 1922 it was reported that there were 126 civic free camp grounds for
tourists along the Yellowstone trail. There were usually kitchen facilities,
plus water, wood, toilets, and in some cases shower baths. In Walla Walla a
large city park was turned over to tourists and a "Greeters Committee" from the
Commercial club called on the tourists in the park each evening.

The Yellowstone Trail received nation-wide publicity in 1915 when Mr. Parmley
undertook to carry a letter from the mayor of Chicago to the mayor of Seattle
in 100 hours by a series of relay cars. The distance was 2,611 miles and the
time consumed was 97 hours. This was largely over dirt roads, hundreds of miles
of which were only cowpaths three years earlier.

The following year the organization carried a letter from the Secretary of War,
handed by him to a driver at Plymouth Rock, directed to the Commandant at Fort
Lawton, Seattle. The trip of 3,754 miles was made in 121 hours and 12 minutes.
The bearers of this message faced a rainstorm for over 1,000 miles and at times
could not do better than 15 miles per hour. The best time was made across
Montana where an average speed of 34 miles per hour was maintained. Police
escorts were provided through the cities traversed en route.

        Charlie Cole, Joe Parmley, Unknown. Mr. Parmley's early day road drag.

It was largely due to the consistent efforts of Mr. Parmley and the Yellowstone
Trail association that Yellowstone park was finally opened to automobile
traffic.

During the twenties the efforts of trail boosters was centered on getting an
all-weather gravelled road across the state. Mr. Parmley lived to see this part
of his dream come true but he died in 1940, ten years before the final link in
the Trail in North Dakota was paved. The last stretch in South Dakota, west of
McLaughlin was completed in October 1950.
        Irene Parmley Trotzig

        First Arch on Main St. Soldiers Day 1918

Memorial Arch

The Memorial Arch

Nowhere has a project been developed which drew the cooperation of every
community group as did the building of the arch in Ipswich. When the Armistice
was signed at the close of World War I and it was definitely known that Edmunds
county boys who had not been killed would be coming back there was a movement
started in all parts of the county to give them a royal welcome. Ipswich, the
county seat was decided upon as the place. That welcome was not to be for just
a day but a lasting tribute to their valor as well as a reminder to later
generations of the part they played in the war.

So it was that an arch of triumph was decided upon and a structure of wood
built across the Main street of Ipswich. This was then painted with a soldier
of the Civil War on one pillar and a soldier of the World War on the other.
There it stayed for a number of years, regularly repainted and decorated, until
the traffic on the street required that it be moved. In 1922 it was placed over
the Yellowstone Trail, properly anchored and again redecorated. The idea was to
have it at a place "Where the traffic of a continent would pass under it."

The wooden arch stood sturdily until a tornado which also wrecked houses and
buildings twisted the arch so that it would be impractical to again repair.
Then the people of the community decided to build an arch for permanence, of
stone, artistic in design, something entirely different that anything else then
built over an American highway.

In 1928 the American Legion and the Lion's Club were named as the two
organizations to take responsibility for building a stone arch. The approval of
the State Highway Dept. was obtained. Their engineer then specified a clearance
between the pillars of twenty-four feet and a clearance vertically of fourteen
feet minimum. The city offered its aid, financial and otherwise and a fund
raising drive was started which proved successful. It was decided that a stone
worker of artistic ability was to be hired who had experience and the man
selected was C. J. Madison, then located at Corpus Christi, Texas. He had built
numerous buildings of native stone in North and South Dakota and was well
versed in this type of work.

The core of each pillar of the arch is concrete reinforced with scrap iron and
steel of every description.  The overall height of the arch is 26 feet. The
lower six feet are of cut granite cut square side, the next eight feet are
narrower and also granite with the addition of follils, stone oddities from all
over the United States and from foreign countries and the top twelve feet of
"rubblestone" construction. Each pier goes into the ground another eight feet
with ample footings. The top horizontal part of the arch is based on four steel
I-beams welded to steel reinforcing framework of each pier. Inside this cross
section of the arch is an electric clock and wiring for the sign "Welcome" and
the name "Ipswich."

On each of the piers is a large bronze tablet  embedded in stone and cement. On
the north tablet is a brief sketch of the start of the Yellowstone Trail and a
lasting tribute to Joseph 'W. Parmley and his co-workers who worked in the
interest of a good road from Plymouth Rock to Puget Sound. On the south pillar
is another plaque with this wording: This pillar of the Memorial Arch of
Ipswich is erected in honor of the valiant men of the community of, Ipswich who
answered our country's call to arms in the World War of 1917-1918 and as an
everlasting monument to those who so nobly made the supreme sacrifice." Below
are then named the men from the area who lost their lives in World War I.

        Original Arch after being moved to Highway 12. 1922

        Present Arch under construction. 1928

        The Arch. 1958

Contents of the outside or visible portions of the arch are varied and
interesting. There are stones from nearly every state through which the
Yellowstone  Trail passes. Many Indian clubs gathered from the prairie haunts
of ancient Indians, a beautiful piece of petrified wood from North Dakota, a
number  of stones brought from Europe, Asia and Africa, all are embedded in the
arch. Stones taken from sacred places in the Holy Land are to be found, as are
other stones peculiar to only Hawaii, Philippines and Pacific islands. Gold
quartz is there from the Homestake mine, also scoria from the burning coal mine
of North Dakota. One rarity is a large chunk of sand calcite crystals from the
Bad Lands, another is the remains of a meteorite.

Capping each pillar are two almost perfectly round boulders. One came down with
the glacier, rounded in the ages it was ground by the ice. The other is a
"cannon-ball" taken from the river of that name in North Dakota.
        Merrill Bebermeyer

OLD COURTHOUSE CONSTRUCTED IN 1889

The former Edmunds county courthouse which was built in 1889 and 1890 was
considered at that time one of the best buildings that could be built.
Constructed of stone and brick at a cost to the county of $11,000 it was
considered adequate for many years to come. Public records had accumulated by
1935 to such an extent that there was not room in the building for some of the
county officers. The offices of the sheriff and the county judge, as well as,
that of the agricultural agent had to be housed in other buildings in down town
Ipswich. Furthermore there was a constant menace of fire as this old building
was not fire proof.

        Old Court House being torn down. 1934

        Edmunds County Courthouse, Ipswich, S. D. Erected in 1934; Dedicated
        June 5, 1935. Photo by Moonlight

NEW COURTHOUSE

In 1933 the depression and total crop failure gave plenty of time to think and
devise plans to help us out of our difficulties. One statement was that now
would be an opportune time to build a new court-house for the reason that such
a project would furnish employment. However, everyone realized that in view of
the times, citizens would be very reluctant to vote in favor of any bond issue.
The result of such reasoning was that thoughts of building were promptly
dismissed.

The Legislature convened in special session about that time and the unexpected
happened. It was found that some counties in the state at that time had
accumulated substantial building funds, Edmunds county being one among them,
having a building fund of $61,030. 75. Such counties would be in a position to
build without embarrassment provided the matter of a bond issue could be
facilitated.

Sensing that this end could be accomplished in a number of counties if the
provision for bonding the county could be modified to apply only in counties
which already had adequate building funds which, however, were invested in farm
mortgages or other securities. A plan was then conceived whereby provisions of
bonding the county could be facilitated to make possible in such counties, the
building of new courthouses.

A bill was introduced providing that in counties meeting such conditions the
citizens could petition boards of county commissioners to issue bonds for
building purposes, not to exceed the amount then in the building fund of such
county. A circumstance that favored the bill was the fact that the federal
government was giving grants of 30 per cent for public works construction.

During the last days of that special session, the bill was enacted into law, to
become effective August 17, 1933.

The petitions were presented and the Board of County Commissioners found them
conforming with the law, then by a 3 to 2 vote adopted a resolution of approval
and forthwith the Board also resolved to issue bonds to the amount of
$61,030.75, said figure representing the amount then in courthouse building
fund.

This action was followed by the Board employing an architect, and selling bonds
for the different classifications of work. The Board passed a resolution  that
all contracts negotiated shall be subject to the condition that the courthouse
building project be accepted by the Federal Public Works Administration.

The construction work for the new building was begun early in the spring of
1934. The general construction work was under the direction of Foreman Lew
Peters. By the end of the year the building was completed and on January 7,
1935, it was formally accepted by the Board of County Commissioners. The offices
were moved into the new buildings on January 16th.

The Board of County Commissioners which took the initial step in the building
of the new courthouse were: Wm. Stafford, Tom Langan, Carl C. Krueger, A. E.
Herrboldt (auditor), Joseph Bachman, and Ludwig Merkel.

FIRST BAPTIST CHURCH

Among the early settlers who came to Edmunds County, South Dakota were many
Christian people who felt the need, and value, of the public worship of God.

On January 20, 1884 several families of the Baptist faith met in McNamara Hill,
and organized a church with 13 members. Shortly after this a Sunday School was
started which has been  continuously maintained since that time.

The first pastor, W. T. Williams was ordained by the church.

Services were conducted each Sunday in McNamara Hall, till the Methodist people
who in the meantime had built, kindly offered the use of their building. As
time passed, many of the Methodist moved to other locations. The few who were
left did not feel that they could continue with their work, and the building
was sold to the Baptists, and this building was used for many years.

During that time the membership increased and between the years of 1906 and
1910 the building was repaired, a Sunday School room added on the second floor,
and a furnace installed. A large  parsonage was also completed in 1912.

At the annual meeting in 1922, Mrs. Leota Beebe, a charter member, offered to
give two thirds of the costs of a new $25,000 church edifice. The offer was
accepted, and $11,000 was pledged by the membership. Work was started in June
1924. Much of the labor was volunteered by the men of the church and
congregation. Stone, of the native granite, and gravel, all from Edmunds
County, was brought to the site, and the building was erected under the
direction of Mr. C. J. Madison of Kansas City, and his crew of skilled workmen,
masons and stone cutters.

The church was dedicated, debt free on April 4, 1926, as a thank offering from
the people for the many blessings received from the bountiful hand of God.

The work of the church has gone steadily on under the leadership of the pastors
who have served us, men of God who have given of their best that the Word might
be spread abroad.

Faithful members and officers have also served, giving of their time and
talents in carrying on the work.

The membership has continued to grow during the seventy five years of the
church's existence, and they truly give thanks to the Heavenly Father for his
love and guidance, for without His leading, nothing lasting could have been
accomplished.

HOLY CROSS CATHOLIC PARISH

Among the early settlers to come to the Ipswich territory there were people of
the Catholic faith. They were of a good hardy stock, to be able and willing to
face the hardships of the pioneers who turned these wide open prairies into
productive farms and good homes. The first settlers to arrive here right about
the time that the Milwaukee Railroad built its main line through this territory
came from Iowa, Wisconsin, Nebraska, and even as far away as New York. They
were Irish, German, French and English. From foreign countries also they came;
mostly, however, about the turn of the century. These were mostly Bohemian
stock from Bohemia - a few coming from Minnesota - and from Russia.

Among the pioneering Catholic families we find record of the following:
Families of Conrad Bickert, Engler, Peter Kammers, Nick Kammers, Will Rogers,
Patrick McGillick, Peter Hansen, John Ward, N. J. Andrew Perrion, John Kimmitt,
James Kinney, Frank Kinney, Pat Cox, John Cox, John Shaken, John O'Reilley,
John, Tom and Ed. Doonan, Frank J. Tracy, John Kearns, Frank McCue, John
Dieterich, Martin Blank, Patrick Doyle, and James Guhin. Of the Bohemians it
seems that Wenceslaus Sindelar and his family were about the first to come,
believed to be about 1884. The next few years saw the arrival of the following
families: Frank Simon, Frank Hamak, Albert Kub, Frank Pribly, Martin Zahratka,
John Jakes, Joseph Bicek, Frank Bicek, John Ballatta, Vincent Stulc, Anton
Stulc, Anton Blaka, Joe Kub, Frank Kub, Lou Havel, John Stanek, Frank Kuchenka,
Frank Ballatta, Anton C. Ballata, John Markovetz, Frank Schnorek.

About the year 1890 and thereafter the Germans who had previously emigrated to
Russia came to this county directly from Russia and settled here. They were
known as the German-Russians. The earliest of these to settle here were the
families of: Anton, Jacob and Mike Fischer, Joe Schumacher, Mike Biegler, Mike
Dutenhoeffer, Ludwig Kramer, Wendel Dosch, Jacob Hagle, Joe Bender, John
Tuscherer, Joe Gauer, Daniel Lacher, Sylvester Ussellman.

To take care of the spiritual needs of these first Catholic settlers Priests
from Aberdeen would come - at first irregularly, but as the population grew
more frequently. The best known and most popular of the first priests to attend
to the Ipswich Catholics was Father Haire. The first Mass was offered in what
was known then as McNamara Hall in Ipswich. This building was located on the
west side of Main street where the present H. L. Woodworth office now stands.
The hall was upstairs over the General Store. Mr. N. J. Engler, father of
George and Bert Engler, served that Mass. This was January 9, 1884

        New Holy Cross Church and Rectory

According to available records the first resident Pastor here was Rev. Bernard
J. Hens. It was under his pastorate that Holy Cross Parish was organized as an
independent parish. The Deed for the church property was given by Charles and
Delia Prior of Minneapolis, Minnesota. Property cost $50.00 and was located
where the present new edition to Ipswich Public School now stands. The Articles
of Incorporation of Holy Cross Catholic Church are dated May 28th, 1887. It was
this year that the first Catholic Church, a frame building, was built on this
property.

The first Baptism recorded is: Frances A. O'Rielly, Aug. 22, 1886.

The first marriage: Louis and Barbara DeRouchey, who settled in Hoven, November
4, 1886.

The first burial: Martha Ussellman, August 17, 1897.

First Confirmation class: June 17, 1903, by Bishop O'Gormann.

The early Bohemian settlers seemed to be concentrated to the South, Southwest
of Ipswich. The Early German Russians mostly to the East, North east. Bohemian
Priests, notably the late Father Bouska, would come to take occasional care of
these good people whose language problem made it hard for a non-Bohemian Priest
to minister to them.

In the year 1906 the German Russians built a frame church for themselves,
located about 12 miles Northeast of Ipswich. This "New Strasburg" Church served
as a Mission church to Holy Cross Parish until about the early '30's. At that
time the final unification of the people into Holy Cross Parish of Ipswich was
completed and the New Strasburg Church abandoned. This church was finally
dismantled in 1950. In the year

1908 a new Rectory was built and a new and larger Church was begun at a new
location. Finances did not permit the completion of the proposed church so only
the basement was built. This was under the pastorate of Father J. O'Hara. In
1911 Father Frederic Bormann became pastor of Holy Cross. Seeing the increase
in Catholic population  of the parish he began to think and speak about a
parochial school so that besides the three traditional R's of all schools the
children would also receive the fourth RELIGION. He felt that it was not
sufficient to train only the mind of a child, but also that his will must be
properly guided along moral lines. Only a religious education could accomplish
that. After various experiments at holding classes in the basement of the
Church, or rather what was then the church, it was decided to build a school
building. The corner stone for this was laid in 1920 and has been in continuous
use since its completion in 1921. At the present time there is an enrollment of
140 in Holy Cross School. The Benedictine Sisters, whose Mother House is in
Yankton, staff the school, It took a long time to pay for the school during the
hard times of the 20's and early 30's.

        Interior of Holy Cross Church

Father Bormann, a genius at trying to invent something to raise money for the
payment of the school, finally succeeded in building a candle for church use
which was and still is outstanding in the nation because of its good and long
burning qualities. The result was LUX CANDLE COMPANY, the only  industry in
Ipswich. Father Bormann was Pastor until his death December 1, 1947. On January
6, 1948 Rev. Clarence W. Trautner became Pastor of Holy Cross Parish. He
continued the fund raising campaign, which Father Bormann had started, for a
new church. From 1909 on the basement was the only church known to the
Catholics of Ipswich. Years were a bit better for the financial status of the
people. In 1951, with $125,000.00 on hand, construction of the present Church
and Rectory was started. The property on which the new construction took place
was acquired from Mr. and Mrs. E. E. Hubbard located directly across the street
from the old Church and Rectory. Construction was completed June 2, 1952. The
First Mass celebrated in the new Holy Cross Church was on June 15, 1952. The
formal dedication of church and consecration of the Main Altar took place on
July 22, 1952 with Bishop William 0. Brady officiating. Total cost of new
church and rectory, furnishing included, was $250,000.00. In 1953, the year
after the new church was built, the old church basement was remodelled into a
parish hall at the cost of about $18,000.00. And old Rectory was remodelled
into a Sisters' Convent. In three years all debts were paid. Those were good
years and people so very cooperative.

At present Holy Cross parish has 250 units with a population of right around I
100. Basically the nationalities of the people is somewhat the same as it was
fifty years ago, but a continuous change is occurring with the coming of new
generations and intermarriages among them so that by now a rather favorable
leaven is bringing all together into one single family of God. And all
foremost, of course, good Americans.

The Altar Society is the largest society in the parish, about which more is
written in another place in this book. The Men and Women Foresters are active.
For special study there are at present four well-functioning Study Clubs, viz.:
St. Monica's, St. Gerard's, St. Ann's and Ave Maria. For the future it is hoped
that all the members of Holy Cross Parish will ever be true and loyal citizens
of our Country, State and City and that the motto in stone over the front
entrance of Holy Cross School will inspire a good life FOR GOD AND COUNTRY.
        Rev. C. W. Trautner

THE IPSWICH CONGREGATIONAL CHURCH

The Congregational Church of Ipswich, began in 1883 as a Sunday School at
Freeport located one mile east and one-half mile north of what is now Ipswich.
The Reverend Belt, a brother of the late Mrs. H. T. Dailey, Hattie Fitch
Rogers, and Mr. and Mrs. J. H. Marshall organized the Sunday School. Mr. Belt,
a Congregational minister preached.

Mrs. Caborn writes, "I think some of the services,  at least, were held in the
Marshall store; seats were improvised from store boxes, sawhorses etc; which
was moved to Ipswich later and is now the residence of Mr. and Mrs. Jacob
Theis."

Later a union Protestant Church was organized, composed of Methodist, Baptists,
Presbyterians,  Episcopalians and Congregationalists. It was necessary to have
a name to be eligible for aid from any Home Missionary Building Society. As the
acting minister was a Congregationalist, the majority voted for that title. The
Baptists and Methodists joined hands and started a second church.

The Congregational Church was incorporated on September 27th, 1884 and was
called the Ipswich Congregational Church.

The church building was erected that fall and the first sermon preached January
4th, 1885 by the Reverend J. Norton. Rev. Norton stayed six weeks, Rev. G. W.
Honey stayed eight months followed by James H. Kyle 1886-1889; later he was
Senator Kyle. Rev. Kyle might truly be called 'Father of Congregationalism in
Edmunds County, South Dakota.

Ministers came and went many not staying more than a year until the pastorate
of Rev. E. B. TreFethren who served from 1899-1905. It was he who built the
parsonage. The ministers followed in order, Reverends I. J. Treiber, Harry
Evans,  Godfrey Matthews, George Green, H. T. Owen, E. C. Salter, M. C.
Haecker, David Graham, Lester E. Smith, George E. Hartz, and presently John A.
Northrop.

Rev. I. J. Treiber succeeded Rev. Kyle and under his pastorate a young peoples
organization called Christian Endeavor was organized; also an adult Welsh class
with John Owens (father of Mrs. J. D. Williams) as its teacher. A Sunday School
Library was provided about this time by Mrs. J. H. Marshall.

The Rev. Harry Evans came in 1906 and stayed until 1912. These were years of
many new immigrants. An epidemic of typhoid fever broke our and Rev. Harry
Evans, frail as he was went about sitting up at night helping to care for the
sick in homes. The F. W. Brunkow family had three or four stricken at the same
time and the brother Fred died. Rev. Harry Evans and the neighbors had to
remove the body from an upstairs window because of the seriousness of the ones
down stairs.

It was during the years of Rev. Godfrey Matthews pastorate that the beautiful
stained glass windows were given as memorials and in honor of members.

Rev. Matthews and his sister Grace were lovers of good music. Rev. Matthews and
Mrs. Frank (Ruth Hill) Nichols visited the business men collecting a needed
Fifty dollars in order that a new organ might be purchased. In writing of the
incident Ruth says, "It was through the influence of Rev. Matthews that I
became interested in music, for which I  remain indebted" Ruth's first
selection on the organ was from Mozart. Rev. Matthews and Sister Grace
organized the first all girls choir, such as we have now.

Probably the longest pastorate was that of Rev. E. C. Salter Sept. 1927 to
April 1937. The west addition and the basement of the present Church were
built. These were perhaps the most trying times, since early days, as we
suffered drouth, the closing of Banks and the depression.

The period in the history of our Church the most was the ordination in 1935 of
Stephen Williams, son of J. D. Williams, in the Congregational Church at
Russell, Kansas. "Steve" served as a Chaplain in the United States Army during
World War 2 and was stationed in the Philippines and later in Japan. At
present, he is pastor at the First Congregational Church, Emporia, Kansas.

John Weber Bruce, youngest brother of Mable Moritz became affiliated with the
Congregational Church December 1907. Shortly after he left  Ipswich, he joined
the Navy where he did YMCA work in China for two years. It was during these
years working with youth he felt the call to become a minister. He married a
girl of the M. E. Methodist faith and joined her church upon his return to
Freeport, Illinois. He had three churches out from Freeport at one time. At
present he has but one, that being in Freeport, Illinois. He has charge of
youth in their summer camps. We are always happy to hear of the success of our
youth.

On December 12, 1943 the Church observed its sixtieth Anniversary with fitting
ceremonies. Superintendent Rev. S. W. Keck, of Huron delivered the anniversary
sermon. Special guests were Mrs. L. L. (Alice) Caborn, Mr. and Mrs. George
Jaquith, Mrs. John (Elizabeth) Owens and Mr. and Mrs. George C. Meadows.

The Rev. Lester E. Smith came in January 1948 and it was during his pastorate
that the remodeling of the sanctuary was again taken up. Rev. Smith accepted a
call in April 1950 to Pierre, South Dakota, but the work of remodeling
continued. Mrs. S. W. Keck delivered the first service in the remodeled
sanctuary October 29, 1950. Not many who were there that morning will forget
the thrill of that service, held in the remodeled sanctuary October 29, 1950.
Not many who were there that truly inspired the spirit of worship.

The Rev. George E. Hartz coming from Bertha, Minn. preached his first sermon
January 1952. From April 1950 until January 1952 the Church was served with
supplies. The Rev. George E. Hartz, as pastor was loved by all with whom he
came in contact and served his Church with all his strength until death came
from a heart condition June 9th, 1956 while on duty. Mrs. Hartz, Jan and Doris
are missed in the work of the Church.

In 1943 the Women's Fellowship started a fund for an electric organ. Christmas
(1943) found the Baldwin Electric organ installed. Merritt Johnson of Aberdeen,
South Dakota gave a beautiful organ concert in the sanctuary for the organ
benefit. With the help of friends our organ was fully paid ($2,900.00) for in
1944.

Mrs. H. J. Bebermeyer presented to the Church in memory of H. J. Bebermeyer a
memorial plaque. A permanent record book of memorial gifts given has been
placed in the narthex.

In memory of Mrs. Robert (Bird) Hill the church pews were given for the choir.
This gave the Woman's Fellowship another inspiration of starting a fund for
church pews. In 1956 four hundred dollars was put on certificate of deposit.
The Memorial Committee having considerable amount on hand decided, together
with the trustees to order twenty-six Church Pews from the Prairie States Wood
Arts, Redfield, S. D. to be installed soon.

The Woman's Fellowship completed the room to the southwest and the Roger's
Circle furnished it, to be used as a family room for funerals.

The Men's Fellowship have completed a Sunday School room on third floor. There
are still more rooms to be completed.

The church comes to this Seventy-fifth annual milestone conscious of the grace
of almighty God and the loving stewardship of consecrated Christian people,
who, through all these years have given of their prayers, their service, and of
their substance. Aware of its heritage of a living past, the Church strives
onward to fulfill its Mission.

ZION EV. LUTHERAN CHURCH

The first Lutherans that moved into this area made requests for the services of
a Lutheran pastor. Their requests were answered by pastors coming from Leola
and Hosmer. The roots of Zion can be traced back to a little school house in
Union township. Here they gathered for lack of a church of their own. Things
appeared brighter when they were permitted to use a church building in that
area. Gradually the shift toward Ipswich led to services being held from house
to house.

1921 was a joyful year for the little congregation for that year marked the
arrival of the first resident pastor, Rev. Walter J. Meier. Since its
organization it has received continual services of pastors from the Wisconsin
Synod, of which it is a member.

The church still felt a deep need for a building to call its own. Not until
1931 could a used church be purchased and moved to its present location. It
formerly stood in the county of Claude Valley, southwest of Roscoe. The church
still serves the congregation well. It has gone through a fire, undergone
renovations, submitted by the natural ravages of time and still be happily
called "our church" by its 110 baptized members.

Until about 1927 Zion formed one parish with the congregation at Loyalton.
Since that time it has been united with First Lutheran Church of Faulkton.

Zion is generally classed as a conservative church and is a member of the
Wisconsin Synod. It is supported solely by the free-will offerings of its
members.

The present officers of the congregation are: H. Wockenfuss, President; B.
Wockenfuss, Secretary; D. Linaman, Treasurer.

The following pastors have served the congregation from its beginning: Rev. W.
J. Meier, Rev. E. Schaller, Rev. R. Gamm, Rev. W. Schmidt, Rev. H. Birner, Rev.
M. Eibs, and Rev. L. Grams.

THE POWELL CHURCHES

The first settlers arrived in Powell, March 22, 1883, and put up the noted Big
Shanty, a 20 x 40 frame building, on a claim occupied by the late Huge Griffth,
a leading member of the Colony.

This was the first frame building erected on the vast prairie west of Aberdeen
and served as hotel, church and meeting house. Here, the settlers dined, slept
and worshipped the God who had brought them to this vast wilderness.

It became the social center as well as the religious center and from the very
first, religious services were held despite the fact that there was no church
or paid preacher.

More immigrants came in the spring of 1884, to the land not already occupied,
built a large school house and used it as a place of worship until the church
was built a little later.

        Powell Presbyterian Church

The church was built in 1886, though the first meeting, according to records,
was held on September 9, 1884, at which meeting a Calvinistic Methodist Church
was organized and Owen E. Williams and John P. Jones elected Deacons.

In 1866, the new church was erected at the cost of about $1,000. The Rev. John
W. Morgan was the first Pastor.

After the building of the Methodist Church, it became apparent that the Welsh
were not of a single mind and that the services that were extended by this
group would not serve well the whole community so a Congregational Church was
organized and built.

The large new church, 40 x 60, was built on about the present site of the barn
which stands on the farm across from the present church.

It was, unquestionably, the largest frame building west of Aberdeen, when
completed in the summer of 1889 and was very artistically furnished with rugs
and other fine appointments, at a total costs of $3,000. Rev. John T. Lewis was
the Pastor.

However, the 1890's were not kind, population shifts had already set in and in
1902, the building was sold and moved to Ipswich where it became a part of the
present Congregational church and the parsonage.

On June 2, 1911, the Methodist Church was completely destroyed by a tornado.
The faithful congregation proceeded to build a new church which was dedicated
on Thanksgiving Day.

It was during the 1923-33 decade that the disbanding of the National
Calvinistic Methodist Welsh Church and the assimilation of the congregations
into the Presbytery of the Presbyterian Church, U. S. A. took place and with it
the last full time minister and the yoking with Roscoe and Java fields.

The church reached its modern era and the inevitable happened when a minister
was secured who could not preach the Welsh language. The Rev. J. Rhys Evans was
the last Welsh minister to serve the church.

In the period between 1941-45, the church became a member of the Aberdeen
Presbytery.

On October 11, 1953, the Seventieth Anniversary of Religious Service to the
Powell community was observed, with one of the three services, entirely in the
Welsh language by the late Rev. Stephen Jones, Madison, South Dakota.

During the period 1883-1958, eighteen full time and part time ministers served
the Powell Church, with an average membership of eighty members.

On June 8, 1958, the church celebrated its Seventy Fifth Anniversary of
Religious Service to the Powell community and a memorial to the "Pioneers of
Powell".
        Blodwen Richards

IPSWICH WESLEYAN METHODIST CHURCH

During the summer of 1931, Rev. W. C. Brannon, then pastor of the Aberdeen

Sunday, interest and attendance increased. On November 15, 1931, under the
leadership of Rev. J. C. McGovern, the church was organized under the doctrines
taught by the Wesleyan Methodist Church of America.

The following twelve persons were charter members: Mr. and Mrs. Henry Albee,
Mrs. W. H. Tullis, Mr. and Mrs. Charles Linden, Mr. and Mrs. H. B. Engel,

never ending labors during the construction of the church building.

Origin of W. C. T. U.

The Ipswich Woman's Christian Temperance Union was organized in 1884 and was
disbanded in 1956.

Prominent leaders during these years were: Mrs. W. E. Herrick, Mrs. Melissa B.
Parmley, Mrs. Ruby Jackson, Mrs. Floy L. Cochran, Mrs. Pluma White, Mrs. Pearl
Fear, Inez Perisho, and Mrs. J. W. McCarter.

Ipswich for several years was the largest Union in the State of South Dakota
and every one of the 145 members were subscribers to the Union Signal Paper and
the Crusader (a paper for children.) This was placed in every school in the
county. Prizes were given to all High School boys who finished the School
course without smoking.

During this time members of the Union attended two World's Conventions. They
also attended every District and State convention where numerous prizes were
won by Mrs. W. E. Herrick for being a member for 63 years and having the
largest number of Union Signal subscribers, and for giving the paper to all
ministers in the city.
        Mary Herrick

Ladies Aid Society

The Ladies Aid Society of the Congregational Church was organized shortly after
the founding of the church in the 1880's. Mrs. C. H. Barron was the first
president, and as today, its main function was giving Aid to the Church
whenever it was needed. Many of its charter members were pioneer Mothers and
Grandmothers of Ipswich citizens of today.

The meetings were held every Tuesday and the time was designated from three to
five. The initiation fee was twenty-five cents and a fee of five cents a week
was collected from each member.

afternoons. At one meeting a month a twenty-five cent supper was served to
members and their families.

Ice Cream Lawn socials were considered profitable during the summer months.

When the church treasury wasn't able to meet its expenses members of the Ladies
Aid went out from house to house and collected from its members.

In the 20's the Ladies Aid was sponsoring two bazaars a year, one at Easter
time and one in December.

Legion Boys, served four public suppers and 5 public dinners.

organizations of the Congregational Churches. It was then that we began an

how much she can increase her investment in one year. The plan for dividing the
group into Circles was begun in January 1953.

Altar Society

The Altar Society of Holy Cross Parish is a society composed of the ladies of
the parish. The purpose of the Altar Society is social and financial; social in
that if affords a chance for the women of the parish to meet each other more
intimately as a group,  exchange ideas and discuss matters and problems of
religion and life; and financial in that they society takes it upon itself to
handle all the affairs of the parish, which have for their purpose raising of
money for the parish, and thus alleviates to a great degree the financial
burden involved in running a parish the size of Holy Cross.

In early years of the society, it was difficult to put on dinners or bazaars
because the conveniences were very inadequate. In the days of Rev. Father
Bormann, the ladies worked very hard during the summer months in order to have
a bazaar in the fall, usually October or November. At that time, the Opera
House and St. James Hotel were the only available places. It meant work, for
all the equipment had to be borrowed.

In the first years, the society was divided into seven bands, each with its own
leader, but later it was thought best to have the ladies meet as one band, in
order to have better social contact.

Rev. Father Bormann passed away in 1947, but what he started was continued by
his successor, Rev. Father Trautner. After the completion of the new church,
Father Trautner and many of the faithful and willing men remodelled the old
basement into a hall, with a spacious dining room having a seating capacity of
about 250 guests. The kitchen was well equipped in every way, so the ladies
have now been rewarded for it is now a pleasure to work.

The last four or five years have been very good in a financial way. Under very
capable leaders and the very fine cooperation of the members, the society has
raised over $20,000 during this five year period.

We pause to honor the many good ladies who always did their part in every way
and who have passed to their reward. "God bless them."
        Josephine Archer

Cum - Chat - Awhile Club

Cum Chat Awhile Club was the name chosen on March 18, 1931 when the meeting was
held with Mrs. George Hall. Previous to that time it was known as the
Missionary Aid Society and then Rosette Ladies Aid until a July 1927 meeting
when it was organized under a new name and new bylaws and became known as
Community Ladies Aid, dissolving obligations to the Baptist Church, with which
the Aid had been associated for many years.

Even before known as the Missionary Aid Society a group of pioneer women had a
Rosette Aid affiliated with the little country church which was located west of
the John Krambeal farm. For many years this group of women held ice cream
socials, oyster stew suppers, basket socials and picnics furnishing recreation
and programs for the families of the Community. Some of these early members were
mothers or mother-in-laws of some of the members in the present club.

Mrs. Bowers was elected president of the newly formed Community Ladies Aid;
Mrs. E. P. Jones, vice president; Mrs. L. Barker, secretary and Mrs. Marion
Hummell, treasurer. At that time there were twenty-seven members.

After changing to Cum Chat Awhile Club, election was held in June with Mrs.
Lester Davis, Mrs. Isabel Olson and Mrs. Nellie Bantz as officers for the club
of twenty-eight members. Meetings were held twice each month with all day
meetings during the winter when the menfolks were invited for dinner.

At present the membership is only eleven and the social meetings are held on
the last Friday of each month. Among the membership roll of 1927 those who are
still members are Marion Hummell, Mrs. R. M. Kitchell, Mrs. Floyd Bowers, Mrs.
Lester Davis and Mrs. Margaret Stabenow.

Present officers are Mrs. Leo Auchampach, president; Mrs. Sidney Wilcox, vice
president and Margaret Stabenow, secretary and treasurer.

Twentieth Century Club 1914 - 1958

About forty-four years ago, a small group of pioneer ladies decided to get a
little more knowledge of the world and its people. They felt a deep need for
serious study, as well as some fun mixed in with work and everyday household
chores.

Mrs. James, Mrs. Doolittle, Mrs. Allen and Mrs. Ring, with several others met
at the home of Mrs. Alice Caborn, on the afternoon of August 19, 1914, and
there organized the Twentieth Century Club of Ipswich.

The above mentioned ladies were the first officers and were to serve one year.
Dues were one dollar; colors were green and white; and the club flower was the
white carnation.

The object of this club, then as now, was mutual improvement, and wider
knowledge so they fittingly chose for the motto, "Through knowledge, we behold
the worlds creation."

Sixteen women were charter members. In early years membership was limited to
twenty, later on to twenty-five and at present the limit is again twenty
members.

At this time the members felt that December, May, July, August and part of
September were too busy months for serious study and social fun, so these were
designated vacation time.

During the first year, the club federated with the state federation and so
joined a national federation of several million like-minded and awakened women
of the nation and the world.

As an example of the variety and interest of their programs we find the
following: "Instrumental Music" by Theo Jones; "Parliamentary Drill" by Mrs.
Caborn; "Writers of the Revolutionary Period" by Mrs. Coulter; "Washington
Irving & His Works" by Mrs. Hartzell; "Orators & Statesmen of the times" by
Mrs. Brewster; "Nathaniel Hawthorne" by Mrs. Jones.

However it was not all study as the records tell of hayrides, sleighing,
parties for brides, plays, banquets for husbands and guests, picnics, musicals
and pantomimes. For instance, take as an example the New Years party of 1915.
it was held in the Masonic Hall and the dinner was served at ten-thirty to
sixty guests. The tables were decorated with white carnations and ferns and the
menu was: roast turkey with dressing, mashed potatoes and gravy, celery,
olives, cranberries, jelly, pickles, cabbage salad, rolls, ice cream, cake,
coffee and mints.

Of interest in the old records, were the debates in which the members
participated. One debate was on "Women's Suffrage" in which Mrs. Caborn took
the affirmative and Lillian Thiel, the negative. Alas, the judges voted in favor
of the negative. Another topic for debate, "Shall the Bible be taught in the
Public Schools?" This time the members were divided into two teams with Mrs.
Caborn heading the negative and Mrs. James the affirmative side. The
affirmative won this time.

We find that Dr. White spoke on "Care of the Sick and Home Sanitation" at a
meeting and Hon. J. W. Parmley spoke on "The Yellowstone Trail". Other
professional and business men were guest speakers.

In 1917, the club entertained the District Convention, May 16-17, and the
Congregational Ladies Aid served the banquet at fifty cents a plate. The 1925
convention was also entertained here, as well as the 1949, and the 1958
convention will be held here in the fall.

In 1930, these ladies were preparing papers on such subjects as, "Are we giving
Japan a square deal?" "is Japan a menace to world peace?" "How about
Hydro-Electric Power on the Missouri?" At one meeting Ada Meadows told of her
travels in Palestine and Europe.

The Club suspended meeting during both wars, so as to work long hours in the
Red Cross rooms, making hospital supplies. Many kits and boxes of goodies were
sent to service men. Busy hands kept knitting needless clicking constantly at
every  gathering those days.

The members planted and cared for the library park flowers beds and the Zinnia
Garden on Main Street, as well as instigating spring clean-ups and paint-ups
for homes. They held many art exhibits for the public; held annual guests days
and an annual party for the faculty of the school.

Twentieth Century Club pioneered in starting a library in a small room in the
Parmley building, with Mrs. Allen in charge several afternoons a week.

Club members have given financial aid through the years to "The Childrens
Home," "Abbott House," "The Hospital," "Holiday Food and Gift Boxes," "Care,"
"Voice of America," "Books for Europe," "Korean Orphans," "Music and Art
scholarships," and all the various Red Cross and Health drives.

Our local Progress Club was sponsored by Twentieth Century several years ago.
They have proved to be a splendid help in our present-day community affairs. We
are very proud of them.

The present members of T. C. C. feel a deep respect and reverence when we
remember the great accomplishments of our valiant women of bygone years. Our
years spent in club work have added vital hours to our lives and we sincerely
hope much good to the Ipswich Community. To quote from a poet:

        "Coming together is a beginning,
        Keeping together is progress,
        Working together is success."
                Emily C. Jones

Commercial Club

A meeting of the businessmen of Ipswich, South Dakota was held at the C & C
Cafe March 21, 1933 with L. J. Thomas acting as Chairman. At this meeting it
was decided that a Commercial Club be organized for The City of Ipswich. In the
meeting that followed, a constitution was drawn up and the  officers elected to
take charge of the newly formed Commercial Club were: H. J. Bebermeyer,
President;  L. B. Cronholm, Vice President and E. L. Engler,
Secretary-Treasurer.

In the earlier days of the Commercial Club, much time and effort was spent in
obtaining new and better roads throughout Edmunds County especially U. S.
Highway No. 12 and a road north from Ipswich to the Edmunds-McPherson County
line.

Much time was also spent in the possibilities of building a new Edmunds County
Court House in the City of Ipswich. Mina Dam and the Scatterwood Lake projects
were also among the many hard working projects undertaken by the Commercial
Club. The original name, Mina Dam was later changed to Lake Parmley through the
efforts of the Commercial Club.

Many other programs were sponsored by the Commercial Club, some of these are
still sponsored by the Club today, such as 4-H, 4-H Achievement Day, Boys State
expense, Gala Day prizes, Farmers Night, children's Christmas party, Marble
tournament, skating rink, swimming pool, park facilities, Boys Scouts, Baseball
and softball fields.

Much effort was also put into the possibilities of having an airport in
Ipswich, and one time some 80 acres of land was obtained for this purpose.

After much work and many meetings with the County Board of Commissioners and
State and Federal officers, the new Edmunds County Court House dedication was
set to be on June 5, 1935 and at a later date a motorcade went to McLaughlin,
South Dakota to celebrate the oiling of U. S. Highway No. 12 through Edmunds
County on September 9, 1938.

Much time and effort was also spent in setting up such programs as Trade Day,
Turkey day, Big Hat Day, Harvest Festival and having an annual high school
athletic banquet.

At a meeting of February 14, 1939 it was  decided that a Trail Day celebration
be held and it be made an annual affair.

Back through the years, the Commercial Club sponsored many other very
worthwhile programs such as the NRA program, and the AAA Marketing program,
Community picnics, Municipal sales, Bond drives throughout the war years and
following these, the Ipswich Community Hospital, Oiled streets, better street
lighting, addition to school and 4-H Club Building.

Books Written by Former Ipswich Residents

"In the Midst of the Years" was written by John William Arbuckle who was
principal of the public school in Ipswich, South Dakota for two years about
1888 and 1889. He later made his home in Waterloo, Iowa.

In all of his classes was William Harlow Briggs, author of, "Dakota In The
Morning" who at thirty years of age filled an editorial chair in the house of
Harpers in New York City. His parents came to Dakota Territory in 1883 where
his father, Judge H. C. Briggs was an eminent lawyer of Edmunds County. Another
son of Judge and Mrs. Briggs became the manager of a leading journal in the
city of Los Angeles and later the postmaster of that city. His name was Henry
Richmond Briggs.

"Higher Than the Sky", was written by Frances Cranmer Greenman who lived in
Ipswich, and whose parents were married in Edmunds County after her mother Emma
A. Goodell gave final proof of a claim located at NE 1/4 Section 21. She also
became state president of the Women's Temperance Union. Her father Simeon
Harris Cranmer was a close friend of Walt Whitman, and active in affairs of the
Populist Party, one of the early liberal political organizations.  Besides
writing her autobiography Frances Cranmer Greenman is an artist of renown, and
has painted portraits of many famous people. Their home while in Ipswich was
what is now known as "Kingdom Hall".

"The Land They Possessed" was published under the pseudonym "Mary Worthy
Breneman which is a mother daughter combination. The "Mary Worthy" is the
mother part and the "Breneman" is the daughter.  Mary Worthy lived 17 miles
north of Ipswich for a short time before moving to Eureka. Her father Joseph
Worthy had an office in Ipswich where he sold farm machinery and drove the 17
miles from his farm home in Ipswich every day.

Melba Blunt Hard now of Brainard, Minnesota is the author of several books of
poems. She  attended the Ipswich schools and is a member of the Minnesota and
South Dakota State Poetry Society. She has also written magazine articles and
songs.

"Three Grandmothers Flew South" by Flossie Briley is a children's book about
her trip south with her two sisters, Belle and Dewey, all of whom lived in
Ipswich with their parents, brother, Ward and  sister Lottie McCafferty. Their
father was an early days sheriff. Mrs. Briley has been a school teacher,
librarian and newspaper reporter. She now resides in Dell Rapids, South Dakota.

"Day Dreams and Realities" was written by E. B. TreFethren in 1896. He was the
Congregational minister in Ipswich from 1899-1905.

        Ipswich Public Library

The Ladies Library Association of Ipswich was organized on May 23, 1886. No
records are available for the first nine years as they were destroyed in a fire
September 19, 1895. A reading room was located in a small building where
Herrick and Jackson warehouse now stands and was in charge of Charles Kinney.

The record of the first meeting that we have was November 4, 1896 with Mrs. Joe
Brown as President. Mesdames Chubbuck, Wing, Huntley, Daly, Arnold, Brown and
Gertrude Williams were noted as being present.

Some time between September 21 and November 4, 1898 another disastrous fire
occurred and through the heroic efforts of Blanche Barker, Laura Wing and
Gertrude Beebe, seventy-five books were saved.

Mention is made twice of writing Mr. Carnegie regarding a donation and a
Carnegie Library for Ipswich. Nothing came of it.

On May 1, 1901, Miss Jessie Crissman was elected to the position of librarian.
In November of that same year Miss Crissman resigned and Miss Helen Smith was
elected and at the August 2, 1905 meeting, Miss Genevieve Tate was elected
librarian. Later Mrs. Allen was elected and the present librarian is Lillian
Witz.

The minutes of the meeting of May 7, 1919 show that the city had taken over the
library and on May 5, 1920 the association was disbanded.

The present library building was erected by the heirs of Marcus P. Beebe as a
memorial to their father. The architecture resembles an old English home
adapted for library use with the large windows. This library was dedicated
August 21, 1931. Governor Warren E. Green gave the greetings from the State of
South Dakota, Miss Leora Lewis, State librarian at that time and the Hon. Doane
Robinson, president of the State Historical Society were here and spoke as did
several local people. The Ipswich High School Band preformed at this dedication.

The interior of the library is one large room with beam ceiling and houses over
9000 books. This collection includes encyclopedias, philosophy,  psychology,
religion, sociology, language, dictionaries, science, useful arts, fine arts,
literature, poems, dram, essays, history, biography, fiction and books for
children.

The very fine electric grandfather's clock was donated to the library by the
members of the Ladies Library Association. Although most of the original
members of the association, which was started in 1886, have passed on; their
dream of a library was more than fulfilled.

The present library trustees are Mesdames Frank Archer, Willard Brown, Herbert
Crissman, and Foster Pierce and Mr. W. K. Herrick.

Many are interested in the Prayer Rock on the south side of the entrance walk.
This five ton boulder had laid on a farm south of Mobridge for as long as anyone
can remember. Before the white men came, a deep path had been worn to this
rock, these early travelers made two sets of hand prints into the face of the
rock. The Prayer Rock is placed with the same incline, just as it was in its
original position overlooking the Missouri River.

The library grounds as well as the adjacent park have ornamental rocks at
corners and marking pathways which are petrified wood chunks, specimens of
glacial erosion, etc.

The basement floor of the Memorial Library is partly used for a county museum.
The museum was started during the depression years of 1932 and 1933 as a WPA
project, with exhibits obtained either by gift or loan from residents of
Edmunds county.

Here can be found relics showing the geologic history of the area as well as
the history of the county from pioneer days. The museum is now financed by the
city of Ipswich and some of the older exhibits were this past year repaired and
restored where necessary by a project which used the talents of high school
students, who also act as caretakers during the summer vacation period when
tourist travel shows an interest.

Fire Department

        This fire wagon sold by Frank Stanford of Ipswich in 1884, the first of
        its kind in Dakota Territory.

Early boom towns with their wooden buildings usually proved good material for
destructive fires. The result of such hazardous conditions was that the town of
Ipswich suffered several such destructive fires which wiped out a large part of
the business section.

As early as 1886 or '87 the town had sunk an artesian well in the block south
of Biegler Motors, because the well had great pressure, hopes were entertained
that the city there had fighting facilities. A pipe line was laid east of the
well and then north reaching into the business section. To this pipe a hose
could be attached which afforded what appeared to be a fair stream with
considerable force.

All went well for a few years but then the well ceased to function and the city
again was forced to go back to the former bucket brigade method.

During those days two very destructive fires occurred. The first wiped out all
the buildings from the present drug store south on the west side of the street.
The only buildings remaining were the drug store and the building which is
south of the drug store. On the east side it took all the buildings from the
First State Bank down to the last building in the block then occupied by
"Frenchie" Bocket's restaurant. Some time later the blocks north of these two
formerly destroyed were practically wiped out.

The city did not have relief from this situation until after the city sunk
another artesian well which gave a copious water supply. After that men took
heart and concluded that it was necessary that a fire company should be
organized for the purpose of having fire drills.

It was April 7, 1911 when a group of men met and formed the first organization.
Other men joined between the initial organization meeting and the next regular
meeting. Thus this was the beginning of a real fire fighting aggregation that
has continued down to the present day.

At the first organization meeting the following officers were elected: Chief,
W. E. Herrick; Vice- chief, L. E. Smith; Captain, Nick Kasel; Foreman, L. E.
Bigger and Roy Smith; Secretary and Treasurer, H. E. Beebe; Trustees for 3
years, C. L. Jackson, and 2 years, A. L. Jones, for I year, Geo. McEachran. In
the years that followed the fireman took active steps to organize the company
so that movements in case of fire would be properly coordinated so that
effective fire fighting could be established. They also began to add additional
equipment, which included a electric fire alarm system.

The first complete record of a fire being put out by the department occurred on
Jan. 14, 1912. The fire was in the Catholic parsonage. It was discovered at 1:
a. m., by Father Borman. He rang the church bell to give the alarm. Fire
apparatus arrived at 1:05 and by 1:30 a. m. the fire had been extinguished.

In 1923 a new truck was purchased and used for many years. It is still
available for service should the occasion arise. The latest truck was purchased
in 1950. This all purpose truck has the latest equipment  for fighting farm or
city fires. The present chief is Don C. Herrick.

        Ipswich Fire Truck

Ipswich Public Schools

        Old Brick School House. Built in 1886.

Before the town was even platted, a little girl named May Dibble began her
meager schooling in a sod home on the empty prairie. In the winter of 1883, she
was the first white child in the new settlement.

That fall, August 27, 1883, the first term of school was taught by Charles T.
Brown from Norwich, N. Y., who received his first license from J. W. Parmley,
who was the first County Superintendent. According to reports the first schools
were held over Parsons Hardware or Marshalls Store.

When the three settlements of Georgetown, Edmunds and Freeport moved together
to the railroad and became Ipswich, the town was platted in 1884. At once the
question of a school became uppermost. We find records stating that three
schools were started  with competent teachers. It seems a Miss Buchanan taught
south of the tracks and Emma Rice and Mrs. A. B. Allen taught north of the
tracks. In 1885-86 school was held in the same school houses, with a Prof. 0.
J. Trace and Augusta True as teachers. These two schools are still part of the
former homes of Ed Lass on Main Street and Victor Strom, east of the bank.

In 1886, the old brick school was built with Prof. Narregant, Emma Rice and
Miss Buchanan as the first teachers. For many years there were no buildings
north or west of the school. The playground was a big, glorious cow pasture
where the children drowned out gophers, picked flowers and played 11 pump, pump
pull-a-way" at recess. This old brick building was finally torn down in 1952,
as the walls were in danger of crumbling.

When Edmunds County was organized in 1883, the Commissioners appointed J. W.
Parmley as the first Superintendent of Schools. He was asked to plat school
townships, which the settlers would later give names. Ipswich Township was
named for the home town in England of Charles Prior, Superintendent of C. M.
and St. Paul Railroad.

General Beadle, the first State Superintendent of Schools sponsored a law
concerning the use of school lands which have benefited past and present
generations.

        Old School with addition built in 1909

As Ipswich grew, with more and more settlers the first school was getting
crowded, and there was need for some higher education. So the east half of the
present north building was erected to care for the first six grades downstairs,
the seventh and eighth grades upstairs, with a small highschool beginning
upstairs. Thru many years Miss Jeanette Lewis taught seventh and eighth grades
in this room and a splendid teacher she was.

Miss Ava Hudson was the first graduate of a high school in Ipswich in 1906. In
1907, Mabel Moritz, Genevieve Hubbard, Florence McCackern and Fannie Jacquith
graduated from the first four year course taught by Charles Paul who later
married May Picton.

The curriculum was: Geometry; Algebra; Latin, 4 years; English, 4 years;
Physics; American History; English History; Bookkeeping; Physical Geography. No
modern frills in those days.

In 1912, the first Boy Scout troop was organized with eight boys enrolled and
named Wolf Patrol.

A large class of eight was graduated several years later, about 1909, and from
then on the high school classes grew larger until today there are often fifty
in the senior class, and a total highschool enrollment of close to two hundred.
The present grade enrollment is nearly one hundred. In the years before 1920
the grades often had from thirty to forty pupils in a room taught by one
teacher.

In 1920-21, a fine modern building costing about $100,000 was erected south of
the old brick school, which was abandoned for many years until in the depression
thirties it was used as a dormitory and lunchroom for a brief time. The new
school took care of eight grades, a four year highschool, a gymnasium, a large
assembly, superintendent's office and lounge.

During the succeeding years Home Economics, Commercial and Music were gradually
introduced in the curriculum and after World War I foreign languages were
taught less often, as well as some higher mathematics classes.

About 1923, a commercial course was started with Mrs. Thielen as the first
teacher. This course has proved very valuable as it has enabled so many
graduates to find work without further training. It has also helped students
find parttime jobs in college as well.

For a time, a teacher training course was also offered, but it proved to be of
brief duration as its value was questionable. Practice teaching could not be
successfully supervised with a small faculty. With a fine normal school in
Aberdeen, it was thought to be a duplication of effort and so discontinued.

Some Home Economics classes in sewing were taught by Elsie Theil, then the
seventh and eighth grade teacher. This was before the Smith-Hughes System with
state aid was established in the highschool.

Organized athletics was given more attention during the years of Paul Selby's
tenure, and ever since has had increasing interest shown in its activities. The
programs in football, basketball and track have produced some championship
teams and individuals. They have helped fill our bulging trophy cases in the
front hallway.

        Ipswich Public School 1920

For several years the athletic program seemed to crowd out any serious efforts
in music, debate and dramatics. Then interested parents wanted to give a more
varied activity schedule for more students to participate in as not all cared
for athletics.

In the succeeding years, a state championship orchestra was developed largely
through the inspired efforts of Mr. Roy DeNomme. The band also became a
splendid organization, which with all the music department, brought back a big
share of medals and plaques throughout many years and contests.

The large vocal groups attained their fine perfection under the infinite
patience of Mr. E. C. Coddington. They never missed bringing back to Ipswich
the highest honors and most points. Our athletic field is named Coddington
Field because of his fine work.

We should mention the hard work of the mothers in sewing white surplices for
the first years, and later providing, with the generous help of the whole
community, the lovely gold and maroon gowns for the singing groups and the
handsome wool uniforms and caps for the band.

The music department has indeed been a fine investment. It has opened a door to
the beauty in music and has given the joy of accomplishment to many a young
musician.

The declamatory and dramatic classes have also given much pleasure to the
community and also creative satisfaction to the student. Our school produced
many excellent speakers, debaters and orators who brought back numerous medals
from county and district competition.

Here we would like to give honor to some of the early board members who served
long terms: John Smith, Q. W. Hutchinson, John Owens and Edward Jackson, Mrs.
Gilbert Jones and two clerks served long terms, Mabel Moritz and Albert Strom.

In 1935, a disastrous fire ruined a large part of the east half of the new
building. It occurred the night before classes were to begin in September.
Until the damage could be repaired, classes were held in churches, the Masonic
hall and library.

The Ipswich school became an independent district in 1920-21, including all of
the township. Buses were used to transport the children and have proved very
safe and convenient. This consolidation has enabled the school to provide an
adequate education for more grade pupils and free tuition for rural high school
students.

Throughout the years from 1936 to the present time, the courses have been
expanded to include a modern Smith-Hughes Agriculture and Home  Economics
course, Industrial Arts, Visual Aids, photography and work in practical
Journalism. The main idea behind this expansion has always been to provide more
students with more opportunities to participate in subjects of particular
interest to them.

In 1956-57 a need was felt for more space, as the grade population was
increasing rapidly. After several mass meetings, the Board of Education was
empowered to remedy the situation. They sold $90,000 worth of bonds, engaged an
architect, let bids to the various contractors and in about a year the building
was ready for use. It took many long hours of hard work by the school
authorities and interested individuals, but the rewards were great.

The $100,000 structure is joined to the main building at the south. It is
strictly modern; housing three grade rooms, a fine large music room with
smaller soundproof practice rooms, a splendid science room and a perfect Home
Economics department. Each has the very best in modern equipment; cork floors,
colorfully decorated walls, oil heat, toilet facilities and fireproof
throughout.

Every year the Board has modernized the lighting, and equipment. New desks,
tables and bookshelves have been built for an adequate library in the high
school. Visual Aid equipment has been purchased and it used in a special room,
as well as office machines and photographic supplies.

A football field has been leveled and seeded and bleachers built.

In 1951-52, a large steel Quonset building expanded agricultural program. This
subject has proved very popular with the boys of highschool age. Many boys, as
well as group judging teams, have earned numerous medals, plaques, cash,
scholarships and interesting trips at various competitions.

The exhibits shown in the spring, by the girls in sewing and the boys in
woodworking and crafts, are proof that they can learn to use their hands as
well as their brains in school.

The scholastic ability of the graduates is proven in the long parade of
successful teachers, lawyers, ministers, doctors, engineers, business men,
druggists, chemists, farmers and not the least, fine homemakers.

Now in the year 1958, Ipswich Independent School District No. 14, can review a
fine record of steady gain for seventy-five years. It has done a fine work for
the surrounding community, and only the future and our memories can measure the
results in the lives of hundreds of students who have passed through its doors.
The teachers, the school board and the parents may feel well repaid for their
loyal support and abiding faith, in this most important endeavor: "Their
School."
        by Emily C. Jones

4-H and Extensions

Extension Work began its first service in Edmunds County in April 1918 when R.
R. Buchanan was installed as an emergency agent. Although records of this early
period are very incomplete, information gained through reliable resources
indicate that the county at this time was rapidly being settled and developed,
and the farsighted men of the county saw the need of an agricultural
organization through which proper guidance in its development could be
maintained.

During the spring of 1919, Mr. Buchanan was called to Roberts County and he was
followed by W. C. Boardman as county agent. Following Mr. Boardman, who went to
Brown County came Dean Davis who continued his work here until 1921. Mr. J.
Carl Laney succeeded Davis from 1921-1923 as the last agent to be employed up
until the drought emergency period of 1933.

Boys' and Girls' Club work, now known as 4-H, had its first beginnings in 1920.
Club Work then consisted of one main project such as Livestock, Corn, Garden,
Canning, Sewing, etc.

Alex Pond, Ipswich, was the first delegate of Edmunds County to National Club
Congress at Chicago in 1924. Alex was then a member of the Triangle Corn Club
under the leadership of Joe Clemens, Ipswich, and County Agent Laney.

During the fall of 1933, Mr. Fred A. Beers was placed in the county as an
emergency agent. Home Extension Work was a new program for the women of Edmunds
County in 1934. Five clubs were organized: Mrs. Carl Krueger, Ipswich, a
continued member, was chairman of Belle-Pembrook Extension Club. Later the name
was changed to the Pioneer. Mrs. Carl Blank, chairman of Merry Bunch Extension
Club, is now an active member again. Others were: Mrs. E. E. Hubbard of Harmony
Club, Mrs. Charles Corliss of the Loyalton Club, and Mrs. Delbert Hodson of
Powell Club which was later changed to the name of the Eastern All Star.

Youth Club Work became active again in 1934. Seven clubs were organized during
the year by Mr. Beers, Emergency Agricultural Agent. These clubs included 4
sewing clubs, 1 potato, 1 corn, and 1 handicraft club.

The first 4-H Achievement Day was held during the summer of 1935, a joint event
with the Women's Program. The first Corn Show was also held that year in the
fall.

Oscar E. Prestegard transferred from Campbell County to Edmunds in 1937 and
served as County Extension Agent up to the time of his death November 14, 1956.

        O. E. Prestegard

Mr. Prestegard will be long remembered by many for his keen interest in youth
activities, home extension work and all activities which benefitted people of
all ages.

An interesting special activity for many 4-H members was the 60-65 piece 4-H
band organized during the summer of 1949 under the leadership of Algie Faris,
band instructor. Uniforms for the band were obtained through popular
subscription. The band played a number of concerts for local events and special
celebrations in the neighboring counties.

        4-H Band

In 1944, Mrs. Jeanne Marie Wahl was appointed as the first Home Demonstration
Agent in Edmunds County.

Mrs. Mickey Bovee came to the county as Home Agent in 1950. Mrs. Bovee left the
county during the spring of 1952 to continue home extension work in her home
county at Rapid City.

During the summer of 1954, Mrs. Eleanore Gruebele, Home Demonstration Agent at
present, came to serve the county. Leonard Nelson, County Extension Agent was
employed at the beginning of 1957.

Extension club members with the assistance of 4-H members of the county
contributed toward the International Farm Youth Exchange Fund for Harlan
Holsing, Ipswich, who was selected as an IFYE delegate to Pakistan in 1955.

Early Day Buffalo Hunt In Edmunds County
Copied from Ipswich Tribune, dated July 31, 1884.

Altho quiet a number of Buffalo have been see in this vicinity it is seldom
that we hear of one being killed, The first one was brought into Ipswich last
Friday, and excited considerable curiosity. On Thursday of last week John Erbe,
who resides on 123-74, discovered one near his house, and finally succeeded in
getting within shooting distance. He sent four rifle (balls) into the beast
before it fell, one of them entering near the heart and another making its way
through the stomach. Mr. Erbe summoned help to dress the monster, and brought a
large portion of the meat to Ipswich, where he found a ready market for it as
twelve and a half cents a pound. Mr. Erbe estimated the buffalo's weight when
shot to be 1800 lbs basing his calculations on a loss of one-fourth in
dressing. From the tip of the nose to the center of the head it measured two
feet, and the horns at the base had a circumference of twelve inches. The head
was the finest we had ever seen, the hair in front being over eight inches long
and very curly. We hear of several other parties who are watching for buffalo
seen in their locality, and another may be expected on our street any day.

(I can vouch for the truth of the above article as I was in Ipswich when the
meat was brought in on a wood rack and I bought a chunk of the meat and helped
eat it. I was acquainted with the Erbe boys, John, Henry and George and August
and when John and I met, the buffalo was mentioned. This was probably the last
wild buffalo killed east of the Missouri river. Geo. C. Meadows.)

The Picture of this Buffalo head was taken by John Doonan, who homesteaded
north west of Ipswich and operated a photograph gallery in Ipswich, D. T.

Prairie Chicken Hunting

Our early day sportsmen had very little difficulty getting prairie chickens as
they were very abundant, and shooting prairie chickens over a good pointer or
setter was considered the tops in sports. The season usually opened Sept 1st
and on that day all the livery teams and shot guns were spoken for far in
advance, many rented shot guns from Engler Hardware and H. R. Roberts, and all
took to the field with guns, dogs and food. Shells in those days cost 65c for
smokeless powder and 50c per box for black powder. Usually every one got more
prairie chickens then they could possibly use and with no ice or refrigeration
one would have to drive about the town and try to give their surplus away.

Duck Hunting

        Early day Hunting scene in Edmunds County

Ducks and Geese were very plentiful and in the early days, the season was open
in the spring about May 1st, however most duck hunting was done in the fall
when the season opened on Sept. 1st. Bold's Lake, about 19 miles N. W. of
Ipswich was one of the favorite spots. Many times hunters would drive up with
tents and spend a couple days shooting and cooking their meals. One such group
of old timers consisted of George Perry, Tom Shaw, Chas. Bostock, Dr.
Auchampach, Frank Tracy, George Meadows, Ed Engler, Dr, Bigger, Bill Swale and
others, they really enjoyed these trips according to the yarns they would spin
about them afterwards. Scatterwood Lake about 26 miles S. E. of Ipswich has
also been a very good spot for ducks and geese in the fall. The north part of
the lake has been placed in a reserve and affords an excellent resting place
for the fall flight but the Game Department has purchased the south part and
this part is Public Shooting Area. Thousands of ducks and hundreds of geese
remain at Scatterwood Lake each fall until the lake is frozen over and many
duck hunters spent many hours in their blinds in the corn field of Edmunds,
Brown and Spink Counties awaiting their arrival with a great deal of
anticipation and a gun full of shells. The fall of 1957 was an excellent one
for mallard shooting in this area.

Pheasants

The Game Department very wisely planted the Chinese Pheasant in the state and
the first open season was in 1919. The first open season in this section of the
state was in Spink County. Our local hunters made many trips to Spink County
where the season in those days opened at sunrise and continued till dark with a
limit of three cock birds. More recently every county in the state has open
seasons, and the limit has ranged from three to ten birds according to the
number of birds available. Of recent years the shooting hours on pheasants has
been from 12:00 noon to sunset. The Pheasant populations runs in cycles
according to the severity of our winters and favorable hatching seasons and I
believe the years of 1944-45 were the peak years, when we had as many as I
00,000 out of state hunters in So. Dak. 1957 was also a very favorable year and
1958 indicates a big increase as we have had another mild winter and have
carried over a large number of birds. The most successful method of hunting
pheasants is to have about two-thirds of your party (maximum hunters in one
party is twelve) walk thru the corn fields while the balance wait at the other
end, this method usually affords good shooting for every one. Some pheasant
hunters, however, prefer to just drive the side roads and shoot the birds that
show up in the ditches. Some days this method pays off rather promptly and then
again one can drive all afternoon and scarcely get a good shot, for as soon as
the season is open a day or two the birds get wise and either fly or run into
heavy cover before the hunter can stop his car and get out to shoot. However,
any method you choose makes wonderful sport and Mr. Pheasant is unsurpassed as a
table decoration.

Deer in Edmunds County

Edmunds County has in deed come into its own as big game territory-Deer having
migrated into our territory for a number of years. The first open season in
Edmunds County was the Friday, Saturday and Sunday after Thanksgiving in 1948.
The hunters were required to use only shot-guns with slugs. We have had three
open seasons and the local hunters seem to have good luck in getting their
deer. Mr. Lewis Smith, our local Game Warden estimates that at present we have
between 300 and 400 deer in Edmunds County, and we pheasant hunters frequently
get quite a thrill out of jumping several deer while on our pheasant drives.

Trail Days

The idea of Trail Day originated with Warren "Everlastinglyatit Put" Putnam, a
traveling hat salesman and South Dakota booster. The Ipswich Commercial Club
sponsored the event and it was called "Trail Day" to commemorate Ipswich as the
"Home of the Yellowstone Trail". The first few years, the business men and
others of the community would publicize the event by means of a "Booster Trip",
traveling to surrounding towns putting on street programs with a small band,
posting bills, and otherwise announcing the coming event. The last few years it
has been expanded to a two day celebration and this year in conjunction with
our 75th anniversary, the most ambitious program ever, is being planned.

The First Trail Day

The city of Ipswich turned the calendar backward a half century and more in the
celebration of "Trail Day" on Monday, June 19, 1939. Promoted and sponsored by
the Ipswich Commercial Club with the idea of bringing back memories of early
day South Dakota history and events the day turned out to be a big event for
old and young. The youth were given an idea of conditions and life when their
grandparents carved out new homes on a virgin prairie and then elder residents
including many pioneers renewed old friendships of many years standing as they
brought up memories of early day life.

Trail Days

The day started with a parade, as near as possible like that of the old days
when horse drawn vehicles were the only means of taking the family on an
excursion. Everyone, Ipswich residents as well as farmers from many nearby
townships, entered into the spirit of the day and the parade was made up of
many wagons, buggies, saddle ponies, etc.

For the free entertainment of the thousands of people who thronged the streets,
the Ipswich Commercial Club obtained the Jay Gould Out-Door Circus. They had a
good, clean show both afternoon and evening along with the usual number of
rides to amuse the youngsters, a large midway and other side shows. The
Commercial Club in its effort to please the many visitors arranged for a large
outdoor dance floor. Music was furnished by the Gould Show organization.

While "Trail Day" had many and varied attractions, the old settlers' program in
the afternoon, as a whole, probably was of the greater significance. Each
pioneer was given a blue badge having printed on it in gilt letters, "Edmunds
County Pioneer."

Traffic on Ipswich Streets was packed, blocks well into the residence section
being packed with cars. Night saw an even larger crowd than in the afternoon,
when it was estimated that 5,000 people jammed the downtown section following
the parade.

Belle Township

Belle township got its name from the school which was named for Belle Slocum,
daughter of an early settler, M. A. Slocum.

Names of the early settlers who were here some time in the year of 1883 are;
John Elliot, M. A. Slocum, Lundbloom, Moore, Grant, Barnsteele, Dewpew,
Brockelhurst, Ole Olson, Daniel Rodman, Fred Blakeman, Chas. Krueger, Dan
Miller, Julian Stratton, Carl Sanborn, Harry Grabler, Albert Krambeal, Jake
Cole, Geo. Hall, Job Gile, Geo. Williams, Garlick, Alice Cabron.

The first telephone was installed on the wire fence from Julian Stratton to
Chas Krueger. The Strattons lived half a mile south and half a mile west of
where Carl Krueger now lives. Later others joined  in on fence lines or a short

Adrian Township

Adrian Township, 124 - 69, is located in the central part of Edmunds County. Ed
Jackson, an early settler, named it "Adrian" for a township in Michigan by that
name. It was organized sometime between 1898 to 1902. Among others who helped
organize the township were Ed Cochran, George Swalve, Carl Sanborn and James
Champlin. There are, of course, others whose names cannot be  recalled, but are
entitled to much credit.

Most of the very early Pioneers have passed on. It is certain that they endured
all the hardships of drought, blizzards, grasshoppers and prairie fires while
toiling to build homes and make a living. Many of them gathered cowchips for
fuel.

The land is quite rolling and there are many rocks in places. It is best suited
for cattle and sheep ranching and today, as in the early days, large herds of
cattle can be seen grazing in the pastures. There are several large sloughs and
springs that provided water for stock, and the grass on hills and valleys was
cut for hay.

Proof on abandoned claims which had never been completed were cancelled and
re-opened for filing in 1899. The following is a list of some of the early
Pioneers:

Huntly Township

Traveling westwardly beyond Ipswich on the Yellowstone Trail for three miles,
we see looking from left to right, a very level stretch of farming land. This
is Huntly Township. To be more precise as to its location we should say, Huntly
Township, Section No. 123, Range 69.

Many of the energetic pioneer families came from our neighboring states, Iowa,
Wisconsin, and Minnesota. Some of the early families were Charles W. Huntlys,
John Wards, Marvin S. Cochrans, J. C. Meyers, John Crawfords and F. Clintons.
Others were J. F. Hardts, J. Wards, Andrew J. Perrions, C. Lovetts, Thomas
Kimmitts, Alexander Curries and Ralph Christians.

In the beginning Huntly and Adrian were one township. They became a Civil
Township, Section No. 123 - 124, Range 69 on July 7, 1890. The first board
members were John Meyers, R. H. Christians and John Kimmitt. Later Huntly
became organized. However their township officials served the Huntly Annex as
Adrian was then called. J. C. Jensen served as one of the earliest assessors in
1883.

Looking back into the Deed Records of 1884, Charles W. Huntly purchased the S.
E. quarter of Section 21, Township 123, N. Range 69 W. The price was one dollar
and twenty five cents per acre. This land is now the Paul Hoyle farm. Other
deeds were registered in this same year: Ellen Huntly to Eliza Huntly, John
Ward to Bridget Ward. Chester L. Pratt was the Register of Deeds of the County
of Edmunds, Dakota Territory.

        Cheese Factory. Located six miles west of Ipswich on U. S. 12. About
        1890.

John T. Kimmitt and wife Bridget D. Kimmitt sold the S. E. quarter of Section
21 to The Edmunds County Diary Co. This was in 1884. In 1890 the Cheese Factory
was built on this land. John James was the first cheese maker. Then farmers
bought shares in the enterprise. They brought their milk to the factory and
then in turn they received their just returns. The cheese maker was very wary
of the milk. He'd taste it and if it didn't come up to his standards-it would
go down the drain. The cheese was used by the farmers and also shipped to other
sections of the country. Some pioneers tell that the farmers could take home
the whey and skim milk if they so desired. Others say all was used in the
making. The factory operated for five or more years. Hobort Hilton was a second
cheese maker. Later he was a teacher in one of the Huntly Schools.

Beebe, the town eight miles west of Ipswich has been serving the Huntly people
approximately fifty years. F. E. Clinton, a young man from Minnesota was the
first dealer. This was in 1910. It was a store of general merchandise. This
first store was located on the south side of the tracks. Other business places
in Beebe were, Woodman's Hall, the Post Office, Lumber yard, Blacksmith Shop,
Elevator, Snyder's Cafe and a Cream station. In the early twenties, Mr. and
Mrs. J. F. Hardt became the owners of the store. For the past 16 years Mr. and
Mrs. E. T. Hardt have managed the Super Valu as it is now called. They still
handle general merchandise. The Beebe Elevator is the only other business
operating today.

Marvin S. Cochran was one of the early Huntly School Clerks. Ed Cochran taught
the N. E. Huntly School the fall term of 1902 and spring term of 1903. Some of
the families having children attending at that time were John Kimmitts, Tom
Kimmitts and Marvin Cochrans. The Ed Cochrans came from the Huntly Annex.

Mattie Lovett Hoyle taught the South Beebe School for two terms. This was an
eight month term in 1905 - 1906 also in 1906 - 1907. She held a Second Grade
Certificate and received $40 per month. The A. J. Perrion children attended this
school. The Huntly School Board members were J. C. Meyers, Marvin S. Cochran
and John Crawford. Mrs. Hoyle had done some other teaching in Huntly when she
was seventeen. A friend had suggested that she write the teacher's examination.
She did and received a Third Grade Certificate.

Huntly Township had three schools in operation during the entire early period.

Cleveland Township

In 1884, Mr. Wenceslaus Sindelar and his son, Joe, came to look for some land
for homesteads, found what they liked, in Township 122-Range 69, filed and
built some housing, and returned back to Iowa for the rest of their families.

Mr. Sindelar put an add in the Bohemian paper to let others know about the
opportunity of obtaining government land.

He was not the first one here, there were a few others here when he arrived,
but by 1886, there were many more, about 16 Bohemian families and other
nationalities, too. The first homesteader was George Cleveland.

In 1887 the Township was organized and named for George Cleveland. The first
officers were; Joe Sindelar, town clerk; Joe Markovetz, treasurer; George
Cleveland, Thurlow Beebe, and Ike Morse, townboard supervisors. The first
school board officers, in 1887 were; Vincent Sindelar, school clerk; Joe
Markovetz, school treasure. The first teacher in the winter of 1887 and 1888
was Wm. Abcitt of Powell, then Gab. Oriely, and Miss Jane Jones taught for
several years.

The first few years were hard, breaking of the land was slow, therefore the
tilled acreage was low, but as years went by, more acres were tilled.

In 1889 a draught set in, and as the result, poor crops. There was a very good
crop in 1892 and another poor crop in 1901. In 1911 there was a total failure
and in 1916 there was bad rust on all hard wheat. Again in 1926 there was
almost a failure  and in 1932 a good crop, but no price. Years '33 and '34
almost everyone remembers.

In the beginning some people had pretty hard times, especially those that came
here with very little for fuel. Some burned twisted hay, others picked up
cowchips. As times improved they bought coal. The only means some had for
breaking up land was an oxen-drawn breaking walking plow. To seed, the grain
was scattered by hand or some had a little cyclone seeder. Harvesting was with
a scythe, or a reaper. Thrashing was with a flail by hand. In a few years, the
horse powered threshing machines were introduced and as years went by and times
improved, more horses were put in use, better machinery was bought, and more
and more land was worked. Joe Markovetz and Sindelar's got a horse powered
threshing machine, and used it in the neighborhood for years and with farming
more stock was raised. Between the good crops and the poor crops, the pioneers
prospered. In the early 1894's or 1900's they started building better homes and
farm buildings.

During the first few years, the prairie fires were a hazard. With the prairie's
burned off in the fall, there was nothing to hold snow on the prairie during
the winter and it didn't take very much snow with a good wind to have a
blizzard. That is what happened on January 12th, 1888, the worst blizzard in
the history. Chas, Sindelar, one of the old timers, should remember that
blizzard, as he and his brother John, the Scharf and Morse children with their
teacher Wm. Abcitt, were marooned in the school house all that day and night
until the noon of the next day.

        This farm was homesteaded by Mr. and Mrs. Victor Strom in 1885. This
        house was moved from Edmunds by horses and wagons in 1893. It replaced
        the old sod shanty. It is still used as living quarters for the Bailey
        family.

In March, 1886, Mr. Wenceslas Sindelar was laid to rest in the St. John
Cemetery, the piece of land he had donated for that purpose. Next to be buried
was my Grandmother, Elizabeth Hamak, on December 24th, 1887. May 1st, 1901 Mr.
Frank Simon Sr. was buried, and on that very same day, Ferdinand Stule, 9 year
old son of Mr. and Mrs. Antone Stule, drowned in a slough, while his parents
were attending the Simon funeral.

In the early days, water shortage was a problem. After periods of time when
shallow dug wells and tubelar wells, which were mostly pumped by windmills,
were used, the artesian well came into prominence  and solved the problem.

        Stone house built during Indian scare. Now on Sindelar Farm.

The first telephone line was built in the southern part of the township in
1914, and in the northern part in 1917. March, 1924 was the first time our mail
route came through from Loyalton and about 1918 some farmers put in light
plants. The late 20's we were trying out the wind charger light plants and in
1950 the R. E. A. came through. Now every farmer has electricity with all the
conveniences available. From buffalo paths, and wagon trails, we now have
graded roads and two county graveled roads crisscrossing the township.
        Mrs. Chas. Simon

Liberty Township

People began migrating and settling in what is now known as Liberty Township as
early as 1881 it is said, but 1883 seemed to be the time when majority of
settlers arrived, but it is difficult to ascertain  as to who was the first to
settle in this area. The eastern portion of this area was settled by the Welsh,
the central by the English and the western portion by the Czech's or Bohemians.
While claims may have been filed in 1883 or even earlier, records indicate that
proving up did not take place until in 1886 through 1890's.

A petition from residents of township 121-69 asking for civil township
organization was presented to the County Commissioners and motion was granted.
Said township was declared organized by the name of "Liberty" and the first
town meeting was set at the home of Evan Jenkins, Powell, S. D. on Oct. 23,
1886. No record is available as to the officials elected at this meeting, and
the earliest verifiable record is that of 1891 which indicates that the
following were officers: David A. Stafford, John  Getsman, and William W.
Thomas, Supervisors; C. S. Pool, Assessor. Addresses of all residents were
Powell City, S. D. and Vermont City, S. D. The total valuation was shown as
$9,180 with assessed property listed as follows:
        Horses 79 Sheep 241 Hogs 45
        Cattle 456 Mules 1 Wagons & sleds 42
        Dogs 27 Agricultural tools $1,333
        Household items $515
        48 persons paid road poll, and 38 school poll.
        This is compared to a present valuation of $87,454, 32 horses, 100
sheep 22 dogs, 960 cattle, 4 tractors, and $19,048 other agricultural tools.

The year 1958, marks the beginning of gravelling of township roads.

School organization took place shortly after Liberty was settled, and 3 school
buildings were erected and school sessions were held as early as 1885 as two of
the pupils attending this school still survive, namely Fred Getsman and Grace
Getsman Hutchinson, both of whom now live in Ipswich.

A Miss Lotta Scott was the teacher, and the school building was on the
northwest corner of the NW quarter of Sec. 11 . (This school was later
destroyed by a tornado and was rebuilt on the southeast corner  of this same
quarter.) This school was known as the Getsman school. The two other schools
were located in the southeast part of the township, known as the Viertel School,
which was destroyed by fire in 1908, and the other was located south of the Joe
Kub farm, later known as the Goodale school.

The first school meeting recorded was in 1887, with C. H. Roberts shown as
Treasurer. No record can be found as to the other school board members, until
in the 1890's when Oscar Viertel, Mrs. E. J. Jenkins and Don Goodale were on
the school board and remained in that position for a number of years.

To complete the community life of the settlers, Sunday Schools were organized,
and religious meetings held in the schools. A Rev. Kyle of Ipswich, later a
Senator, organized the Sunday Schools. Attendance was good. The Welsh settlers,
attended Church and Sunday School in Powell City, with many people walking to
and from services. The English settlers attended the Sunday Schools within the
township, while the Bohemian settlers, mostly Catholic, were periodically
ministered to by a Bohemian Priest who came to the Community, or they traveled a
long distance to Ipswich to attend Church.

A Rev. Roberts also helped conduct religious meetings in the area, in the
Dunker or Dunkord religion, which is a German Baptist brethren practice,
similar to those of the Quaker and Mennonites.

        Liberty Belle School

In the 1900's a need was seen for the improvement of the school situation in
the township, and 3 modern buildings were erected and put into use in 1920.
These were named "Liberty Pride" in the southern part of the township, "Liberty
Bell" in the northeastern part, and "Liberty View" in the western part. Due to
the depression and drought of the '30's, many of the young people were forced
to seek a means of livelihood elsewhere, and therefore did not settle in
Liberty township. Consequently, the pupils for our three school diminished and
only one or two of the schools were operated. In 1957 "Liberty Pride" was sold,
and this year, 1958 marks the closing of all our one room school buildings, as
the Liberty School District is now combined with Vermont, and grade school
pupils will be transported to Loyalton.

Liberty township has been an agricultural area, with diversified farming, with
cattle numbers mostly on the increase, thus showing a trend to more ranch type
farming at the present time. It has a tendency towards rough land, with a
number of areas being best suited as natural wildlife development. One
artificial lake, known as the Stafford Dam affords fishing facilities for many
of the county people. Several large natural lakes also afford duck hunting when
moisture conditions are favorable. A great many trees, shelterbelt areas, have
been started in the part five years which add a great deal to this area.

These early settlers endured many hardships, and some left while they still had
the means to leave, because of drought, grasshoppers, prairie fires and hot
winds. Many of the  settlers came with a trade with which they had hoped to
make a living, but due to crop failures, they scarcely eked out a living for
their families. Many were too poor to leave, and it took many years before they
were able to accumulate the necessities with which to earn a living. Many of
these had never known poverty before, thus we owe these early settlers a great

Vermont Township, Loyalton & Vicinity

The wide open space of this great Northwest became known to people in the
Eastern states and also as far as Europe through reports back by the early
explorers, fur traders and missionaries. It beckoned them to come west and make
a home for themselves and their children. There were vast acres of rich prairie
soil, a variety of wild life, big game and fur-bearing animals. The people
craved for the opportunity to make a new home for themselves even if it meant
hardships and sacrifices.

A small group from the state of Vermont set out for the West. They came as far
west as Aberdeen, S. Dak. by train, from there they journeyed further west as
far as what is now known as Vermont  Township and settled one miles west of the
present town of Loyalton.

It took courage to start a new life in those days. The winters were cold and
much snow could be expected. They found themselves busy building sod houses and
preparing for the days ahead.

Good news came around 1884 when they heard that the railroad was going to run a
branch line south of Roscoe. According to the first route that was planned the
railroad would have been five miles east of their settlement. In was changed
though, and came through only one mile east of them. The first daily run was in
April 1891. The first depot agent was a Mr. Dickenson followed by a Mr. Irvin.

The people decided to start a town which was to be located on W 2 of N. E. 4
Sec. 21-121-70. The name chosen was Vermont City. This tract of land was first
owned by Aaron McKinney on Nov. 26, 1886 and sold to John A. Bowman on the same
day.

It was platted by John A. Bowman on April 18, 1887. Was sold to Mary Batchelor
on Nov. 12, 1887. Harney Willey became the first postmaster in 1886 Hosey
Paddlefoot operated a small store in so called Vermont City. A Mr. Allen once
operated a Store on the west side of track 1886 to 1889.

The petition for organization of Vermont Township, for civil purposes, was
granted on July 6, 1886. The first assessor, as records show, was J. E. Sheldon
in 1891. The board members were; W. N. Hutchenson-chairman. George Hancock, and
D. M. Chamberlain. Mr. Hancock was in the mercantile business later. Mr.
Chamberlain lived 1 1/2 mile S. W. and was noted for selling patent medicine.
He lived there until his death around 1914 or 1915.

Benjamin Bowman was the owner of the east half of N. E. 1/4 of 21-121-70, where
the present site of the Town of Loyalton is located.

hiring a teacher. This brought trouble and had to be settled in court at
Faulkton. When the time came to return home, there was no train that day. As
related, the group all walked home together. The name "Hardscrabble" was
applied to the community from this episode and that reference is heard at times
today.

About the time the railroad came through, the homesteaders began to come and
settle on claims, the community grew in population. It is understood, to file
on a claim, you must build a claim shanty and live on the land a certain number
of years before you could claim ownership. Several lost their rights by not
doing so, they would built their shanty and pretend they had lived on the
claim.

Powell Township

With the great flood of immigration into Dakota Territory in the early '80s
came the Welsh colony whose destination was Edmunds County. The colony under
the leadership of William E. Powell, General Immigration Agent of the Chicago
Milwaukee and St. Paul railroad, and consisting of about 300 families arrived
in Aberdeen, March 22, 1883. The original intention of the leader at that time
was to establish the colony on the line westward, and near where Ipswich is now
located. But finding that the larger portion of the land in that vicinity was
already entered, the leaders of the colony moved southward, and located about
10 miles due south of the present county seat.

The first settlers arrived in Powell March 29, 1883 and put up the noted Big
Shanty (Shante Fawr, as the Welshman says it) 20 x 40 ft. frame building on a
claim occupied by the late Hugh Griffith, who was a leading member of the
colony. This was the first frame building erected on the west prairie west of
Aberdeen, and served as both hotel, and church, and meeting place. Here the
settlers dined, slept, and worshipped the God who had brought them to this vast
wilderness. They were all musically talented, some coming directly from Wales.
Among them were coal miners, stone masons, horsemen, carpenters, cooks,
blacksmith, storekeeper, druggist, preachers, school teachers, and the majority
tillers of the soil.

There was a great deal of coming, and going the first summer folks staying in
the Big Shanty until they had built some sort of shelter on their own claims.
Lumber, groceries, everything was hauled that long 36 miles from Aberdeen that
first summer. Shanties were going up on every claim, some tar paper covered,
and others of the sod variety, each having its advantages and presumably its
champions.

        Horses that were killed on the Ed. O. Evans farm by lightning. June
        1911.

On May 16, 1883 a post office was established by the members of the colony, and
Morris Jones chosen postmaster. August 7, 1883 he was duly appointed by the
government as postmaster for the colony. November 1st, of the same year the
township was organized as a school district No. 30 Twp. 121 Reg. 68, and the
first schoolboard members were John J. Reese, Hugh Griffith, and J. D. Jones.
The first school was built in 1884 near where the town hall now is, and Rev. J.
Williams was the first teacher. A meeting was called to find a name for the
township and village at the "Big Shanty". The majority vote-Powell-for the
efficient Immigration Agent William E. Powell, who was the Moses Who led the
host to occupy this fair land. During that winter it became apparent to the
leaders that the townsite was not centrally located, and a vote was taken which
resulted in the removal of the Big Shanty two miles further south, and
establish the center of the town now Powell Township. The Big Shanty was then
sold to Morris Jones, and Daniel Williams who homesteaded on N. E. 1/4 of Sec.
21 gave him a lot to build a house, also postoffice and store on same lot next
to the Methodist Church, the present Presbyterian Church.

On August 13, 1883 the Board of County Commissioners met, and appointed the
following from Powell John H. Hughes for sheriff; John J. Reese for Justice of
Peace. J. Morris was a Notary Public. On August 27, 1887 J. D. Jones was
appointed Constable. There were two members of the early legislature from
Powell, the late Hon. John J. Reese, and Hon. A. H. German. The late Thomas A.
Evans served as County Commissioner from the district. Two sons of the late Rev.
and Mrs. J. T. Lewis were elected to county offices, A. W. Lewis Superintendent
of schools in 1908 and Emrys R. county Auditor in 1922 and is the only one left
of the family, residing at the Masonic Home in Zenith, Washington. The first
death was Nellie Reese, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. John J. Reese in January 1884.
John Richards and Mary Williams is reputed to be the first couple carried at
Ipswich, Edmunds County on April 23 1884 by Judge J. Rice. The first deep well
was dug by J. J. Reese assisted by Hugh Roberts, it was 100 feet deep and
walled with Dakota stones. The first artesian well was drilled on the place now
occupied by the Benjamin Richards family, and it still furnishes the water
supply.

The pioneer homestead of J. J. Reese, now occupied by Mr. and Mrs. Matthew
Evans is the oldest house in the township, and the lumber was hauled from
Aberdeen.

Powell had during its existence these Postmasters and service record: Morris
Jones, August 1883; John T. Llewellyn, November 1886 to August 1887, then Henry
Hodson was appointed, and the postoffice was in his home, the present home of
Mrs. W. M. Hodson, Mr. Hodson and his son W. A. had the appointment until it
was discontinued on November 1, 1908, and the Rural Free Delivery routes
started out of Ipswich with W. S. Barnwell, as our first carrier, followed by
Elmer Lewis, W. Richmond, Charles Colliers and the present carrier Gerald
Taylor.

From these early years there are many interesting things that took place, among
the very interesting characters was Mrs. Seth Lewis who was mid-wife for the
entire community during those years. She presided at the birth of nearly all of
the babies. While none of the babies remember their first meeting it is well
remembered that many an expect ant father rushed to the nearest neighbor to get
the woman to come and stay while he rushed to the Lewis home to bring Mrs.
Lewis. In those days it wasn't uncommon to use a stone-boat for transportation.
There were no fences to speak of so a direct path across the prairie would be
taken to hasten the time.

In the fall of 1904 typhoid fever raised a fearful havoc in the community,
taking the lives of Mrs. Henry Hodson and two sons Charles, and Edward, and
Miss Nellie Williams daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Evan J. Williams. In the spring
of 1907 dyptheria broke out resulting in the death of two young people, Mary,
daughter of Mr. and Mrs. John Richards, and Hannah, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. W.
R. Williams. Here again in the fall of 1918 the Flu epidemic swept through
taking one member of the R. R. Williams family, and three members of the
Richards family. It left its mark of suffering in many homes. Doctor Robert
Hill of Ipswich was the community physician, during these epidemics.

On July 1, 1937, the first day of the fishing season at Hamak's Lake brought a
drowning tragedy which took the lives of four people; Evan Williams, his two
sons, Glen and Kenneth, and Dale Jones, son of Mr. and Mrs. Hugh Jones. There
were five persons in the party, the only survivor, Miss Alice Benton, is a
sister of Raymond Benton.

There was the era of the big Threshing rig with the fireman who got up at 3
o'clock A. M. to fire up. The cook who slept and prepared the meals in a
cookcar had breakfast at 5 A. M., and the men were expected to be in the field
by sun up. Eight or ten bundle wagons hauled bundles with three to four extra
pitchers. The old streamer had to have an engineer and separator man, a water
man and from three to five grain haulers depending on the distance. Noon was a
gala time for everyone except the cooks who had to feed the crew possibly 20 to
30 men. Plowing was done with 5, 6, or 8 horse teams; binding and heading were
methods of handling the grain. We have been advancing each year mechanically
until all work on the farm is done by tractors, engines, and electricity.

On June 2, 1911 a tornado struck this area, and destroyed much of the townsite,
and several farms, schoolhouses, and the church. While no one was killed,
property damage was great. Roland Williams and Mrs. David J. Morris were
seriously injured.

Every quarter section of land that was available for homesteading was taken,
and little "shanties" were built of various sizes, according to the size of the
family. Now Seventy five Years later there are approximately thirty-five farm
dwellings. The second, and third generation of the following  pioneers John
Richards, Henry Hodson, Cassius Pond, F. P. Sinclair, John W. Williams, John P.
Hughes, John B. Evans, Daniel Williams, Owen Roberts, Mrs. Ann Griffith, David
J. Morris, Evan J. Williams, Roland Williams and Robert R. Williams have the
land in their possession and a few occupying the farms. There are very few of
the earliest members left; among worthy of mention, Mrs. Mary Sinclair who
celebrated her 100th birthday in August 19, 1957. Some have moved away, and you
will find many names in the cities of the dead of Powell and Ipswich.

The horse and wagon days, cowtrails, dust roads, kerosene lamps are in the
past, and the wheel of progress goes on, and on. Electricity in every farm,
gravel, and hard surface roads. Anyone can walk into a Super Market and, carry
home your meal in packages ready to eat, what a contrast to the experience of
one of the earliest settler, Richard Theophillis who asked the postmaster if a
one cent stamp would take a letter to his wife back in London, England. It was
all he had so the postmaster paid the rest of the postage. This man lived in a
small shanty without a floor in it. He dug a hole in the ground, and put straw
in it for a bed. He covered his shanty on the outside with snow, and lived on
boiled wheat, and a 49 pound sack of flour. This was not the only case of this
kind. This much for the first 75 years, what will be said of the next 75????
        Jennie Griffith

Union Township

Union Township settlement by homesteaders began in 1883. By 1885 there were
from two to three on every section. The scarcity of water caused many to leave
until there were only thirty, more or less, in 1901.

Mr. and Mrs. F. Drager and Agusta, Charley, Emil, Herman and Edward who came in
1883, Mr. and Mrs. A. W. Olson and Arthur in 1889 and Hans Tobiason in 1893 and
the Royles were early settlers who established permanent homes here.

The Olson home is the only one that is still owned and operated by a
descendant; Albert Olson, a grandson and his family.

Edward Drager lived upon old homestead fifty years or more and Hans Tobiason
lived upon theirs until the family was grown up and Mrs. Tobiason (nee Agusta
Drager) had passed away.

A cyclone destroyed all the buildings except a granary on the Tobiason home in
1902. Their oldest child, Helen was killed and the baby then (Mrs. Ida Lewis)
was badly cut and the mother began losing her hearing and lost it entirely in a
short time after the injury received by the storm.

Emil Drager of Ipswich and Arthur Olson of Aberdeen are the only two of the
first families now living.

Fred Drager, now in Aberdeen was born a year or more after they came to Union
so is one of, if not the first born in Union.

In 1866 a church was built 1 mile north of the Center on a ten acres plot given
for the church and a cemetery by the Reeves family, who homesteaded that
quarter in 1883.

Mrs. Reeves was Sunday School superintendent there for over twenty-five years.
They drove out to attend church for years after moving to Ipswich. The church
building was moved to Java in 1936 or 37.

There were two school buildings, the south one on section 25 and the north one
upon section 9. They should have been upon skids the way that they moved to
places more convenient. The north one finally was the N. W. Union and the south
one the S. E. Union.

Anna Mundt (Mrs. L. J. Robertson of Aberdeen) taught in the north building in
1891. Her pupils Arthur Olson, Mollie De Lapp (Reeves) Frand, Nellie and George
Moule. Two Nelson and two Standard children (unable to learn their names).

Jennie J. Jones taught in Union in 1893. She took a cook-car to live in by the
side of her school and an early teacher did that at the south school so I think
it must have been she.

Lillie White taught in the very early days and is the first teacher Emil Drager
remembers.

Charles Moule was the treasurer in 1884. More settlers began moving into Union
about 1904 and before 1908 two more school houses were added, until about 1940,
all four were used. Then the children were married and living some other place
and some families had left until one building was plenty so the S. W. school
house was moved to with in one mile of the center of the township. A few years
ago more room was needed so the N. E. school house was moved one half mile west
and one mile north. And the N. W. building was torn down to build an addition
on to Center School and the other old, old, school building was sold and is now
a granary upon the Harris Swanson farm.

Knut Lee who homesteaded, where the
Alva Wolter's home now is, had the first artesian well drilled in Union and it

Now in 1958 there are twenty-five homes consisting of one hundred three

descendants of the original owners. Theodore Behselich, S. W. Mount, Nick
Heinz, and Henry Swanson. Mrs. S. W. Mount is still living in the home place.

History of Harmony Township

The pioneers of Harmony Township came early in the spring of 1883. The majority
of the people came from Wisconsin by rail as far as Aberdeen. Wagons, drawn by
oxen and horses, were used for the rest of the journey west. Harmony was
settled by a variety of nationalities, Welsh, German, Irish, English and
others. The summer of 1883 was a busy one with breaking a little sod, planting
small patches for food, building a shanty, gathering fuel and locating water
holes.

On October 7, 1883 a son was born to Martin and Theresa Blank in Harmony
Township, Section 19. They named the baby Martin and he was the first white boy
born in Harmony, the second in the County. Martin presently lives in Ipswich. A
very early letter written by Theresa Blank, tells how serious the water
shortage was until an underground spring was located on Section 7. Pioneers
hauled the water from this well for ten to twelve miles around.

On February 4, 1884 a petition was signed by 23 names asking that township 122,
Range 68, be organized as a civil township and that the name be Harmony. The
petition was granted on February 19, 1884 and the first town meeting was held
at the house of Mr. K. Kirk, N. E. of Section 15. The very earliest families
included Martin Blank, Albert Smith, D. C. Pierce, George H, Loomis, Fred
Johnson, Ezra Drew, Job Jillson, M. W. Kirk, George McEachran, Tom Doonan, Dow
E. Lewis, and others. Mr. and Mrs. John P. Hughes, parents of Curtis Hughes,
lived a number of years on the farm now owned by Tony Streit. Later, this
family moved south into Powell Township.

The first assessor book of 1891, shows the family names of Ira Dibble, T. L,
Davis, T. M. Callahan, I. W. Chase, D. D. Jones, Peter J. Evans, Lee and Roll
Loomis, Thomas Evans, John Griffith, Owen and Wm. Rowlands, Charles E. White,
Jane Jones, and others. The Charles E. White family settled on Section 14. Dr.
Walter White, a son, practiced medicine many years in Ipswich. Lilla White
taught school several years in the township. Several living in this area today
can tell about the unforgettable character of Thomas Evans, known as
"Tom-Fifty". This Welsh pioneer was very popular with all the children,
teaching them Welsh songs, games and stories. He was a bachelor and had a
shanty south of the Pfaff farm.

Harmony Township grew very fast the first twenty-five years. Township expenses
were mostly for organization, paying the clerk, supervisors, and overseers;
road and bridge work; plowing fire guards; erecting and maintaining guide
posts. $1.50 an acre was allowed for plowing fire guards, the supervisors
received $2.00 a day, the town clerk $1.50, and the Assessor $3.00. The town
treasurer was paid a salary of $15.00 per year. In 1897, the total township
expense was $156.15, in 1898 it was $124.89, and in 1899 it was $147.89. From
the old minute book, the following was recorded; June 7, 1897: T. M. Callahan
gave the report that the Board could not compel the land owner to fence the
open wells. If it was done, the town would have to meet the expense.

Family names kept changing as new ones moves' into the township, sons and
daughters began taking the places of the older ones, and many became
discouraged and went back east. From 1903 on, the family names of John Pfaff,
Joseph Brady, Peter Morgan, J. C. Stolsig, R. R. Buikema, Peter Lass, J. P.
Jones, Peter Geditz, A. J. Leech, Aaron Gsell, the Streit Brothers, H. B.
Engel, E. E. Hubbard, Ed D. Jones, Boon Flesher, Tom Shew and others appear in
the records.

During the 1930's, when one dry year came after another and Harmony Township
was a regular Dust Bowl, as was the rest of the County, the Public Works
Administration came to our aid and constructed the Blank Dam, which is located
on Section 7. All local labor was used. This gave the people some income to
tide them over. When the Dam was completed it was stocked with fish. For a
time, it was quite an attraction, for the ones that liked to fish.

The present township officials are: Supervisors, Bert Ertz; chairman, Matt
Morgan, Cas Helgerson, Mike Schumacher is Treasurer, Andrew Kraft is Assessor;
Peter Geditz, Jr. is clerk.

The present school officers are: Allen Roberts, chairman; Reuben Oschner,
Treasurer; and W. Wietgrephe, Jr. Clerk.

There are two rural schools operating in the township today. Mrs. Harold
Markuson is the teacher in the southwest school and Mrs, Albert Kadlec is the
teacher in the Center School.
        Roy Engel

Harmony Center School

Harmony Center School is a standing landmark. Built in the early 1880's, it
still stands and is in use today. In the fall of 1883, the County Board
designated Harmony School District as No. 19. The first judges were F. M.
Wright, M. K. Kirk and Ezra Drew. Albert Smith was the first treasurer. The
district received their first apportionment of the General funds of $55.22 in
April of 1884. At one time, there were 21 boys and 1 girl attending school
here. The girl was Jennie McEachran, third from the left in the picture.

Fountain Township

Reminiscence relative to Fountain township, 123, 67 in Edmunds County South
Dakota.

I, the undersigned, will endeavor to relate and describe in a humble and
unbiased way, and to the best of my ability, as to the pioneer owners of farms,
and present owners and operators of above township from 1883 to present time of
celebration of the seventy-fifth Diamond Anniversary of the city of Ipswich on
the ninth and tenth of June of Nineteen fifty eight (1958).

In enumerating owners and operators, I will also incorporate, any state,
county, or township offices in which capacity they have served.

I will commence in the Northeast corner of the township and continue in
rotation pertaining to farmsteads ... Michael Fischer, previous owner of
Northwest of section 3, has served as township supervisor and treasurer of
school board. Present operator is his son Wendlin J. Fischer.

Anton Fischer, previous owner of Northeast quarter of section 4. Present
operator is Jerome Adler. He is the present school treasurer, and a director of
Craven Elevator company.

Casper Burgod and Son present owner and operator of Northwest of section 8,
123, 67. He has served as a township supervisor, and at present is a director
of Farmers Elevator of Craven. He is also a director of the Ipswich
Co-operative Association, and Ipswich Super Market Grocery Store.

Fred W. Busch was previous owner of Southeast section 8 in 1900. This is now
owned by Mrs. John Schumacher and operated by her two sons Adam and Paul
Schumacher.

Sylvester Usselman, previous owner of Northwest of Section 11, now owned and
operated by Ludwig Usselman. Sylvester Usselman had served as township
supervisor in 1900.

Adam Dosch, previous owner of Southwest of Section 12, now owned and operated
by his son Benhardt Kesseler. Wendelin Kesseler also served as a township
supervisor.

Daniel Lacher, previous owner of Northeast of Section 15, 1893. He served as a
school board officer for ten years, township assessor for five years, chairman
of Liberty Bond Drive during World War One, and director of Farmers Elevator of
Craven. Present owner is Jos D. Lacher and Sons. Jos D. Lacher has served as
school board officer, township assessor, director of Farmers Elevator of
Craven, director of Ipswich Co-operative Association for twenty-three years, and
Co-op Grocery Store for eight years.

Pius Gauer is the present owner of Northwest of Section 18. He has served for
many years as a township officer. The previous owner was the late Lester Jones,
who also served for many years as a township and school officer.

D. D. Jones, previous owner of Southwest of Section 19, is now owned by John W.
Dosch and operated by Emil Hettich.

C. H. Rebrud, previous owner of Northeast of section 20 in 1883. He had served
as State Representative, County Commissioner, and township officer, school and
civil. Northwest of Section 20 is now owned by William L. Engle, and operated
by his son Richard Engle.

Sebastian Baer, previous owner of Northeast of Section 22 in 1901. This is now
owned and operated by Andrew F. Braun and Sons. Andrew F. Braun has served as
school and township officer for several years, and was also a director of the
Farmers Elevator of Craven. In 1921 Andrew F. Braun built a new home on the
Southeast quarter of section 15, which is now operated by his sons Joe D.
Braun, and Richard C. Braun.

Joseph Gauer, previous owner of Southeast of Section 22, was a township officer
for many years and a director of the Farmers Elevator of Craven. Southeast 22
is now owned and operated by Jacob J. Gauer and his Sons.

Wendelin Dosch, Sr., previous owner of Northeast 24, present owner and operator
is his son, Mike Dosch. Wendelin Dosch Sr., the previous owner in 1888 was a
very active board member of civil and school affairs, and was also a director
of the Farmers Elevator of Mina.

Frank Braun, previous owner of Southwest of Section 26 in 1905 was a blacksmith
by trade and carried on custom work along with his farming operations.
Southwest of Section 26 is now owned and operated by August Braun and Sons,
(Joseph A. Braun and Jack Braun.)

George C. Meadows, one time owner of Section 30 and later acquired by J. F.
Hales in 1908 who operated it at that time. Present owner and operator is his
son, O. R. Hales. O. R. Hales also held school and township offices and was a
township assessor.

Joseph Ertz, previous owner of Northwest 32, now owned and operated by his son,
George Ertz. Joseph Ertz served as township officer, civil and school, and was
a director of the Farmers Elevator of Craven for several years. George Ertz
also is a very active member in the township, serving as civil and school
officer, and a director of Farmers, Elevator of Craven at present.

A. H. Miller, previous owner of Northeast of section 33 in 1883 had been a
county commissioner of Edmunds County and an active member in the township,
civil and school.

Broder Peteren, previous owner of Southwest 33, was a township officer and also
served as township treasurer. Present owner and operator is Lloyd Larson, who
is a present director of the Farmers Elevator of Craven, and a township school
officer.

Adam Braun, previous owner of Northeast 34, now owned by Lamont and operated by
Harold Artz who at present is township clerk.

I will now proceed to summarize to the best of my recollection and ability as
follows: First I will mention farmsteads, which have disappeared and subsequent
improvements. Southeast quarter of section 20 once known was the Jim Jackson
farm. He operated said farm since 1890, and was a breeder of trotting horses
and a very extensive farm operator. He vacated in 1902 and moved to Bottineau,
North Dakota.

The C. P. Blunt farm, now vacant, is located in southeast of Section 28 and is
now owned by Oscar Schumacher. Speaking relative to Oscar Schumacher, he is the
present owner of the Craven Corners, located on the Northwest corner of
Northwest quarter of Section 28 which was constructed in 1950.

The Craven Farmers Elevator Company was acquired by the farmers from Charlie
Brady who built the original elevator in 1909. At a latter date the farmers
acquired it. In 1930 this elevator structure burned down and was replaced with
the present structure. Worthy of mention is Edward J. Heiser who was manager of
the Farmers Elevator of Craven from 1932 to 1950.

A recollection relative to Craven, South Dakota: In 1912, Craven had the
following business establishments: grocery store, lumber yards, blacksmith
shop, and last but not least, also a saloon. In relative to the saloon, in 1917
a state law on municipal ordinance of the city, a saloon had to be over five
miles away from a dry or temperance city. So the saloon at Craven was moved one
mile east and a new town site was established and called Baerville. Baerville
only lasted four months. The saloon and town of Baerville vanished and people
had to quench their thirst with water and non-alcoholic beverages. Also a few
more words about the store at Craven. This was a two story structure built by
Henry Fauss, and Sons, William and Henry, the later now a resident of Aberdeen.
The lower story of the structure was used for general merchandize, living
quarters, and the Craven Post Office. The upper story was used as a meeting
center and dance hall, where the writer alleviated many a dull moment.

Referring to an old land mark now situated on the Southwest quarter of section
9: Shortly after 1880 a reservoir was constructed covering an area of fifteen
acres. This reservoir is almost a perfect circle. The wall around the reservoir
through the center was about sixteen feet deep. The object and intention were
to irrigate the entire section 9. An artesian well of six inches was sunk to
fill the reservoir, and furnish water for irrigation purposes. The well lived
only a short life, went bad, and the entire anticipation developed into a
failure. The proprietor, Washington Lemnaster according to records, became
insolvent and the property went into the hands of receivers.

Now a brief recollection as to the Fountain Township school system Number 15:
Miss Jeanette Lewis was county superintendent of School from 1902 to 1906. At
one time Fountain Township had four schools in operation, located as follows:
Northeast school, located on Northeast quarter of Section 10, Northwest school,
located on Southeast quarter of Section 8 Southwest school, located on
Northwest quarter of Section 33 and Southeast school located on Southeast
quarter of Section 15. (Fountain Township, 123, 67)

Relative to teachers of above schools: First Northeast school built in 1901 and
taught by Miss Catherine Hirch. This is where the writer started his
preliminary education. Northwest school was constructed in 1905 on the
Northeast quarter of Section 18 in 1910. Later it was moved to its present
location (Southeast quarter of Section 8). The first teacher of this school was
Caroline Rebrud, now Mrs. William Engle. As to the Southwest school, the writer
has no recollection. The Southeast school was built in 1906, and the first
teacher was a Mr. Taylor. The teacher succeeding him was Lilly Jones. Of the
four schools in discussion, only the Northeast school is still in operation at
present. The Southwest  school is closed at the present time. The other two
schools (Northwest school and Southeast school) are no more; they were sold and
vacated.

A fond recollection on January 6th nineteen hundred and two (1902). We had
about fourteen inches of snow on the ground, a southwest chinook wind developed
into a gale of fifty miles per hour. The writer with his parents visited at the
Wendelin Dosch Sr. home. When we returned home at five P. M. all the snow had
disappeared. With a two horse sleigh and no snow the horses played out, but we
finally arrived home. To our surprise, as we reached home, there was the John
Tuscher family with the stone boat and their bedding. They spent the night with
us as the fifty mile an hour gale blew the roof off of their sod house. The
Tuscher farm was located on the Northwest quarter of Section 10 and no longer
exists.

Last but not least we are all obligated to our creator, and master, regardless
of creed, color, or nationality. So in 1905 the people of the Catholic faith,
of Fountain and bordering townships, erected a house of worship located on
Northwest corner of the Southwest quarter of Section 2-123-67. The church was
completed in 1906 and was known as the NEW STRASSBURG CHURCH. The first church
services (Holy Mass) were celebrated in March of 1907 by the Rev. Father
Dahlman, Pastor of St. Marys Church in Aberdeen at that time. The writer of this
periodical was very fortunate to participate as an acolyte at the above
ceremonies. Church services were held in the New Strassburg Church up until
1950, when the structure was salvaged.

Finally, I hope some one will have the fortitude and courage to continue on where I left off, when
the City of Ipswich will celebrate its Centennial in 1983.
        Jos D. Lacher

History of Ipswich Township

Ipswich Township No. 123 range 68 was named for the home town in England of
Charles Prior, who was Superintendent of the C. M. & St. Paul railroad.

The School district was organized October 1, 1883. J. W. Parmley, the first
County Supt. of  Edmunds County, was ordered to plot the school  districts, and
the settlers were named as they pleased.

Two school houses were built in the township. One was on Sec. 8 and the other
on Sec. 14. The first apportionment of $297.13 with which to run the school was
made in February 1884 for district No. 14 to A. B. Allen the Treasurer of
Ipswich Township Schools. The school building on Sec. 8 was later sold to Mr.
Mike Schweich to be used as a house and later they added an addition, and now
is the home of Mr. and Mrs. Joe J. Krumm now of this township. The school
building on Sec. 14 was sold to Julius Haveck for a granary, and it still
remains in the township.

The schools were consolidated in 1921 or 1922.

Ipswich Township, itself, was organized on July 11, 1890. Several years after
the school districts were platted. The first Township officers were: John
Doonan, A. L. Jones, and L. E. Evans.

Some of the first homesteaders who proved up on their land and received their
receipts in 1884 were: Lester E. Jones, Arthur B. Chubbuck, James L. Crawford,
Samuel J. Sherman, A. L. Jones, Mayo P. Jaquith, W. P. Tate, Henry C. Briggs,
Lewis M. Chase, and Joseph Brown which was the father of Willard Brown, the
first white child born in Edmunds County.

August 4, 1883 in Freeport Dakota Territory, Robert W. Jones homesteaded in
Powell Township in 1896, thus classing him with the Pioneers of Ipswich
Township.

Jacob Adler another pioneer, settled in Ipswich Township January 1903 living
there many years, but later moved to Aberdeen, South Dakota. The farm since
then has been occupied by a son Leo, who has lived there the past (37) years.

Will Briggs, the oldest son of Henry C. Briggs, who lived on the S. W. quarter
of Sec. 34 is the author of the book, "DAKOTA IN THE MORNING". The scene of
which is located in Ipswich and the vicinity.

The main part of the house which still stands on their quarter was brought here
from Kalamazoo, Mich. in sections ready to be put together.
        Mrs. Byron Jones

Old Settlers Poem

Dedicated to the Old Settlers of Edmunds County
by Rose Seelye Miller

In eighteen hundred and eighty three,
        This virgin soil rolled fair and free.
Like a great green ocean, whose lapping waves.
        Frothed with buffalo bones, in its caves.
Starred with Pasque flowers at the edge of its snows.
        Wreathed with the blossoming, pink wild rose.
The winds were fragrance, and the glowing sun,
        Made a path of glory, when day was done.
Far to the West that path of gold;
        Far to the West the prairies rolled.
While far from the East came hurrying feet.
        To this land of promise, fair and sweet.
And men were kin in those old glad days.
        When life was promise, and hope was praise.
When all sat down to a common mess,
        And gave thanks to goodness, it wasn't less.
And none complained though the coffee boiled.
        And none said a word if the beans were spoiled.
And the banker or lawyer washed his plate,
        If it was washed again, before he ate.
Sixteen could bunk in an eight foot shack.
        And were sure, that of room, there was no lack.
And if on occasions we would drink
        We crept lo some slough with a slippery brink.
Of germs and microbes no one talked.
        For man was the biggest germ that walked.
And they hadn't time to study out,
        The reasons why rich men had the gout.
E. Drew was the first to cross Snake Creek,
        And of all Edmund's county to take his pick,
Others quickly followed that slender trail,
        Till 'twas beaten hard by the travel frail.
Young cities sprang over a night.
        And perished as quick as a mushroom might.
Old Georgetown thought it was on the frail,
        Where the great steel serpent should fish his tail,
And Edmunds and Freeport were fully sure,
        They too were the cities to endure.
But lo! In the fall of eighty three.
        When the first train pulled out gay and free.
Twas from the site where Ipswich was to be.
        And George Loomis moved his grocery store,
        From Georgetown, and other too, galore.
Fell over each other to get the land.
        Where the railroad city took its stand.
in eighteen hundred and eighty three.
        A youth whose initials were M. P. B.
(The prettiest stripling over seen.)
        Clerked in a store in Aberdeen.
And he dispensed ribbons and smiles and lace,
        With no short measures, in any case.
J. Parmley organized schools galore,
        In eighteen hundred and eighty four.
Or it may have been in eighty-five.
        But whatever the date, he was "Man Alive,"
And was ready with first aids to the heart,
        Of the school ma'am who wanted to get a start.
Willard Brown was the baby of greatest fame,
        The first in the county to stake a claim.
There was Stowe and Parsons and' Jewett and Cox,
        And a man named Herrick sold drinks for his rocks.
There were Jackson's and Johnson's a dozen or more.
        And Brown's Smiths and Joneses, of course by the score.
There was Stacy Cochrane that man of fame,
        Who walked fourteen miles, and was not lame,
When he reached his school, and without a slip,
        He sailed forth on his homeward trip,
And baked his pancakes, or brewed his tea.
        For a mighty man in those days was he.
Judge Briggs, grand and firm and fine,
        We cannot pas him without a line,
With a Master's brain and a massive heart,
        Too big a man for a sordid part;
And the very earth has raised to this man,
        A monument better than craftsman can,
For the trees that were set by his noble hand.
        Writ his name in beauty over the land.
C. Barron, he was a "rose in bloom,"
        With a gait requiring considerable room,
Shout, shout from the housetops, clear, that then,
        Ipswich was the City of Handsome Men,
Are men embraced women, if preacher's are right,
        Then, as ever, they shone by rejected light.
The only old settler here today,
        As a "brother pauper," is Slocum, M. A.
Cap. Arnold, "Tis rumored in early day,
        Drove a racing ox team far away.
Can you see the setting of the stage?
        The Captain, the oxen, the gay equipage,
And a lady, Here let the curtain fall,
        On "the dear, dead days beyond recall."
Looking back as we do, o'er the fleeting years,
Their joys and sorrows, their triumphs and tears,
We see this country and this land,
        Owned by a people, great and grand.
Great in the fact that they've been true.
        Grand in [he fact that they've come through,
The slough of Despond and the Castle Despair.
        And have reared their fortunes fair and square
Some have been handicapped in the fray,
        Yet all have fought in a valiant way;
And someday too, all shall be crowned,
        When the fight is closed, and the records found
It is not the getting of wealth or fame,
        But keeping steadily in the game.
And playing our part, whether great or small,
        That pleases the Umpire of us all.
The cup of Life has many slips,
        Ere the rich, red wine has reached our lips,
But the golden bowl is with us still.
        And some day we shall drink our fill.
And we who have lived these many years.
        So close to each other our falling tears.
Drop as freely over our brothers loss,
        As when we, ourselves carry Gethsemane's cross,
Have our roots sunk deep in the prairie sod
        While our hopes unit in a common God.
And our hearts now beat, as all hearts should.
        In the bond of a common brotherhood.
Bald, or black, or haloed with grey,
        We pass out to-night and go our way.
Halt or lame or wrinkled by tears.
        Bearing the weight of a number of years.
Yet our hearts are as young as in eighty three.
        When our Youth claimed the prairie wild and free,
And we thrilled with rapture to touch the sod,
        Where the foot of a white man had never trod.
And someday our hearts shall thrill again,
        With a mightier rapture than this has been.
When we cross the River and stake our land,
        Where green pastures lie on every hand,
And we shall meet, on some Great Day,
        In the land where "old settlers," forever stay.

        The Edmunds County Wedding Quartette 1902.
