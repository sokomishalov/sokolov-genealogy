				DEDICATION

To the Pioneers who settled this region of South Dakota, endured privation
and hardship, wrested a living from the bare prairie and built their homes
from the prairie sod when no other material was available, society
acknowledges an everlasting debt.

To this land also came the oppressed of Europe to find opportunity and
freedom. Many of them found great success through hard work and ingenuity.
Among these were the German-Russian people whose descendants populate
Bowdle and this area of Edmunds County.

At the invitation of Catherine the Great to her own native German people,
many had migrated to Russia accepting her offer of freedom of land,
politics, religion and freedom from military service. They learned to love
the rich soils of the Odessa and Crimea areas of Russia where they settled,
as the climate was moderate, crops were abundant, and fruits of many kinds
flourished.

But they also found a new worry. Their new homeland also abounded with nomad
bands which swept down upon the villages where the Germans had thought to
find protection in the security of their numbers; but fields were often
plundered and sometimes even homes and the livestock driven off.

A new fear crept into their lives when Catherine the Great died and her
successor soon found he needed more manpower to fight the Turks and
Napoleon's invading army than he could muster from his native Russians. It
was this great feat that again the young men of the families would face
enforced military servitude as they had in Germany, that prompted them to
look toward the new land.

Many young German-Russian men, and older men with families found a way of
escape; some of them had relatives who had come here earlier to help them
obtain passage money for the long trip to America; and many worked and
saved toward the day of the new land, America.

South Dakota became the richer for their endeavors and to them and all the
other pioneers of this area, to the people who followed in their footsteps
and put forth a continual effort for the progress of the community, we
respectfully dedicate this book.

		The History of Bowdle

The City of Bowdle, as do so many of the towns located along railroads, had
its location determined perchance, -it depended upon the decision of the
railroad officials and the surveyors, as to where, in 1886, their new road
would have its terminus.

It was questionable in the minds of some people and newspaper editors as to
whether there would be a town actually located at what now is Bowdle. This
is indicated in the Bangor Rustler, dated May 28, 1886, which states, "The
grading between Ipswich and Walworth Counties on the Milwaukee extension is
being pushed vigorously...being fourteen miles east and one mile south of
Bangor."

	ALEX M. BOWDLE

The platting of the townsite of Bowdle was completed on Saturday night, May
20, 1886, on section 21-123-73, the present location of Bowdle. The town
was named after the townsite agent, Alex M. Bowdle, of Mitchell, Dakota
Territory, who was also an official of the railroad company. Mr. Bowdle
acquired some of the lots for himself as some of the abstracts of property
in Bowdle will show.

The Bowdle Pioneer of May 24, 1886, states, "Town of Bowdle platted
Saturday night...." The Pioneer office was located, at its beginning, about
where Mrs. Fred Grosz's garden plot is now located, on Milan Street.

The first issue of this paper, Volume 1-Number 1, is dated May 10, 1886,
and the second issue is dated May 24, 1886, Volume 1-Number 2. From this we
can assume that the May 10th issue was edited and printed at Scranton,
Dakota Territory, in Walworth County, on the river. Since no paper was
printed on May 17th, it would indicate that the building was being moved
from Scranton to Bowdle between May 10th and May 21st. The Pioneer of May
24, 1886, stated, "...on Sunday night the Pioneer (of Scranton) was on the
ground." We can assume that the Pioneer building was the first building,
or one of the first, in Bowdle, except for what shelters the construction
crews may have had, which likely were tents. There is a possibility that a
saloon may have been the first business of the new town.

It would seem that the next buildings to come to Bowdle were saloons. The
Bowdle Pioneer of May 10, 1886, states, "Bowdle has five saloons and
whiskey is prohibitive in Edmunds County." From this article we can gather,
if true, that the first buildings, or at least businesses in Bowdle, were
saloons. These may have operated in tents also as there is no record of
their existence.

	Mr. and Mrs. Fred J. Bowman

Fred J. Bowman was the editor of this paper. Mr. Bowman, prior to coming
here, had edited the Le Beau Pioneer, which became the Scranton Pioneer.

Although Bowdle was platted in May 1886, it was not the seat of business
for the first settlers in this area. The area of Walworth County must be a
part of the history of Bowdle, since Bowdle was the terminus of the
railroad until 1900, and the people in Walworth County used Bowdle as their
trading center. The first post office in this area was at the Kelly ranch,
or station, which was located on the southeast corner of the southwest
quarter of section 29-123-73, in Bowdle Township. This quarter of land is
now owned by Arlyn Erbe, just west of the airport quarter. Kelly station
was a place where the stage coach would change horses on its daily trip
from Aberdeen to Le Beau. These stations were located about every fourteen
to eighteen miles apart and also served as the post office for the settlers
in the areas.

Kelly station consisted of a housing for horses, which may have been a tent
affair. Also located there was a blacksmith shop, which was always a
necessity in those days, and a place to eat and sleep; although not listed
as a restaurant or hotel.

The old stage coach trail hit Bowdle, before its existence, about where the
present schoolhouse is now located.

Edmunds County was organized October 1, 1883, and was divided into
townships which were also later made into common school districts. The
Kelly Common School District #9, consisting of Bowdle Township, was
organized July 7, 1884.

In July 1919, the County Superintendent of Edmunds County proclaimed that
by law the Kelly Common School District #9 should be organized into an
independent school district. The election was held Tuesday, August 5, 1919.
The results show that 19 votes were cast and it carried. The officers
elected were: H. C. Baer, R. L. Lemke, Alfred Davidson, George Danser, and
Valentine Wentz. George Weishaar was appointed the clerk.

On August 20, 1919, the Kelly Common School District deeded block number
29, on which the school building in Bowdle is located, to the Bowdle
Independent School District #9.

	BOWDLE'S FIRST HIGH SCHOOL

In 1886, Bowdle had its first session of school in a room on the second
story of the first business place constructed in 1886, by G. E. Aurand and
L. E. Amerpohl. This room was also used for Sunday School and on Saturday
afternoons, as a court room. Since the population of Bowdle grew so rapidly,
after the town was platted, it was necessary, in 1888, to construct a
school building of suitable size. The building was constructed of lumber and
served first as a grade building and later as a high school. Teaching of
high school subjects began with the year 1901-1902, when the ninth grade
was added. The southeast wing of the building was added in 1908. The first
graduates from the four year high school, which was begun with the 1911-1912
term, were Grace Baker and Ruth Boyd, in 1912. President Nash of Aberdeen
Normal delivered the commencement address. Beryle Ring and William
Mickelson were the first to graduate from the two year course of the high
school.

Increased enrollment causing crowded conditions in the old building plus
the dangers of fire, caused the people to build a new brick building. In
1918, the old building was torn down and during the interim, grade school
was held in the opera house, located where the Schaible Garage is now and
the high school was held in the old Masonic Hall, up stairs, where the
North Side Cafe is now. The new building was completed in February 1919. In
1937, an addition for a gymnasium-auditorium was voted on for a second time
and carried. The addition was completed in 1938.

In 1959 the Bowdle School District went through reorganization. The
following board members were elected: William C. Miller, Pres., Mike Mitzel,
Vice Pres., Ervin Gross, Bernard Bachman and Alvin Bauer. Wilmer Schaible
was appointed clerk and Ramon Schriver, treasurer. In December of 1959 an
election was held for the purpose of bonding the newly formed school
district in the amount of $250,000.00. This failed to get the 60% of the
votes necessary. In February the 10th, 1960, another election was held to
bond the district in the amount of $260,000.00 for the purpose of building
a grade building, shop, bus housing and acquire some land for playground
east of the school building. This bond issue carried and in August of 1960,
construction was started and at the present time is near completion.

The history of Bowdle is limited because the newspapers of the Pioneer were
destroyed by fire. The city of Bowdle was incorporated by an election held
August 15, 1911. President of the board of trustees of the town of Bowdle
was A. G. Bickert, members were H. G. Slyder and Pete Geib, clerk was H. E.
Yaukey. The election results were 79 votes for incorporation and 11 votes
against. Bowdle therefore became a third class city, which is determined by
population. The population on June 13, 1911 was a booming 671. The town was
entitled to only two saloons. The saloons on this date were allowed as
follows: Peter Grismer and Henry Leidholt, lost was Thomas O'Malley. The
town watchman was, at this time, Sam Haring.

Railroad construction began from Ipswich west and was terminated at Bowdle
in 1886. The C. M. & St. Paul railroad was completed to Bowdle on September
9, 1886. The first regular train came to Bowdle on Sunday, September 12th.

Bowdle became a booming railroad town on the completion of the road. All of
the areas west, north and south of Bowdle did their trading here since this
was the terminus of the railroad. There was no other way to move their
produce or to purchase their needs, they were dependent upon the railroad,
which today is no longer true. There was considerable agitation from the
people in and around Bangor to ship out their grain in the 1890's by
steamboats on the Missouri River. Attempts were made to find a suitable
place to load, but the river was not dependable as the river was frozen in
winter and was not navigable until May and June in the spring. One reason
for the agitation to find another market source for their grain was that
they felt that the elevators in Bowdle were not giving them a fair deal.
They claimed that the local elevators would raise their prices on Saturday
noon, when the farmers came for their shopping and after they had unloaded
their grain. Then while the farmer went home to get a load of grain in on
Monday for the rise in price, remember they used horses and oxen, by Monday
noon the elevator would drop the prices, so when the farmer arrived here
afternoons, the price was no longer what it was when he went home on the
Saturday before. They also complained of the dockage on the grain. Business
was booming and the population of Bowdle, increased by leaps and bounds,
starting from a very few in 1886, to a population of 600 to 700 in 1900. By
1900 the railroad went west to the river, at Evarts, and this hurt the town
of Bowdle. The population reached a standstill and business, which had
flourished, had to adjust itself downward to where the businessman could
make a living.

Bowdle was known for its vices of saloons as well as its virtues. This
article taken from the Bangor Rustler of June 4, 1886 states, "That the Le
Beau whiskey saloon outfit has gone to Bowdle."

The first settlers came to this area in about 1883, after the county was
surveyed and organized. Most of them came from eastern states for the
purpose of getting cheap lands. Some came only for that reason and as soon
as they had proved up their land they went back to their eastern homes.
Most of the early settlers, however, came to find a new frontier and to
establish a livelihood where the land was cheap and living conditions were
hard. To those pioneers we owe a great deal, they were rugged
individualists with a desire to find a new home and for some to obtain
wealth. Later from about 1888 on we have the migration of immigrants from
Russia, the German-Russians, Germany and the Scandinavian countries.

In the early history of the pioneers in and around Bowdle, we read of the
severe hardship of living conditions with drought, heat, hailstorms,
tornadoes, prairie fires and the ever present Dakota blizzards. There were,
however, also many good times and happy times some of the old timers tell
of the dances they attended and the parties, which were held in the homes
of the pioneers. In 1892, we read that they had a bumper crop while in
1886-7-8, there were poor crops. This was at a time when most of the
pioneers came to this area and was the cause for many to return to their
native homes in the east. In 1886, Spring Lake and Swan Lake were dry,
overgrown with tall grass and rushes.

The blizzard of 1888, January 12, is sighted by most of the old settlers
and was the worst in the annals of history to that date. One must realize
that the pioneers were new in this country and did not know of Dakota
blizzards before that time and one can assume that it was a terrible
experience, however, it lasted only from morning to the next morning.
Because of the open country, lack of trees and shelters, no doubt, made
this storm worse than those storms to follow in later years, some of which
lasted more than three days.

The Indian scares of 1889 and 1890 are also a matter of record among the
pioneers, and were caused by frantic whites, who because they were frantic
or because they wanted to drive out the settlers in order to take over
their claims and to steal their property while they were gone, never did
materialize. However, the settlers did scare and many left their homes to
come to Bowdle to make a stand, some even left the country permanently.

One has to use the imagination somewhat to picture this country during the
early days of our pioneers. One must think of this country as a land of
rolling plains and prairies without trees or cultivated land, fences or
roads as the conditions which existed here when the first settlers arrived
and for a good many years thereafter. Mr. Amerpol states in a letter that
they put up markers out of town in every direction showing the number of
miles and directions to Bowdle. These markers were put out as far as twenty
miles because there were no roads and it was easy to get lost. Without
trees, we read that the birds nested on the ground. Nature was in its
rarest beauty, untouched by civilization, with wild flowers known only to
these open prairies. There was also plenty of wild game, buffalo, deer,
prairie chicken, wild duck and wild geese, even the white crane. With the
coming of civilization to this area everything has changed, whether for
better or worse, we are the heirs to their efforts and what we will do with
what they have created and passed on to us only time will tell. Lest we
forget, let us always strive to uphold the traditions which they set for
us, the hardships which they endured for us, so that we may enjoy their
blessings with a feeling that we must always carry on in the same tradition
which they so gallantly bore for us. Let us always strive to go forward and
progress like these pioneers who had fine homes other places, but came here
because they were not satisfied with just living in fair comfort in their
former homes and most of all they were peace loving people.

The town of Bowdle from the time of its organization in 1886, was served by
a Board of Trustees, of which there were three members. In addition to the
members, one of which was the president, there was appointed a clerk. The
following served as mayors of the city of Bowdle since its incorporation:
Dr. William Edwards 1911-1912, was the first mayor of Bowdle, Rev. Keller
1913-14, Ole Larson 1914-16, Jacob Kurle, Sr. 1916-18, John C. Gross
1918-20, C. W. Young 1920-22, L. Merkel 1922-24, John Erbe 1924-26, K. J.
Kundert 1926-28, John Erbe 1928-30, J. B. Hoffman 1930-36, John Geib
1936-38, J. K. Moser 1938-42, John Oster 1942-46, J. K. Kurle 1946-48, W.
C. Miller 1948-52, Joseph V. Stappler 1952-60, Helmer Leno 1960--.

The town of Bowdle made great progress under the leadership of its city
officials from the time of its founding to the present day. From wooden
sidewalks to concrete sidewalks, from gas lamps to electric lights, from
dirt and mud streets to graveled and then oiled streets, from oxen to
horses to automobiles and airplanes and many other changes from then to now.

	Graveling of Main Street

After the incorporation of the town of Bowdle in 1911, the biggest steps
were taken in the development of Bowdle. The municipal light plant was put
in operation in about 1911 and this served until sometime in 1922, when the
city contracted with the Farmers Cooperative Milling Company for power and
light. This served the city until August of 1925. In August of 1925,
Northern States Power Company was given the franchise to furnish current
and later this was turned over to the present operator, the Montana-Dakota
Company. The reason for changes being made on the first two plants was that
these plants did not give satisfactory service and current. The electrical
current was off from midnight to seven in the morning. It was not until the
Northern States Power and Light Company took over that Bowdle had electric
current 24 hours a day.

Dr. William Edwards operated the first hospital, possible from his first
arrival until he built the front part of the present building, in about
1918. After his death, Dr. T. D. Jones operated this hospital when the
Bowdle, Community Hospital Association was formed. This association then
added an addition of the back, in about 1927. The hospital operated with
Dr. Jones and at times a second doctor until about 1937 or 1938. For a time
after that it served as a first aid home, operated by a Mrs. McCall. With
Dr. Powell here the hospital functioned again for a short time. Then it
closed until 1947. In 1947 the association again became active and raised $
17,000 by subscription and at the same time elected new officers and
proceeded to call for doctors and nurses. However, after a short time Dr.
Dunn left and nurses were hard to get and after a number of attempts with
different doctors and the difficulty of financing the operation, the
association deeded the hospital property to the city, provided it would
assume the mortgage obligation against it in the amount of about $3500. The
date of deeding by the association was about September 6, 1949 and the city
on October 3, 1949 advertised its acceptance for ownership.

	Dr. T. D. Jones      Dr. Anton Hyden

The city then operated the hospital until May 7, 1951, when it was closed
because it was operating at a loss, due to the fact that there was a lack of
patient admittance, lack of doctors and nurses. Dr. Rainis Berzins came in
the fall of 1953 and after his arrival the hospital again went into
operation in October and November of 1954. Because of a good doctor and
good management, the hospital showed a profit and continued in operation to
the present time.

In June of 1957 a mass meeting for the purpose of discussing plans to keep
Dr. Berzins in Bowdle was called. A committee was appointed to see if an
addition could be put on the old hospital. It was finally decided to build
a new hospital with Hill-Burton funds.

The city bonded itself for monies in the amount of $50,000 and the balance
was raised by subscription plus other funds that the city had on hand. The
hospital will cost about $335,000.00. Money was collected from Bowdle and
the surrounding communities. At the present time the site is set and
contracts have been let and work will progress in the spring of 1961 and
should be completed sometime in 1962.

March 10, 1920, a special election was held for the purpose of voting on
water and sewer system bonds. This election carried. The water and sewer
system was completed in 1921, at a cost of $157,804.94.

There was a lot of dissatisfaction with the construction work as quite a
number of leaks appeared in the water mains upon testing, so final payment
was withheld. The bill was finally paid off. The city water bond was in the
amount of $40,000 and the sewer bond was in the amount of $23,000. The
balance was to be raised by the sale of water and sewer rights for all
property lots, some of which never were paid. Except for some errors in the
survey for depth of sewer, we have one of the finest systems in South
Dakota. We have the distinction of having the highest water tower in South
Dakota. Since that time we have added new wells, reservoir, and disposal
plant. The last $3000.00 of the city water and sewer bond was paid of
August 1, 1949.

The streets are now in fine condition being covered with a dust proof
asphalt. The city over a number of years improved the streets with gravel
and during the WPA days they were covered with a good gravel and during the
WPA days they were covered with a good gravel base. The first oiling of the
streets took place in August of 1951. Most of the people paid for oiling
the first time without question. The oiling has proven satisfactory where
there was a good gravel base and this base was there on most of the streets.

The city ventured into the liquor control plan by an election held on April
20, 1937, the results of which were 94 votes against and 122 votes for. The
municipal package sale went into effect on July 1, 1937. Later in about
1946 or 1947, the on sale was added. This has been profitable for the city
as well as the control of the liquor business.

In December of 1949, the city went into snow removal on Main Street. This
was a new venture and caused some friction. However, today it is taken for
granted. In January 1952, the city purchased the first snow plow for the
purpose of opening the streets. One of the reasons for these ventures was
the vast amount of snow that winter and for that reason had the acceptance
of most of the citizens of Bowdle.

In order to keep the alleys clean, to cut down on the flies and to keep the
dump grounds in order, the city let its first contract for hauling of ashes
and garbage on July 5, 1949, and has continued this plan to the present
day. This has improved the conditions of our alleys, for they have been
graveled and are maintained. In this way it has been an improvement to the
whole city.

	Bowdle Volunteer Fire Department

The first meeting of the Bowdle Volunteer Fire Department, was held on
April 17, 1901. Officers elected were C. L. Wood, Chief; L. E. Amerpohl,
Assistant Chief; J. L. Owens, Secretary; and R. T. Hill, Treasurer. The
company was composed of thirty-six members. Henry Baer, one of the original
members of the Department, still resides in the city of Bowdle.

The first equipment of the company consisted of two chemicals and a hook
and ladder cart. Later two hose carts were added, after the installation of
the city water system. The first fire alarm system in Bowdle was a bell
mounted on a tower. This was erected in August of 1917.

The present electric fire siren was purchased in April of 1929. The fire
truck was purchased by the city of Bowdle, in 1948.

In 1956, the farmers of the community, organized to purchase a community
fire truck to serve the farm area, which is manned by the Bowdle Firemen.

Fire Chiefs in the past sixty years were C. L. Wood, Simon Ring, Sam

		The Histories of the Bowdle Churches

		The Methodist Church

The history of the Methodist Church in Bowdle is linked closely with the
history of the town, and as far as is known, it was the first church to be
established here. Its first services were held in store buildings when the
town was in its very first stages of growth. Worship services were held by
the pioneers in the Tom Ward General Store building, the upper room
furnishing adequate space for meetings. The building stood where Art
Merkel's office is now located. Sunday school was held in the upstairs of
the Aurand and Amerpohl Store building, which at that time was located
across the street east from the city fire station and pump house. Later the
building was moved two blocks west and to the south side of the street. As
nearly as can be ascertained the stores were meeting places in 1886 and
prior.

After Bowdle's first hotel, the Dumont House (now Reuer's Garage) was
built, services were held in the hotel dining room every two weeks. Rev.
Samuel Franklin Brown of Bangor was the minister. In the summer of 1887 he
organized the Methodist Society of Bowdle. The schoolhouse was completed
that year and a Rev. Harrington was the first resident pastor. Services
were held in the upstairs of the school. Through funds raised by the Ladies
Aid an organ was purchased.

Rev. E. O. Bullock, succeeded Rev. Harrington, as he made the first record
of membership that of Mr. and Mrs. G. C. Aurand on December 19, 1888. On
July 31, 1892 Pastor Ward received Mr. and Mrs. Charles Dorwin and Mrs. Mary
Baker into membership.

The devotion of these people to the church was steadfast. For thirty years
Mr. and Mrs. Aurand taught in the Sunday School; Mrs. Aurand teaching the
children and Mr. Aurand the young people. Mrs. Dorwin was Sunday School
Superintendent. Mrs. Aurand also served as steward collecting money for the
minister's salary with the assistance of her mother, Mrs. Mary Cooley.

Pastor's salaries were a problem and Rev. L. McCullock eked out a living by
working part time in the Aurand and Amerpohl Store. He was industrious,
however, and despite two jobs managed to build a home here, which of recent
years has been occupied by the John Gieb and John Dewald families.

1895 was a big year in the church. Through the efforts of Pastor J. B.
Dibble, donations from the citizens and the Methodist Aid Society and
Methodist Extension Society, funds were raised and a church edifice built
on its present site. It became not only a church for members of the
Methodist denomination, but for all in Bowdle who did not understand or
speak German. With the coming of the German-Russian immigrants most of the
churches here were conducted only in the German language for many years.

In 1923 Mr. Aurand died and left a bequest to the church. This financed the
basement of the present building where Ladies Aid and other meetings are
held.

No record is given as to when the parsonage was built, but in 1945 the
proceeds from the sale of the building and funds from the sale of a
Methodist Church in Mound City, along with that raised by the members and
Ladies Aid financed the remodeling of the church edifice, which was
redecorated and the new aisle carpeting laid. Mrs. Art Merkel and Mrs.
Ernest Merkel refinished the pulpit and communion table

From 1927 through 1930 Bowdle and Mobridge were united as a charge, the
pastor living at Bowdle. For several years Bowdle and Selby were united
as a charge, until 1955 when Bowdle and Tolstoy churches were served by the
same pastor. In 1959 the Seneca Church was added to this charge.

The 1948 church records show 190 persons admitted to membership, 107
infants baptized, 87 members were lost to the church through transfers to
other churches and 33 members' deaths were recorded.

		St. Augustine's Catholic Church

To the early beginning of the parish, we owe a thanks to Rev. Father
Stephen Duren, for having handed on to us the early history of the Catholic
Church in Bowdle. During the summer of 1886, Patrick Hughes, Charles
LeMore, James Guhin, Conrad Bickert and Thomas O'Malley came to Bowdle. In
the fall of 1886, Rev. Bernard Heus, Pastor at Ipswich, South Dakota, in
passing through Bowdle on his way to Hoven, South Dakota, baptized the
first Catholic child and celebrated the first Holy Mass in Bowdle in a hall
over Cox's Store. He also performed the first marriage ceremony, that of a
Mr. Gross and Mary Schumacher. He also officiated at the first Catholic
funeral, that of Joseph Kessler.

Father B. Heus had charge of the congregation until March 1888, when Father
Joseph Stephan became his successor in Ipswich, and he held Divine services
in various homes in Bowdle. From 1889 to 1896, Father Sack attended to the
spiritual welfare of the Catholics, coming from Hoven, South Dakota. In the
fall of 1893, the first meeting was held in the McCall Elevator, the
purpose of the meeting to organize a congregation and erect a church. The
first church was started in April 1894. It was a frame structure and the
cost was $500.00. In 1895, the church was too small to accommodate the
faithful who now numbered 35 families. In 1896, under Father N. J.
Dahlmans, of Ipswich, the church was completed. From June 1897 until
September 1899, Rev. Joseph Dunn, of Ipswich, had charge of the
congregation. From November 1899 to August 1900 Rev. J. Hansen came monthly
from Ipswich. On July 1, 1902, Father Stephen Duren was appointed pastor of
Ipswich and the mission of Bowdle. He held services in Bowdle two Sundays
every month and on all Holy Days. He officiated at the first Christmas Mass
ever said in St. Augustines. On August 28, 1903 Father Duren dedicated the
church, and the church was named after St. Augustine, Bishop of Hippo.

February 11, 1906, began a new era for St. Augustine's congregation. The
first resident pastor of St. Augustine's was the newly ordained priest, the
Rev. Father John Krebs who came from Brooklyn, New York.

His sister came with Father Krebs, worked with him, and was also the first
organist in the history of the parish. Father Krebs worked with great zeal
and the one time Mission of Bowdle became the Mother Church for the present
parishes of Onaka and Roscoe, South Dakota, which parishes, Father Krebs
founded. With horse and buggy, he traveled from parish to parish, and his
work was tedious. He was helped in this great work by his sister, Miss Ann
Krebs. The parish of Bowdle grew in numbers and Father Krebs started
planning the building of a new church. Father left Bowdle in 1912 but the
heroic and generous people of that era fulfilled his dream and built the
present, magnificent, beautiful and Gothic Stone Church, under the direction
of Rev. John Kettler in 1918, who carried that burden that every building
project entails. Father Kettler was preceded by Father W. Krause who served
from 1912 until 1916.

Rev. Father William Schmitt followed Father Kettler. Financial wise, the
times in America were not good, yet much was accomplished by Father Schmitt
and his faithful people.

The successor to Father Schmitt was Father Joseph Hensbach, who also
carried the cross of running the parish in hard years. He also carried the
burden of age, very bravely, as he served faithfully as pastor of St.
Augustines for many years. As the times grew better, a number of great
improvements and repairs were made. Father Hensbach was elevated to the
rank of Monsignor in 1953. After his retirement in August 1955, Father G.
A. Althoff, O. P. was appointed by the present Archbishop of St. Paul,
William O. Brady to take care of St. Augustines.

With two wonderful crop years of 1957 and 1958, the people of St.
Augustines, most generously gave thanks to God, and donated a great amount
for the redecoration of the House of God and also contributed most
generously to the Diocesan Expansion Fund, inaugurated by the present
Bishop of Sioux Falls, the Most Rev. Lambert A. Hoch, D. D. The people also
contributed generously to their own Parish Building Fund, for the first new
rectory to be built this year on the south side of the Church. With the
bells of the church ringing the Angelus three times a day and while the
tower clock rings out the time day and night, we hear it as a prayer of
thanks over the town.

The Parish is proud to have four of their sons in the service of God, in
the Diocese of Sioux Falls, South Dakota. They being the Right Rev.
Monsignor Henry Hoerner, the Rev. Father Pius Mardian, the Rev. Father Paul
Bachman and the Rev. Father Joseph Mardian. Several of her daughters are
nuns nursing the sick and teaching the children in many states.

		The Bowdle 7th Day Adventist Church

The Bowdle Seventh Day Adventist Church had its beginning seventy two years
ago when Evangelist Valentine Lehr held meetings in this vicinity in the
fall of 1889. The meetings were usually held in the sod homes of the early
day settlers and were filled to overflowing by the friends and neighbors
who came to the meetings by ox team or on foot.

The meetings continued through the months and on May 4, 1890 a church of
thirty members was organized. Two of the charter members are still living.
They are Christ Dollinger of Lodi, California, and Mrs. Katie Meier. Mr.
Adam Hagele, Sr., served as the first local church elder.

The first church was built in 1894, seven and one-half miles south of
Bowdle. The congregation soon outgrew this building and a larger structure
was built in 1906. The original building was sold to the Seventh Day
Adventist Congregation of Tolstoy.

The new structure, which was built in 1906, served the congregation until
it was destroyed by fire in December 1945.

The congregation decided to build a new church in Bowdle. The construction
of the church began in the spring of 1946 with the first church services
held in the building in December of the same year. No formal dedication was
made until the building was completed. This took place on May 23-25, 1947.

That the church is fulfilling its mission is evident by the large number of
its sons and daughters that are holding important positions the world over
and at home.

The present membership is well over one hundred active Laymen not counting
those who have gone to more favorable climates.

		St. Paul American Lutheran Church

The history of St. Paul American Lutheran Church of Bowdle, South Dakota,
is much like the community's around it; varied, intricate, rising from
rugged persistence to a unified working force, reaching into many
backgrounds to make up one unit.

The people who make up St. Paul congregation have had their roots in this
community for a number of generations. Though a few belong yet to that
first generation of homesteaders, others came here with their parents and
the remainder have only the memories of stories about the struggles of
grandparents in their efforts to establish their homes and their Church.

Some of these people trace their memories back to a young Lutheran
congregation located west of Bowdle, which held its first services in
private homes. This was what today remains known as Neugluecksthal. This
group got its start in the 1890's, worshipping in a newly built schoolhouse
after 1894, until 1904 when they proudly erected their own new church
building.

The man who nurtured and guided this congregation was the Rev. Ottersberg
of Hosmer, South Dakota. This faithful pastor established a number of
congregations in the area, including the one known as St. John's
congregation about 10 miles north of Bowdle. This congregation will later
also figure into the present St. Paul congregation. Neugluecksthal
congregation was under the care of Pastor Ottersberg for a number of years,
when the congregation decided to affiliate with the Java parish, then being
served by the Rev. George Kirchdoerfer. This arrangement remained until
1919.

Others of the present membership of St. Paul congregation trace their
memories to a small congregation southwest of Bowdle, on the Hoven Trail.

Another portion have always had their roots in Bowdle, and their parents
and grandparents were part of that group which was formed into an active
congregation through the efforts of Pastor Ottersberg, and the Rev. Richard
Tueber. Pastor Tueber succeeded Pastor Ottersberg at Hosmer, and labored
diligently in providing the message of the Word of God to those people in
Bowdle which became St. Paul congregation.

From these four areas comes the present St. Paul congregation. Exactly when
the people from the congregation on the Hoven Trail became a part of the
"in town" group is not definite.

The other three, however, Neugluecksthal, St. John of north of Bowdle, and
St. Paul congregation located in Bowdle, became one parish in 1919. Their
charter from the State of South Dakota forming them into a three
congregation parish corporation was granted to them on November 19th of that
year. This important event took place under the faithful guidance and
leadership of the Rev. Hanselmann, the first pastor of the parish.

This arrangement of a three-congregation, with one interruption, remained
until 1946, when the two rural congregations submitted to the pressure of
the era and joined with the town congregation to form one unified group.
The country churches were closed, and the members considered St. Paul
congregation in Bowdle their Church home.

During the 40 years from 1919 until 1959 the congregation was served by 8
pastors and one interim pastor. Some of these years were marked with
difficulties; yet in spite of this the congregation has grown and has
developed into a stable unit. The present pastor, the Rev. Reuben G.
Schnaidt, assumed his duties in the congregation in June of 1959.

St. Paul congregation recognizes that it has an obligation in the
community, the obligation of giving the message of the Word of God, which
is the message of salvation through Jesus Christ, to all who wish to hear.
To this end it is striving to do its part in the up-building program of the
community of which it is a part.

		Bowdle Pioneer History

On May 10, 1886 Bowdle had a newspaper as the first issue came off the
press. Historically it announced "Bowdle has five saloons and whiskey is
prohibitive in Edmunds County. " It took two weeks (May 24, 1886) until the
second edition came out and this bit of cherished news was recorded, "The
Town of Bowdle was platted Saturday night and on Sunday night the
Pioneer was on the ground." (Moved in from Scranton.)

Though the first newspapers were generally known as proof sheets for their
main source of income was the publication of final proofs by the settlers
who had acquired land through the homestead act, the news they published
generally was not of the same trend as in today's small town newspapers.
The front and back pages were printed in some large city and contained news
items date lined New York, London or some other far off place. Only the
inside pages were devoted to local news. This type of ready-print was used
in the publication of the Bowdle Pioneer until about 1918 or thereabouts,
when the pages were reversed and the inside of the newspaper became ready
print, running more to serialized stories, homemaking problems and farming
features and other "Canned" news. Ready print in the Pioneer ceased about
1937.

Assembly of the type for the inside or outside pages--whichever were
devoted to local news, was indeed laborious. Each letter, space or
punctuation mark had to be picked out of the case, one at a time.

First to serve the community were Publishers Fred and Emma Bowman, who
continued in the game, with interludes of mining in Canada, publishing
newspapers in Canada, Minnesota, Oregon and California. Mrs. Bowman died in
Bellflower, California in 1936 and of them the Publisher's Auxiliary that
year wrote:

"Fred and Emma Bowman started newspaper work just after they were married
in 1876 as owners and publishers of the Prairie du Chien (Wisconsin) Union.
In 1879 they moved to Gary, South Dakota and established the Gary
Inter-State. Sensing a boom on the Missouri River, the restless young
couple migrated there and founded the Scranton Pioneer at Scranton, South
Dakota. The boom did not materialize so they moved 30 miles east to the end
of the Milwaukee Railroad at Bowdle, South Dakota, where they remained
until 1893 when they heard the call of Rainy Lake, Canada, Gold Camp and
established the Rainy Lake Journal."

In the September 15, 1886 issue the Bowman's stated, "Railroad completed to
Bowdle on September 9th when the construction train arrived. The first
regular train came to Bowdle on Sunday, September 12th."

Succeeding the Bowmans was J. L. Owens, but unfortunately it is not
possible to secure information about him. The only tangible bit came from a
visitor at the Pioneer office in 1956 who told of working at the office at
the turn of the century and prices were such that when he asked Mr. Owens
for money for the weekend it was not unusual for him to reply, "is 50 cents
enough?" and his printer said it was--not only for food, but for extras as
well.

It is also impossible to establish the date when Fred Zimmer's name went
into the masthead as publisher, but it was in the early 1900's. He was
assisted by Miss Caroline Hupp of Ipswich, who later became his wife. When
marital troubles arose and they were divorced, Mrs. Zimmers received the
Pioneer and custody of their son in settlement. She continued as publisher
until 1918.

The new publisher was W. Z. Todd, who had among his employees Miss Emma
Pietzke, now Mrs. Adolph Schoenfelder of Iowa.

He soon installed a Linotype and hand setting of type was limited to
advertisements and specialized lines and printing. W. Z. was best known for
his refusal to accept the title of editor, giving himself the title of
architect, on the grounds that he was not the editorial equal of DeVany of
Mobridge or rhyming Will Wells of Webster. But it was his editorials that
endeared him to his readers for many were of a barbed humorous vein,
probably the most popular being his reply to the South Dakota Architects in
which he refused to take out a license "because he couldn't build a pen for
a pig, let alone a house or a public building." The feud, was long, sharp
and humorous, but in 1929 Mr. Todd took his problem along to Veblen where he
purchased the newspaper. Finances there were so discouraging that he moved
to Oregon and later passed away there. Mr. Todd was assisted by his son,
John, who later became postmaster here.

Patsy McNeely of Mobridge purchased the Pioneer from Mr. Todd, and left
Bowdle in 1932 to continue in the Fourth Estate as publisher of the Dupree
(S.D.) Ziebach County News.

The record of longevity at the Pioneer's helm was earned by Albert Senn, of
Deadwood, who bought the paper in August 1932. A second generation of the
Fourth Estate, it was his belief that to "run a good country newspaper
which serves and represents its community was a bigger job than running the
biggest metropolitan daily; for it is the window through which the world
looks at the town and judges it as progressive or recessive."

	Albert Senn

During that quarter century the town moved ahead with black topped streets,
the refurbishing of the hospital, remodeling of the schoolhouse to enlarge
the gymnasium, building of the American Legion Hall, development of the
City Park and Meakins Playground, and many other projects were supported
through the Pioneer with many columns of publicity urging cooperation and
support of the public. The publication proved to be a family business for
he was assisted by his wife as City Editor and after his return from World
War II his son, Robert, also went into the business. Mr. Senn passed away
January 17, 1961.

In 1958 Dale Lewis of Wall, South Dakota, with his wife, Nancy, as
Associate Editor bought the Pioneer and again the town had a new set of
historians to record and "cry" coming events.

So far as is known the Pioneer has always been published in its present
location. Fire struck three times; in 1936 the south side was damaged from
spontaneous combustion, due to a sun-heated boiler on the outside and the
heat from a remelt vat within the building. In 1956 fire burned out the east
side when the Schaible Chevrolet Garage was razed by fire and in rebuilding
the structure, which was enlarged also, it was found that the north side had
been ablaze. Then it was learned that at one time Art Merkel's father had a
blacksmith shop to the north of the Pioneer, when it burned down, the shop
was damaged in the fire. During the reconstruction period, the Pioneer was
printed at Robert Senn's shop in Hoven.

		The Gretna Grain Company, Inc.

The Gretna Grain Company, Inc. was purchased from Herman Baer and Henry
Bauder by Joseph Stappler, in July 1945. In 1946, he added the John Geib
elevator to the company's holdings. A silo was erected by the Geib
elevator, in 1954 to increase storage capacity. Five steel grain bins, each
having a capacity of 5,000 bushels, were built in 1959.

		Henry Bauder and Grenta Grain Company Elevator

The annex of the farthest east elevator in Bowdle, was built before any
elevator. It contained three bins, which held flax, spring wheat and durum
wheat. Under the annex, a pit or underpass was dug, so that when grain was
to be loaded into box cars, a wagon holding about forty bushels and drawn
by four horses, could be driven under the bins and loaded. A chute from the
bin was opened to permit the grain to run out. The incline used in exit,
was steep and two horses were not able to pull the load up to level ground.
Grain was shoveled into the car by one man, and another stood inside the
car, shoveling the grain back into the ends of it.

Two farmers, Joe Heintz and Frank Thomas, who live northeast of Bowdle,
loaded the box cars and received the sum of $5.00 for filling one car. This
was divided equally between them and used to provide groceries for their
families. Frank Thomas was the father of August Thomas, Sr.

Grain prices were extremely low in 1932. Wheat was 18  per bushel from
harvest time until after Christmas. Rye was 11  per bushel and oats 8 .
Threshers charged 11  a bushel for threshing oats. Shelled corn was worth
3  and corn on the cob, was quoted as 3  less than shelled. Roundup coal
was selling at $8.50 per ton at the time and farmers who brought filled
grain wagons to town, got little fuel if grain was traded for coal.

Prices of grain and Roundup coal at Bowdle, in the second week of January,
1961 are as follows: No. 1 Flax, $2. 30; Oats, 43 ; No. 1 Spring Wheat,
$1.85; No. 1 Winter Wheat, $1.75; Feeding Barley, 65  and No. 2 shelled
corn, 75 . Roundup coal is priced at $16.50 per ton.

Mr. Stappler grew up on a farm, now known as the Heier farm, near Hosmer.

Adolph Braufman came to America from Rumania in 1906 at the age of 18-1/2

		Mr. Aurand and Amerpohl

Mr. L. E. Amerpohl left Brodhead, Wisconsin with Mr. G. C. Aurand for South
Dakota on June 12, 1886. They came to Bowdle and bought a lot at the east
end of town. There was no hotel in Bowdle except for a large tent for
housing and boarding new arrivals in Bowdle.

Mr. Amerpohl and Mr. Aurand decided to build on their lot and began
operating a General Store. All the lumber in this building was carted from
Ipswich by ox team.

The upper story was used for a school room, Sunday School and as a court
room. This room remained the only school room in Bowdle until a schoolhouse
was built.

Mr. Aurand brought his family to Bowdle in 1887. Mr. Aurand and Amerpohl
continued in business until 1906 when Mr. Amerpohl retired from the firm
because of ill health. Mr. and Mrs. Aurand moved to Sioux Falls in 1920 to
make their home.

		Mr. Jacob K. Kurle

Jacob K. Kurle came to Bowdle in 1905 at the age of 18 and worked for his
uncle in the Blacksmith until 19 12 when he bought one half interest in the
business. Later he bought out all of the Blacksmith and operated the
business until 1919 when he sold it to John Oster, Jr. He then went into

		Mr. and Mrs. Henry B. Baer

A half-century businessman of the city was Henry B. Baer, who came here in
1901 to go into the general merchandise and hardware business.

Born September 28, 1876 in the German Colony of the Crimean, Russia, he was
the son of Mr. and Mrs. Bernhardt Baer. While a small lad, his oldest
brother, Christ, left Russia to avoid military service, coming to the
United States. The family, happy with his reports of the new country
followed--including the married children and their families, arriving in
1889. Henry's parents farm was located near the site of the now extinct town
of Theodore. His father later homesteaded on a quarter section near their
farm. Dr. E. J. Edwards, Bowdle's first physician, was a neighbor.

The Baer family was instrumental in instituting the Wisconsin Synod
Lutheran Church, locally known as the Baer Church, and which Henry later
served as an officer. When the railroad came to Bowdle, Theodore was
abandoned.

Henry attended the three months terms of the country school and later
completed the business course at the Lutheran College in New Ulm, Minnesota.

In 1886 in Oberwart, Austria, a winery operator, Michael Wagner, was
hearing the call of the new land, and that year brought his family to the
United States. In 1892 Mr. Wagner and his sons left the women of the family
to go to Bangor to seek land. They established a home three miles northwest
of that pioneer settlement. There were four daughters in the family and
Henry was a frequent visitor, riding his bicycle there on Sundays to call
on Sophia, who became his bride on March 12, 1901.

In September, 1901, Henry bought Andy Gerup's store in Bowdle and the newly
wedded couple took up residence here which was to last their lifetime. The
store was located just west of the present Bowdle Hardware store.

In 1906 the store burned, and Henry built the two story cement block
building which now houses the Bowdle Hardware. His brother, Herman C. Baer,
became his partner. They specialized in heating, plumbing and hardware.

Henry became Bowdle's postmaster by appointment of President Woodrow Wilson in
June 1913, and did an outstanding service as county chairman of the Victory
Bond sales. When his successor was appointed in 1923, and the post office
equipment was moved out of his building, on the north side of Main Street,
Henry again pursued his favorite trade--hardware, plumbing and heating. The
long years of toil came to a close in 1948 when he sold out to A. P. Coyer
and retired to live among his friends.

	Bowdle's First Doctor Dr. Edwards

Dr. William Edwards, Bowdle's much beloved doctor. His practice extended
over this entire country and he was often called west of the river. The
life of a doctor in those early days, before the advent of the automobile,
was by no means an easy one.

Dr. Edwards was born near Madison, Wisconsin. After finishing public
school, he attended the University of Wisconsin.

In 1877, he began the practice of medicine at Taopi, Minnesota, where he
met Miss Ida M. Wells, a teacher in the public schools. They were married
two years later. Before coming to Dakota Territory, he practiced also at
Dodgeville, Wisconsin. Coming to Dakota, Dr. and Mrs. Edwards located at
Madison, South Dakota, going from there to Theodore, South Dakota, in order
to acquire some land. They remained there until 1886, when they moved to
Bowdle.

In 1892, Dr. Edwards was elected State Senator from this district. When his
term expired, he became president of the State Board of Health under
Governor Andy Lee. He held all the official positions on the State Medical
Association.

He was the first mayor of Bowdle and established the first drug store in
the city.

Dr. Edwards was active in the civic life of his community, contributing in
many ways to the development of the town and country. Much interested in
farming, and owning several farms near here, he was the first to raise
alfalfa in Edmunds County and was a great believer in sweet clover.

Dr. Edwards passed away in 1920.

		Bowdle American Legion Post 264

After the First World War there had been a Legion Post established at
Bowdle. However, there are no records of any kind in the City that I am
able to uncover to establish the facts except from a few of the First World
War veterans who are still in our midst. However, this background was the
nucleus for the forming of a new Legion Post at Bowdle where those
faithful veterans of World War 1, who had clung to the Legion, attending
meetings as members of the Hosmer and Roscoe Posts. The first Legion Post
had faded away.

The first call to organize a new Legion Post for veterans of World Wars to
meet at the Bowdle schoolhouse December 21, 1945 was issued and who could
dream that it would become one of the hardest working, most cooperative
organization for Community service and the most veteran welfare conscious
group the city of Bowdle was to know .

In forming the new post Albert Senn acted as chairman and Fred Grosz as
Secretary temporarily for the meeting until election of the first Commander 
and officers. There was a very fine representation of the veterans
and a large attendance. Dr. F. A. Miller was elected the first Commander of
the Post. Their first order of business was the discussion over the name
for the new post. The members wished to honor those from this community who
had made the supreme sacrifice and the Post's name could not include them
all so the Post was named after the city which they all came from and the
Post was named Bowdle American Legion Post and the number assigned by the
State organization was #264. The newly elected officers were installed in
January. Nostalgia overwhelms us as we review the list of Charter Members
as forty some members have left the community or have gone west and for
whom the sun has set.

One of the first important acts of the Post was the acquiring of a new
Legion Home for the City. The group raised $1,500 to purchase the old H and
H theater building from Jonnie Hoven in February, 1946. The pleasure of
owning their own home was short lived. On February 3, 1947 a bitter cold
night the hall burned to the ground. It was insured for $1,500.00, but
the Veterans maintained their courage and the same year a building program
was proposed. Oscar Olson and John Oster served as chairmen. It was to be
built on the same site as the old home. Through patience and perseverance
the Vets went to work. Loans of $1,000 each were borrowed to the Post from
ten individuals in the community. Of course we must pay tribute to our
Legion Auxiliary who worked along with the Post). They have a very fine
complete kitchen, the envy of all Posts in the territory. The Post has
progressed every year, has honored its obligations of indebtedness on
schedule and in 1957 our mortgages were liquidated and the Post was debt
free.

  We have conducted Memorial Day Services every year with several
outstanding speakers. Veterans Day has been observed and taken to the
school. The Home is the center of all public activity.

Post #264 has been persistent in their Legion work and we have progressed
with honor and with dignity. The Legion Hall is an asset not only to the
Legion, but to the entire community. We trust that by continuing to conduct
ourselves as good citizens we shall continue to go forward.

The Bowdle American Legion Auxiliary, unit #264 of District 14, was
organized in 1946. A Junior unit was organized in 1949.

Seven Gold Star Mothers have been members of the unit: Tillie Erbe,
Perpetua Geier, Margaret Krumm, Marie Dumdei, Regina Dangel, Margaret
Leidholt and Emelia Feiock. Three of these are now dead and one has moved
away.

Gold Star Mothers are honored at the annual Mothers' Day Tea. All mothers
of the community are guests of the Auxiliary at these teas, which are
sponsored by the past presidents of the Auxiliary.

The list of past presidents includes Ruth Miller, Katherine Hoffman,
Gertrude Olson, Mary Huber, Cecelia Krause, Marcella Grosz, Almo Leno, Irma
Erbe, Emma Erbe, LaVerne Gross, LaVaughn Schlepp, and Esther Voller. Six of
these are still members. Emma Erbe passed away in 1957, and Mary Huber in
1953.

Past-president Cecelia Krause, now Mrs. William Elsing, was a WAC in World
War II. She served as District 14 president in 1952 and 1953. In 1957, she
became Department president of the Auxiliary in South Dakota.

The Auxiliary lost its equipment when the old hall burned, but the group
has equipped a modern kitchen in the new Legion Hall and also helped
finance the cost of hardwood flooring for the hall.

"Service, Not Self" is the motto chosen by the 1960-1961 Department
president, Mrs. Hazel Helland. That seems to have been the motto here, even
though it was never expressed in words. Contributions have been sent to the
Crippled Children's Hospital at Sioux Falls; Abbot House at Mitchell; the
Jewish Hospital at Denver; Sister Kenny Foundation, Red Cross, Polio fund
drives, Cancer drives and the Crusade for Freedom. Generous contributions
have been made to the Bowdle Hospital building fund. Needy families have
been given cash, clothing and groceries. Invalid children have received
checks to help pay medical expenses. Two wheel chairs are available for
anyone in temporary need and there is no charge for their use .

Veterans hospitals at Sioux Falls and Hot Springs are sent cash donations,
playing cards, pillows and pillow covers and Christmas gift items, that
veterans may select to send to their families. Patients at the State
hospital at Yankton were also sent gifts in 1959. Donations are made each
year to the President's project.

There are fifty members in the Auxiliary. This includes the juniors and
Gold Star Mothers.

		Bowdle Flour Mill

The Bowdle Roller Milling Company was organized in 1887 with E. E. Ring and
Simon Ring as incorporators and construction was completed in 1893 and
operated until 1910. John Erbe, John Kock and G. L. Baumgardner operated
until 1918, when it was sold to the Farmer's Co-op Milling Company.

John Kock leased the plant in 1927 who operated it until 1930 when it was
acquired by the Gross Land and Loan Company.

John Leno of Temvik, North Dakota, purchased the mill in September 1931 who
operated it until his death in 1946.

Helmer and Reinhold Leno operated the plant as a partnership until 1949
when Helmer Leno purchased Reinhold's interest.

The mill has been in regular operation since then. 1958 saw major expansion
in the grain line with the addition of a new 30 ton scale, two new
cleaners, a new hammer mill feed grinding and construction of a 120,000
bushel storage for grain.

During World War II the mill was on a 24 hour, 7 day week basis, exporting
flour to world markets.

Peak production was in 1946 when over 9,000,000 pounds of flour was
exported to South America and Europe.

		Bowdle Poets

One of South Dakota's famous poets sleeps the last sleep in the Bowdle
cemetery. She is Mrs. May Phillips-Tatro, the wife of an early Bowdle depot
agent. She came here with her husband, G. W. Tatro, in 1886 and passed away
in 1902 at the age of thirty seven years. Mr. Tatro was depot agent in
Bowdle.

Major O. W. Coursey in writing of her in his book entitled "South Dakota
Literature" says, "with her death the state suffered a distinct literary
loss of the most inspirational writers the state has thus far developed.
Many of her best poems are not premeditated, labored efforts, but are
rather the result of sudden impulse--a genuine melodic inspiration
springing from the human heart. Such was the manner in which she wrote "In
Hayin' Time."

Mr. Ed. S. Wittaker, a reader trained at Dakota Wesleyan University,
accompanied by a ladies' quartet from that institution, was giving a recital
at Bowdle. In his repertoire he recited James Whitcomb Riley's "Knee Deep
In June." Mrs. Tato was in the audience, she caught the inspiration of the
occasion and upon going home, seized her pen and dashed off a companion
piece to it--"In Hayin' Time"--before going to bed.

Mrs. Tatro was on intimate terms with and was recognized by the leading
authors of the nation. She was a member of the Authors' Club of Minneapolis
and a contributor to magazines.

  Walt Whitman, the well-known American poet wrote the following tribute to
her: "In the evening, when everything is quiet, I love to sit with May
Phillips-Tatro and listen to what that beautiful spirit has to tell me of
the night, sleep, death, the stars, flowers and all that she knew and so
greatly revered; such great love--such rapture of jubilant love of
nature--and the good green grass and trees and clouds and sunlight; such
aching anguish of love for all that breathes and is sick and sorrowful; such
longing to help and mend and comfort that which can never be helped and
mended and comforted; such eager looking to delicate death as the one
complete and final consolation".

May Phillips-Tatro was born in 1865 at Grand Island, Michigan. Her parents
died when she was fourteen years of age and she was adopted by Mrs. James
Hewitt, Neilsville, Wisconsin. She married G. W. Tatro in 1886 coming to
Dakota the same year.

Flosie McCafferty Briley, an early day school teacher of this vicinity, and
daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Van McCafferty authored two books for children
which have been published: "Three Grandmothers Flew South" and "Over the
Cuckoo's Nest."

Lorena Hickok, whose father was a butter maker in the creamery here about
1900 began her literary career as a reporter for the Minneapolis Journal,
later a free lance writer she became widely known for articles in the
eastern papers. During Franklin D. Roosevelt's campaign in 1932 she was
attached to the Democratic headquarters as a political reporter, and thus
earned her recognition. After she was in the White House she was associated
with Mrs. Roosevelt as her reporter and also was his representative on
tours of the depressed areas of the U.S.

		Pioneer Settlers and Family Histories

		Mr. and Mrs. Tom O'Malley

Mr. and Mrs. Tom O'Malley arrived in 1884 from Wisconsin before the town of
Bowdle was even started and homesteaded on the farm west of town now
occupied by Mr. and Mrs. Clarence Schumacher. Shortly afterwards they built
a home in town.

Mr. O'Malley opened a saloon as soon as the town was started, much against
Mrs. O'Malley's wishes. It was called Tom's Place and was located in the
building now occupied by the Casey Cafe. After 28 years in this business he
sold out in 1912.

Mr. and Mrs. O'Malley have both passed on and are buried in the Bowdle City
Cemetery.

Mrs. O'Malley is said to be the first woman to live in Bowdle.

		Mr. and Mrs. Sam Boyd

Mr. and Mrs. Boyd came to Bowdle in 1885 and the first winter lived with
Mr. Baker. Mr. Boyd worked at the masonry trade in Northville.

In 1887 they built their farm home of lumber.

Mr. Boyd played the violin for the dances which were held in the homes of
the various neighbors.

In 1907, after 22 years of farm life, they retired and moved into the city
of Bowdle.

There were three children born to this family; Wallace, who passed away,
Mrs. Ruth Sisser of Minneapolis and Mrs. Marjorie Hazeldine of Minneapolis.

Mr. Boyd died in 1925 and Mrs. Boyd lived in Minneapolis until the time of
her death in 1953.

		Mr. and Mrs. Morris Baker

Mr. and Mrs. Baker came to Bowdle in 1884 to homestead. In 1886 Mr. Sam
Boyd and Mr. Baker worked in Bowdle, laying the foundations for various
buildings and doing other masonry work. To their knowledge the first hotel
in Bowdle was the John Loeb's Sr. residence and meals were served at the
Bill Boyd house. A man by the name of Cobb had a hardware store in a tent.

In 1886 Mr. Boyd and Mr. Baker laid the foundation for the Congregational
Church.

In 1910 Mr. and Mrs. Baker moved to town. Their daughter Grace and Ruth
Boyd were the first graduates from the four year course at Bowdle High
School in 1913.

Mr. and Mrs. Baker passed away in 1942. Their daughter, Grace, is a retired
school teacher and lives in Aberdeen.

		Mr. and Mrs. William R. Green

William R. Green better known to old timers as "Billy" Green was born in
Howellsville, Pa., June 10, 1852. He went to Wisconsin in September, 1883
and in November came to South Dakota settling in Edmunds County. In the
fall of 1885 he moved to Walworth County and opened the Walworth County
State Bank, the first bank in the county and was sole owner. The next year,
1886, he opened and established the Peoples State Bank in Bowdle conducting
both banks until 1889 when he sold the Peoples Bank and consolidated his
interest in Walworth County. This same year he was admitted to practice law
in all courts of South Dakota. He was also in the abstracting business.

On December 10, 1896, he married Della Palmer. On 1907 they sold their
interests in Walworth County and moved to Sioux Falls where he purchased
the S & S Packing Plant. He passed away in Sioux Falls, December 1922.

"Billy" Green is remembered as a real booster and promoter who believed in
the future of this county and who invested his time, energy and money in
successful enterprises and did much to get the commercial machinery of
this new country into motion. To this union four children were born; Mary
Plankintou (died in infancy) Amanda Evalyn now Mrs. Clifford Taylor
residing at Seattle, Washington; Della Etholyn, Mrs. Bill McKinaman living
in Albama, California and William R. Green, head of the Chemistry
Department in Kansas City.

		Mr. and Mrs. Hans P. Christianson

Hans P. Christianson and his wife, Katharine (nee Johnson), were born in
Denmark and lived in New York State in 1882. The family of eight came to
Dakota Territory in 1885 and homesteaded south of Bowdle. Harry was born in
1892 and all the children grew up here.

Their children were: Nick, Jens, Hans, Andrew, Ella and Harry all deceased.
Katharine (Mrs. Adolph Leder) who resides in Eugene, Oregon; Ella (Mrs.
Richard Lemke) spent her life on a farm near Bowdle.

Oxen were used to till the soil and haul supplies the first years.

The family home established and built was 1-1/2 miles south of Bowdle.
There cattle were grazed. The older children worked for neighbors and Ella
herded the livestock in the fenceless areas.

Mrs. Christianson died in 1909 and Mr. Christianson in 1931.

		Mr. and Mrs. William Haupt

Mr. Haupt was born in Breslau, Germany and Mother in Lodz, Poland. William
Haupt came to America in 1884 and worked in Freeman, South Dakota before
coming to Bowdle in 1886. He was a carpenter and built the Dumont Hotel and
first bank building and did carpenter work for Dr. Edwards.

William died December 11, 1937, and Mrs. Haupt passed away in 1928.

The five children that are still living are: Carl of Bowdle, Ernest of
Aberdeen, Henry of Selby, Mrs. Bertha Gange of Yakima, Washington, and Mrs.
Hilda Schroeder of Andover, South Dakota.

		Mr. and Mrs. August Erbe

Mr. Erbe left Black Earth, Wisconsin with three of his brothers, John,
Henry and George, in 1881. Their father gave each of them a pair of oxen and
$100. 00 in cash when they left home and with this meager start, they went
west to seek their fortunes.

Mr. Erbe first took up a quarter of land near Ipswich, South Dakota, but
decided to move to the Spring Lake District where his brothers settled,
because the land there was much better for farming purposes.

He built a small house out of sod and lived in it for several years before
he could afford to build another made of lumber.

Mr. Erbe baked his own bread and when he thought he could splurge a little
he'd buy a gallon of canned apples and would eat the contents in one meal!

The big blizzard of January 1888 was one long to be remembered. Many people
suffered great loss of livestock, but Mr. Erbe was fortunate in that he
lost none. During the storm, he tied a string to the door of the house and
holding onto the rest of it, started out in the direction of the barn. It
was possible for him to follow the string back to the house without
difficulty and not get lost. The high wind and the fine snow made it almost
impossible for him to even see his hand in front of his face.

Mr. Erbe batched for seven years and on June 12, 1888 he was married at
Bowdle.

Mrs. Erbe was amazed to find that Mr. Erbe had a pet fox tied up in the
cellar! She felt it her duty to clean things up at once and worked hard at
her task. She picked up cow chips for fuel and they were burned in an old
fashioned straw-burning stove which was used for cooking and heating.

Bedbugs were numerous and Mrs. Erbe went to work immediately to rid the
house of the pests and wasn't bothered with bedbugs anymore!

Indian scares were prevalent and when word would get out that they were "on
the march", everyone was notified to gather his belongings and firearms and
hurry to Bowdle as fast as possible.

There were many prairie fires but they were never burned out.

Once in a while, the neighbor s would get together for dances, the music
for which was usually furnished by Uncle Henry, who played the violin.

Mr. and Mrs. Erbe had five children of whom Earl was the oldest, having
been born on June 29, 1889. He required much care for many months
thereafter and it was feared that he would not survive. Alvin came on
August 3, 1892, and Lucy Erbe (McElroy) followed on July 11, 1898. Delbert
was born in 1901 and passed away at the age of four years from a rare type
of disease. Alice was born on June 25, 1907. Lucy and Alice are the only
survivors left.

		Mr. and Mrs. Fred P. Merkel

		Mr. and Mrs. C. G. (Charlie) Dorwin

	Homestead of C. G. Dorwin, November 1889 Left to right are: James
	Flynn (Mrs. Dorwin's brother), Mrs. C. G. Dorwin and Mr. C. G.
	Dorwin.

Mr. and Mrs. C. G. Dorwin came to Dakota Territory in 1885 and homesteaded
three miles west and one mile south of Bowdle.

The postoffice at that time was called Kelley, on the stage route from
Ipswich to LeBeau. Kelley was located at that time where the Bowdle Airport
is now located.

At that time Ipswich was the end of the railroad. He made the trip to
Ipswich several times with an ox team and once on foot before the railroad
came to Bowdle in 1886.

Glenway, their son, was born on the homestead October 7, 1889.

They lived on this homestead until 1898 when they sold it and moved to a
farm six miles east of Bowdle in Odessa Township. They lived here until
1911 when they moved to the city of Bowdle.

In 1890 when the U. S. Government was having trouble with the Sioux Indians
the report was started the Indians had left the reservation and were on the
warpath. People from as far west as the Missouri River came to Bowdle. Some
even took the train and left the country. Mr. and Mrs. Dorwin stayed in
town for a couple of days until the scare was over and then returned to
their farm home.

When the Dorwin's were homesteading here one of their neighbors (John Erbe)
killed the last buffalo that roamed these prairies.

In 1931 the Dorwin's moved to California, where they resided until the time
of their death.

At the present time there are two of the children living: Glenway Dorwin of
Wauconda, Illinois and Mrs. Floyd (Clara) Sheppard of California.

		Mr. and Mrs. Karl Bonen

Karl Bonen, for years saved his meager wages, toiling long and hard in the
fields, until at last, in 1886, he was able to leave his home in Germany
and begin a new life in America. He had to leave his family and friends
behind, crossing the ocean on a freighter because he could afford no better.
He came to this country alone, with few worldly possessions, but it made
little difference to him for his heart was filled with hope in the promises
of this new land.

After landing in New York, he slowly worked his way to Odessa, Minnesota
where his friends had settled. Here he stayed until he had earned enough
money to buy a cow. His ambition was to stake out a homestead and he had
heard that the best opportunity for this would be in the Dakotas.

So, leading the cow by a rope, he started the long journey alone and on
foot. For nourishment on the way, he drank milk and ate cheese which he
prepared himself. It was cold this time of year and the lakes were frozen,
but not solidly enough to bear the weight of the cow. This slowed his trip
so much that by the time Karl reached the Dakotas, warm weather had
returned.

Karl took up homesteading 18 miles southwest of the town now known as
Bowdle, and here he built his one room house. The house was constructed of
alternate layers of sod and straw bricks, which were made by packing layers
of straw, mud and manure. This was his homestead of 350 acres.

  After he was fairly well settled, Karl sent abroad for his wife,
Ernestine Stock Bonen and their two sons, William (the eldest) and Herman.
Ernestine related many sad tales to her grandchildren later of the
hardships of crossing the ocean, the seasickness, and the never-ending
homesickness for the dear family left behind in Germany.

Gradually the sod house was enlarged to two rooms and oxen were purchased
for farming the rich land. Karl earned $1.00 per day working on the
railroad near what is now the town of Hoven, to save for the pair of oxen.
Many misfortunes overtook the family as they lived their lonely life on the
land, the worst of which seemed to be the winter blizzards. They were
thankful for the fuel of twisted hay and cow chips which kept them warm.
But they were thankful that their family was together and that they were
free to worship in Karl and Ernestine's faith as far back as they could
remember, as Lutherans. They had been used to 'roughing' it even in the old
country as they had little time or money for luxuries, even as now.
Ernestine had been a 'goose girl', one of the lowest paid occupations
possible. Each day she had to herd groups of geese for her employer. Karl
had done the hardest possible kind of manual labor in Germany. But now they
were working toward a goal-land that they could call their own. Ernestine
took in washing whenever possible for more prosperous families who had
settled there earlier and for this she received 25  for a week's wash. The
boys were growing up strong and healthy and each evening the family engaged
in family devotion, giving thanks for all they had. Karl made sure the
reading from the Bible was done while there was still daylight whenever
possible, as candles were precious. As long as they lived it seemed they
were saving in all things, even unto the next generations, things were used
sparingly, so deeply was it bred into them.

One of the oxen soon died and money had to be saved again to purchase
another. This time, however, Karl decided to buy a horse, and so it was
that they used a horse and an ox to work the land for many years. All
groceries were purchased at Ipswich, the nearest place to market and here
they also sold the crops and produce.

Each trip took about seven days. Later the town of Theodore began to
develop very near to the homestead, but it failed to prosper as the railroad
was routed through Hoven instead. Theodore soon completely disappeared.

On this homestead the two Bonen boys grew to manhood. William left home,
taking his wife to North Dakota where he raised a family of eleven
children. Herman continued the farming on the homestead and in 1904, Karl
retired to live in Bowdle, which by then had grown to be a prosperous town
with railroad and highway running through it.

Herman married Emilia Gill of near Eureka and together they farmed the land
and provided for their children. The daily chores were still very
difficult, washing done by washboard and tub, water heated on a
wood-burning stove, etc. Emilia spent many weary days helping Herman with
the harvesting, working in the fields even though she was heavy with child.
To this union was born Emil, the eldest, and then Henry. Soon after they
were blessed with a daughter, Elsie. For ten years they lived thus as a
happy family as the children grew and attended the country school. Herman
had two years of schooling but Emilia, being born a girl, had not had even
this opportunity. She had spent her girlhood keeping house for other
families and in return received material for a new dress each year plus her
room and board. She was always very interested in obtaining an education
for her children. In 1919 they were happily surprised with the birth of
another daughter, Dorothea. At this time Emilia began to suffer poor health
and as the sons grew, Herman began to think of living in Bowdle too and
letting them do the farming. This they did later, only to return again to
farming as the depression of the thirties forced them back. For two years
Herman farmed with only Emil as Henry had decided to barber as a
profession. This he is still doing, having a shop in Aberdeen, South Dakota
after having also been in Bowdle and Hot Springs.

Elsie married Carl Hider of Missouri. They reside in Springfield, Missouri.
Dorothy married Orpheus Krause of Java, who is employed with the U. S.
Department of Agriculture. They recently have been transferred to
Washington, D. C.

Emilia's health grew worse and after a lingering illness she passed away in
1941. The family still gets together but an empty place is always there.
She was a Christian mother who served her family as God would have it,
teaching God's word to those she loved so dearly.

Emil lived on the farm until the problem of sending his children to high
school became too much for them as they traveled 18 miles to school in
Bowdle. Eventually he decided, after giving it a lot of thought, to buy a
farm closer to town. Karl Bonen had wanted the old homestead to always
belong to a member of the family, and it was a hard decision to make. We
shall always remember the farm and the childhood days spent there, and later
the family home in Bowdle. Emil and his family lived near Bowdle for
several years and his five children grew up there.

Emil sold the farm, moved to Bowdle and is employed with the Bowdle Public
Schools.

Herman Bonen sold the family home in Bowdle and purchased a smaller one and
here he resided with his second wife, Carolyn Blumhardt. He was also
employed during his retirement from the farm with the Bowdle Public Schools
until his illness which caused his death in the year 1957. He and Emilia
lie side by side in the family plot. Karl and Ernestine also rest there.

		Dr. and Mrs. A. G. Nickisch

Dr. A. G. (George) Nickisch and his wife, Anna, were born in Germany and
migrated to the United States of America in March of 1882, arriving at the
New York port of entry on April 3, 1882. They immediately came west to
Dakota Territory and settled near the present town of Marion, Turner
County, South Dakota, where Dr. Nickisch practiced as a country doctor.

As new towns grew up all over and lands became available for homesteading
and purchase in the expanding interests of the Dakota Territory west of
Aberdeen along the main line of the Milwaukee Railroad, Dr. Nickisch
explored prospects there in 1898, purchasing some land and a farmstead from
Dr. William Edwards and brought his family in 1902 to the site located
about three-fourths of a mile northwest of the town of Bowdle. He also had
some land holdings near the pioneer cattle town of Le Beau.

Dr. Nickisch participated in community activities that assisted in the
promotion of affirmative action toward division of the Territory and the
creation of Statehood for South Dakota on November 2, 1889. He was
influential in the change of location of the new State Capital when it was
moved from Huron to Pierre. He was also a staunch supporter of the Hon. Joe
W. Parmley of Ipswich who originated the name and promoted the
establishment of the "Yellowstone Trail" which later became the present
route of U. S. Highway Number

In the fall of 1916, Dr. and Mrs. Nickisch retired to live in Bowdle where
they resided until the time of their demise.

At the present time there are six children living: George of Redfield; Karl
and Daniel residing on the old home place near Bowdle; Nathaniel of

		Mr. and Mrs. Friedrich Thurn

Mr. Friedrich Thurn, a native of Neudorf, South Russia, was born January 3,

		George W. McGlenn Family

	McGlenn Threshing Rig about 1890

George W. McGlenn, born in New York state in 1830, with his four sons came
to Dakota Territory from Michigan in 1884. They filed on land southwest of
what is now Bowdle. Mr. McGlenn's home was a stage stop between Ipswich and
LeBeau and settlers came there to get mail. Grandpa McGlenn, as he was

to North Dakota and later Montana. There was one daughter and seven sons

Washington.

Edward McGlenn and his wife, Elizabeth O'Neill McGlenn also farmed. He was
State Senator in 1897-1898 from Edmunds and Walworth County. They moved to

years, living at Aberdeen. Mrs. McGlenn passed away in 1929, he 17 years
later.

Charles McGlenn and his wife, Cora Taylor McGlenn farmed until 1900; then
moving to North Dakota. He ran a grain elevator there until shortly before

Estella McGlenn Hutchinson, daughter of George McGlenn, and husband, James,

		Mr. and Mrs. George L. Merriman

George L. Merriman, the eldest son of Dr. Lewis and Marion Weare Merriman,
was born September 1854 in Beloit, Wisconsin. His father was one of
Beloit's first doctors, settling there in 1842.

George had two brothers, Doctor Charles and Doctor Chester Merriman.

George chose to be a civil engineer, graduating from Beloit College in 1873
at the age of nineteen. He was the youngest student ever to enroll at the
college. Part of his time in college was spent tutoring. In 1874 he went to

		Mr. and Mrs. Anton Stangl

Anton Stangl was a son of Mr. and Mrs. Moritz Stangl, Gross-Eberharts,
lower Austria. Four of their nine children immigrated to the United States
in March of 1884. A brother, Joe, had come to the United States three years
earlier. Another brother, Adolph, joined them in South Dakota in 1898, and a
sister, Elizabeth (Mrs. Charles Meyers), settled in Spring Lake Township in
1907 with her husband and children.

Joe came to the Bowdle community in the spring of 1886, after working in
Wisconsin and Minnesota for 5 years. Ipswich was the last station on the
Milwaukee Road at that time. The railroad was being extended to Bowdle that
summer. In 1904, he sold the homestead to his brother, Anton. He still
resides at this location with his wife, Theresa, and youngest son, Robert.
He will be 97 years young in March of 1961.

Anton came to Bowdle, Dakota Territory in the spring of 1888, after working
on his uncle's farm and in the North Woods of Minnesota for 4 years. He
joined his brother, Joe, in Spring Lake Township west of Bowdle. In 1899 he
purchased two 40 acre plots from the government. These adjoined his
homestead and tree claim.

One of the early incidents that was recalled by all early settlers of this
area was the Indian scare in the fall of 1890. The claim shanty Joe and
Anton lived in, was less than a half mile north of the main Bowdle to
Bangor Road (now U. S. Highway #12). They were warned by one of the many
people going by on the Bangor Road, that the Indians were coming. They
hurriedly got a few things together, hooked their team to their wagon and
headed for Bowdle, too. But before leaving, they carefully hid their 10
gauge shotgun, so the Indians wouldn't find it.

1897 was an eventful year. Anton became a citizen of the United States. He
also was united in marriage with Wilhelmine Caroline Brandt, daughter of
Mr. and Mrs. Johan Brandt from north of Bowdle.

Anton Stangl was an ardent hunter. His most frequent hunting buddy was
Henry Erbe.

Spring Lake was a beautiful lake when Anton first came here. The water was
clear and about 7 or 8 feet deep, with a solid gravel bottom. Every draw
leading into the lake fed it with clear, running spring water. Since
settlement, the lake has been dry and refilled again many times, depending
on the annual amounts of rainfall and winter snows. Almost a foot of mud,
washed in from the plowed upland now covers that solid gravel lake bottom.

The seven children of Anton and Minnie Stangl still reside in Walworth
County. Frances (Mrs. Raymond Lee) lives south of Selby; Vina (Mrs. Ralph
Sawinsky) lives in Selby; Donald, Albert and Orville reside on the original
homesteads in Spring Lake Township and operate the farm as Stangl Brothers.
Shorthorn cattle and grain crops are the principle farm enterprises. The
shelter belts planted as early as 1900 by Anton Stangl and Carl Wolcott,
and many other trees planted by each of the three present brothers beautify
the farmsteads. Emma (Mrs. J. Herman Fiedler) and Irving reside in Java.
Two sons died in infancy.

Minnie passed away in 1926 when only 45 years old. Anton passed away in
1946 when 79 years of age.

		Mr. and Mrs. John C. Erbe

		Mr. and Mrs. Herman C. Baer

		Mr. and Mrs. George Sebert

George Sebert was born January 10, 1858 near Vermont Dane County,
Wisconsin. George was the son of John Sebert and Eliza Klincing Sebert.

In 1883 he came with two of his cousins, George and August Erbe, and a
friend, Wm. H. Payne, to seek land for homesteading.

They squatted on land north of Ipswich. They had difficulty getting water
there so they came to Bowdle in 1886. Mr. Sebert filed a claim near Spring
Lake in 1886.

In 1889 he went back to Wisconsin and was married to Katherine F. Hessman
of February 27, 1889.

Katherine F. Hessman was born in Bohemia March Z8, 1863. She was the
daughter of Frank and Anna Hessman. She came to the U. S. with her parents
in 1868. They lived at Dodgville, Wisconsin until 1871 when they moved to
Mazomanie where she lived until the time of her marriage.

They came back to Bowdle to the little home, a 12' x 14' claim shack.

Their oldest son, Everett, was born November 25, 189Z and is resident of
Selby, South Dakota. George was born July 14, 1895 and is living on the
original homestead. They welcomed an adopted daughter into their home in
May, 1904, who is now Mrs. George Erbe of Mobridge.

In 1897 they built a mud brick house on their homestead. This is still the
home of their son, George and family, who have made this "sod" house into a
modern home of today.

They worked with untiring effort for their community to make it better.

They helped build a bowery on the east bank of Spring Lake. There were many
old settlers picnics held there and many dances, ball games, and etc. were
enjoyed by young and old. Bowdle also had many community picnics there.

Mr. and Mrs. George Sebert moved to Selby to make their home in 1929. Mr.
Sebert passed away April 3, 1942 and Mrs. Sebert on May 11, 1951.

		Mr. and Mrs. J. B. Hoffman

J. B. Hoffman homesteaded in Madison Township. His parents originally came
from Germany and lived in Russia where Mr. Hoffman was born in 1877. As a
young man he smuggled his way to America on a passenger ship. He hid until
the ship was at sea a few days, when he volunteered his services in the
kitchen.

He married Mary Ketterling in Bowdle and lived on the homestead. Born there
were two sons, Egert and Arthur, who still reside in Bowdle.

Mr. Hoffman recalled his first night in Bowdle, before days of our
volunteer fire department. He came on the train on a very bitter cold night.
That night a fire started in a store on the site of the present Gross
store. It smoldered a long time before it was discovered. Mr. Hoffman
helped carry out the merchandise--among which were caskets, hardware and
groceries. He joined other towns-people in carrying water from hand pumps
in buckets. The building and other ones on that side of the street were all
destroyed. After that the present Gross store and bank building were
erected by Mr. Mason. It was thought the fire started in the basement where
potatoes were stored and oil lamps were used to keep them from freezing.

Mr. Hoffman served as township supervisor and school treasurer. In 1919 the
family moved to Bowdle. There the father and son, Arthur, established a gas
and oil business which is still in operation under Egert's management. Mr.
Hoffman was active in civic affairs and was mayor of Bowdle during the
Golden Anniversary year of 1936.

Mrs. Hoffman passed away in 1955. Mr. Hoffman remarried Mrs. Fredia Martz
and they are living in Bowdle.

		Mr. and Mrs. Frederich Walz

Frederich Walz was born in Krien, South Russia, on December 15, 1865 and
came with his parents, Christian Walz and his wife Josephine Beiler Walz,
to America in 1873. They settled around Freeman, South Dakota.

In 1885 he married Margaretha Becker who was born in 1867 in Russia. She

		Mr. and Mrs. Frank Johnson

Frank Johnson was born on June Z9, 1872 in Champaign, Illinois. When a
young lad came to Dakota Territory in the spring of 1888, he worked at what
ever job he could get to earn a living.

He and his brother, Eugene, each filed on a homestead in McPherson County
and batched for a number of years. Their house was a sod shanty. They did
their farming with oxen until they could buy horses and gentle them for
farm work.

They had very little entertainment so often the two of them played the
"fiddle" for dances in the homes.

On May 2, 1906 he married Lillian B. Gibson from Eureka, and they settled
on the farm. In 1907 a son, Russell, now of Wisconsin Rapids, Wisconsin was
born.

In 1910 a prairie fire, started from the neighbors burning a straw stack,
destroyed the buildings and all personal belongings, so they were without a
home. They then moved to Bowdle where Mrs. Johnson's father Ed. Gibson ran
a livery stable, then known as "The Gibson Livery Stable". Mr. Johnson
helped run the business. Ed. Gibson died of a heart attack in 1911 when Mr.
Johnson took over the business alone.

In 1911 another daughter was born; Ruth Goldade of Bowdle. In 1916 Ramona
Werner now of Herried, S. Dak. was born.

Mr. Johnson drove Dr. Edwards near and far to people needing a doctor.
During the flu epidemic there were many days and nights they had no rest in
order to attend the sick.

He also kept a team of white horses that was used for all funerals on the
town hearse.

Mr. Johnson had always continued farming his land where he had homesteaded
until his health did not permit. He had been active in community affairs in
former years.

He passed away April 15 1959 at the age of 87. Mrs. Johnson is still a
resident of Bowdle.

		Mr. and Mrs. Richard Lemke

Richard Lemke came to Bowdle in 1894. He was born in Germany and received
his meager education in Germany. He farmed northeast of Bowdle. He never
changed his source of power for farm work and used horses long after other
farmers had tractors. He raised fine draft horses and Shorthorn cattle. One
buggy team made two trips to Bowdle daily for school and it was estimated
it traveled over 20,000 miles in their time.

Mr. Lemke bought a farm in 1910 and married Ella Christianson. Together
they built and improved the farm to its present size. Their three daughters
were: Katherine (Mrs. A. Hoffman), Erma (Mrs. Melvin Maier) and Ella who
passed away at the age of eight years. Mrs. Maier and her family presently
live on the farm.

In the early days Mr. Lemke was well known as the catcher on Bowdle's
baseball team.

Mr. Lemke served on the school board twenty years and was on the board when
the present high school was built. He was the County Commissioner during
the time the Mina Lake and Dam were developed and the present court house
erected.

Mr. Lemke died in 1943.

		Mr. and Mrs. Charles F. Slate

Among the pioneer business men of Bowdle was Charles F. Slate, a young
druggist who came here from Iowa in 1888.

Charles was born in Fort Dodge, Iowa, on December 4, 1866, to Dr. and Mrs.
Frederick G. Slate.

	Slate's wedding picture taken in 1895

Several years later he became interested in emigrating to the newly opened
West country, and chose Bowdle as the place. He was employed by Henry Maas.
In 1889, under the Territory of South Dakota, he received his certificate of
registration as a pharmacist.

On February 16, 1895, Charles Slate and Ida Lund were married in Bowdle.
Ida was the daughter of Helge Lund, Sr., who had brought his family from
Iowa in 1884 to pioneer in Campbell County,

Charles and Ida lived in Bowdle until 1900, and it was there that two
daughters were born to them. The Slates remembered their Bowdle days as
happy ones, even though their place of business was destroyed by a
disastrous fire which wiped out a large part of main street. This may have
been the deciding factor in a change of location, for in 1900 Mr. Slate
moved his family to Platte, a new town being built at the terminal of the
newly-completed railroad line from Yankton. Here he built a new store, and
it was here that he spent the rest of his working days. Mrs. Slate passed
away in 1947, two years after their Golden Anniversary. From that time on,
Charles spent his winters in California with one daughter, and his summers
in Platte with the other.

The Slates had a family of three children, Florence, Hazel and Charles Jr.
Florence, now Mrs. Harry L. Anderson of Santa Ana, California. Hazel, now
Mrs. William F. Quinlan, lives in Platte, where she and her husband are
now proprietors of the Slate Drug Store. Charles Jr. met accidental death at
the age of 14 years.

Mr. Slate passed away on January 29, 1960, at the age of 93.

		Mr. & Mrs. John Voller

John Voller and his wife, Katherine came to the United States, from Odessa,
Russia, in 1882. They had two children, Dennis and Margaret at that time.
They brought all of their belongings in one small truck.

They homesteaded near Faulkton that year and Mr. Voller worked for a farmer
who furnished him with groceries for his family, and gave him a small
acreage of land to farm.

The family could not talk English and there were few who spoke German in
that locality, so they moved north of Bowdle to homestead. They built a sod
house.

Mr. Voller often told of how he bought potatoes from a farmer, who could
not understand German. He took a potato with him and showed it to the man,
who dealt with him generously.

One summer, Mr. Voller worked for a farmer near Java and was not able to
visit his family for three months. Then he carried to his farm fifty pounds
of flour on his back and new shoes for each one of his family. His wife

		Mr. and Mrs. John Sonnenfeld

John Sonnenfeld arrived in the United States in 1885 and in 1890 he and his
wife, Rosina Fischer Wolf and sons, George and Henry Wolf, settled in Adams
Township, northwest of Bowdle.

The third week in June, 1894, they finished building a two-room house
constructed of lumber. Mrs. Sonnenfeld had painted the front room with lime
and stippled it with bluing as was the mode of the day. One evening the
same week at about seven O'clock Mr. Sonnenfeld came to the house, warning
his wife to take the children and come with him to the root-cellar, as
there was a storm approaching from the southwest. Never having any
experience with a South Dakota twister, she refused his request and
continued with her housework duties. Mr. Sonnenfeld and Christ Sackman, who
made his home with Sonnenfelds, went outside to check on the storm. Just
then the tornado struck and unable to get to the house, they dropped to the
ground and crawled to safety and fortunate enough to the root-cellar. The
tornado was followed with a drenching rain and almost complete darkness. By
the flashes of lightning they saw that the house was gone and began to
search for the family. They found the body of the oldest son, John, and
then about the distance of a city block, they found the rest of the family
huddled together in a lake bed. The younger son, Jake, was unconscious from
a head wound. Mrs. Sonnenfeld had a severely cut leg and head injuries. By
all the misfortune, there still was a protecting hand that shielded George
and Henry, as they were unhurt.

Carrying the dead youngster and the unconscious Jake and supporting Mrs.
Sonnenfeld between them, they made their way to the Adam Wagner homestead,
about one-half mile west. The Wagners were living in a dugout at the time.
All their belongings were wet including their clothing. The night turning
cold and everything wet and nothing to kindle a fire with, it was very
miserable and everybody was waiting for the dawn of morning to seek relief.
When Mr. Sonnenfeld came to Bowdle the next day, Dr. Edwards was out of
town and a young Dr. Murdy accompanied him back. Dr. Murdy stitched Mrs.
Sonnenfeld's leg and did what he could to help but infection set in. Dr.
Edwards made several trips out and finally had to restitch the leg. When
the people of Bowdle heard of the misfortune of John Sonnenfeld's they gave
them bedding, utensils, furniture, and etc. and after building a sod
house, they were able to set up a home again.

In August, 1896, Emma (Mrs. John Klein) was born.

Mrs. Sonnenfeld never regained her health and was a semi-invalid the rest
of her life.

In 1918 the couple retired from the farm and moved to Java, where they
resided, where Mrs. Sonnenfeld passed away in 1929 and Mr. Sonnenfeld in
1936. Jake lived on the farm or homestead until he retired.

		History of Bowdle Creamery

The Bowdle Creamery & Produce Company was originally located one block
north of its present location.

Mr. Ben A. Oesterich operated the creamery for several years, having cream
stations in nearby towns, most of which was shipped in by rail, while some
was hauled by team and wagon and some was brought in by local producers.

At one time they started a farm milk route, skimming the milk at the plant
and hauling the skimmed milk back to the farm. This venture did not plan
out as the transportation of horse and wagon was rather slow and the
skimmed milk got pretty sour, the housewife refusing to wash out the sour
smelling cans and getting them ready to be filled the next morning.

Mr. Oesterich sold out to Mr. C. W. Young, who operated the old plant for
several years and then built at its present location. This was then taken
over by the First State Bank and operated by Martin C. Gross for a short
time and then leased to the Minnesota Creamery and Produce Company of St.
Paul with A. J. Englehart as manager. In May 1926 Edwin J. Blumhardt took
over the management, which position he still holds.

In the past 35 years there were many ups and downs, during the drought
years there were many times when it seemed that it was impossible to keep
going. Many farmers were forced to sell their cows due to shortage of feed
and the cows that were left had very little grass or hay making it
necessary for them to eat Russian thistle, weeds, wild onion and pepper
grass. The flavor of this feed carried into the milk, cream and butter,
making it almost impossible to find a market and selling at a discount from
five to eight cents per pound.

This condition soon improved and cream volume was again on the increase so
that by 1960 the volume of butter made was over one and a quarter million
pounds of that good Bowdle Butter made from good farm separated cream.

The cream is purchased from South Dakota, North Dakota and Montana; from
cream stations, individual shippers and local producers.

		Security National Farm Loan Association

The Association was organized in 1924 under the name of the Security
National Farm Loan Association.

The first Secretary-Treasurer was Karl J. Kundert. In 1933 A. C. Merkel was
elected to succeed him and is still the present manager.

  In the years known as the Dirty Thirties, the Association acquired 310
farms, some by voluntary deed and some by foreclosure. These farms were
leased to more than 500 tenants and by 1944 all of the farms were sold at
prices less than it cost to homestead them. No farms have been acquired
since.

Over the years the Association has loaned millions of dollars on farms. The
present volume being 470 members and the loans outstanding at present are

		Baking In Pioneer Days

Bread has long been known as the staff of life. There is no doubt that
pioneers leaned heavily on this staff, for flour kept well and a supply was
bought in the fall to be stored away for the long winter ahead. Some of the
early settlers who had neither oxen nor horses, carried bags of flour many
miles over their shoulders.

The yeast commonly used was put up in small packages of about five dry
square cakes, called Yeast Foam. It kept well on the kitchen shelf, if it
was kept dry. From it "everlasting yeast" was started by soaking a cake of
Yeast Foam in a little lukewarm water. To this was added a mashed potato, a
little sugar and the water that had been drained from boiled potatoes, after
it was cooled. This mixture was kept in an earthen or glass jar, in the
cellar or cave. When bread sponge was prepared, about two-thirds of the
starter was used. More mashed potato and potato water were added to the
remainder and placed in the cellar again.

Bread of pioneer days must have been exceptionally good, for those now old
enough to have eaten it, say it was light as a feather and baked in great,
round loaves weighing about three pounds each. When a mother served it, she
encircled the loaf with her left arm and with the all-purpose sharp butcher
knife, cut off a thick slice for each person. The large loaves remained
moist longer than smaller ones.

One evening I had the pleasure of visiting with three pioneer girls, Mrs.
Rose Kurle, Mrs. Jacob Gross and Mrs. Christina Buechler. They all recalled
the excellent products that their mothers baked in large outdoor ovens, and
described the methods and baking equipment used by their mothers.

Ovens were made of brick and covered with a plaster made of clay and straw.
The average oven was about four feet wide, six feet long and about five
feet high. The top, or roof was rounded like an inverted U. Steel rods were
used to reinforce it. The floor was of brick and a layer of sand which held
the heat. A chimney was built at the rear. During the time of actual baking,
this was plugged with old clothing, to keep heat from escaping. At the
front end, a ledge was built on which to set the loaves when removing them
from the oven. The oven door was of sheet metal and if it did not fit
snugly, a stick was braced against it while baking. Ovens were built close
to the house or summer kitchen.

Fuel was flax straw or wood. It took about an hour of firing to heat the
oven. The heat was tested by putting a hand into the oven and counting to
ten. If the heat could still be endured after reaching ten, more fuel was
put in. When the heat was considered correct, a hoe-like tool was used to
scrape all the ashes toward the door. In the corner of the oven stood a tub.
The ashes were placed in it and covered.

About fourteen loaves of bread could be baked in these ovens at once. Bread
pans were about twelve inches in diameter and three inches deep. They were
stored in the oven between bakings and washed before being used. Bread
baking was timed by the clock and two hours was required for the large
loaves. Meat roasts were also cooked in the ovens. A dough of flour and
water was rolled into a thin sheet and wrapped around the meat, to keep it
moist. German kuchen was another treat that was baked in the large bread
pans.

Dough was kneaded in a long wooden trough, which easily held fifty pounds
of flour. At noon dry yeast cakes were soaked and a small sponge was set,
using the water drained from boiled potatoes and cooled. This was stirred
down during the afternoon and kept warm. In the evening a large sponge was
mixed and beaten to incorporate air. The warm flour was sifted into the
trough and at one end a hollow was scooped out of the flour. The sponge was
poured into this, and flour sprinkled over to keep it warm. The trough had
a wooden cover and over this a "dough quilt" was placed. The everlasting
yeast sponge was set in the same manner. In the morning, the housewife
added the salt, shortening and water and kneaded the dough by hand.

The loaves were placed in the hot oven with a long handled shovel-like
tool, which also removed them. One man has said that his mother always put
the freshly baked bread on the bed to cool.

This may seem odd to the present generation, but pioneer women had little
table space available, and we know they must have covered the bed with
fresh, clean cloths first. The bread was delicious enough to be remembered
long after, when loaves came out of electric ovens.

           By-Mrs. Jacob Voller

	DWELLERS OF THE PRAIRIE

Long ago, flowers blossomed on Dakota plains,
Amid the verdant grass, near water-filled ravine
And on the gentle hills, while on the ridged moraines,
Stones lay concealed by reddish grass, that grew between.

Silence reigned, but oft the lark, a yellow breasted bird,
Broke the silence with clear, melodious lays;
The cooing mourning doves at dawn were heard,
And ducks betrayed their presence in the lowland haze.

The sharp-clawed badger burrowed deep to make a den,
Where lazily, he slept; he was the prairie creatures' bane,
Who in night's darkness pounced upon a nesting hen,
Or dug for gophers; ate his kill, and slept again.

Herds of fat buffalo possessed the grazing land,
And trod familiar trails to Horseshoe Lake;
There, Indians lurked in trees that now still stand,
To plot sly strategy, providing hides and steak.

When autumn's sun moved to the southern sky,
The Indians went to make their winter camp
On river banks, where squaws hung pemmican to dry,
And gathered wood for warmth of fire, that also served as lamp.

Winter winds brought drifts of sparkling white,
When long-eared rabbits dressed in white fur, soft as down,
Bounced vigilantly, up and down, till spring's sun light,
Like magic, turned their coats to gray and brown.

In 1862, opportunity knocked on many distant doors;
Congress passed the Homestead Act and land could be secured,
By citizens of other states, or folk from foreign shores,
If they built and lived on it, a title was assured.

They came with little and built sod shacks,
Enduring cold, and hail that wrought catastrophe;
Flour for bread was carried miles on backs,
This price was paid, for land that was free.

What words can best describe the pioneer,
Who left his friends in foreign lands,
To begin anew on the prairie here,
With meager funds, few tools, but toil-accustomed hands?

Pioneers who took advantage of the Homestead Act,
Were courageous, God-fearing and indomitable;
They did without what sod homes lacked
And strove to overcome each obstacle.

We, with modern homes and countless luxuries,
Cherish our heritage, and the memory
Of those who came in wagons, or crossed the seas,
To claim the virgin land, for their posterity.

Today, fast trains speed on, and jet trails cross the sky;
Motored farm machinery can run from dusk to dawn.
The farmer of the present day, may have a plane to fly,
And the silence of the prairie, is now forever gone.

Esther L. Voller
